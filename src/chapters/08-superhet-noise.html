History · a trick from the trenches

1918, Paris. Major Edwin Armstrong of the US Signal Corps was trying to detect the faint ignition noise of enemy aircraft at very high frequencies, where the available vacuum tubes gave almost no gain. The problem: tubes amplify well at low frequencies, and a tuned circuit is sharp (selective) only if it is built for a single fixed frequency. His solution was to move the incoming signal to a fixed lower “intermediate” frequency by mixing it with a locally generated oscillation, then amplify and filter at that fixed frequency with a stack of identical, carefully-tuned stages. He called it the “supersonic heterodyne” (“beats at a frequency above hearing”), now superheterodyne. (A French engineer, Lucien Lévy, filed a very similar patent at nearly the same time; the priority fight ran for a decade.) Nearly every radio built since, from the 1930s tube sets to the chip in your phone, uses some variant of this scheme.

The problem the superhet solves

An early “tuned-radio-frequency” (TRF) receiver amplified the antenna signal directly with several tunable stages. Two difficulties: (i) getting a sharp filter whose centre you can slide from 540 kHz to 1600 kHz is very hard (the ratio of bandwidth to centre frequency, $Q=f_0/B$, changes by 3× as you tune), and (ii) every stage of a tunable amplifier had to be adjusted together, and could oscillate. The superhet’s idea: do the hard filtering once, at a fixed frequency.

The superheterodyne receiver. Only the RF preselector and the local oscillator (LO) are tuned (by a single ganged knob). Almost all the gain and the sharp channel-selecting filter live in the fixed IF strip. AGC (automatic gain control) feeds the detected level back to hold the output steady.

Count the advantages. A fixed-frequency filter can use high-$Q$ resonators (crystal, ceramic, SAW) that cannot be tuned. A fixed-frequency amplifier is stable and its gain can be very high (80–100 dB in the IF strip) because it is at a lower, easier frequency than the signal. A single tuned element (the LO) suffices to select any station. And the same IF strip works for every station: selectivity is the same across the band.

The mixer arithmetic

A mixer multiplies the incoming signal by the LO. By the product-of-cosines identity, $\cos(2\pi f_st)\cos(2\pi f_{LO}t)=\tfrac12[\cos2\pi(f_{LO}-f_s)t+\cos2\pi(f_{LO}+f_s)t]$; the IF filter keeps the difference: $$f_{IF}=|f_{LO}-f_s|.$$ Because mixing is multiplication, it translates the whole modulated signal intact (bandwidth, sidebands and all; for FM the frequency deviation is unchanged, Chapter 7) — only the carrier moves. Broadcast receivers use high-side injection, $f_{LO}=f_s+f_{IF}$. The reason is practical (below).

The image frequency: the superhet’s one weakness

Mixing treats $f_{LO}+f_{IF}$ and $f_{LO}-f_{IF}$ identically: both are exactly $f_{IF}$ away from the LO. With high-side injection the wanted station sits at $f_s=f_{LO}-f_{IF}$, and a second, unwanted station at $$\boxed{\,f_{im}=f_s+2f_{IF}\,}$$ (the “image”, the mirror of $f_s$ about $f_{LO}$) also lands precisely on the IF and cannot be separated afterwards. The only defence is to remove it before the mixer, with the RF preselector.

Derivation · image rejection of a single tuned preselector

From Chapter 1, a single tuned circuit of quality factor $Q$ tuned to $f_s$ has $|H(f)|^2=\dfrac1{1+Q^2\big(\frac f{f_s}-\frac{f_s}f\big)^2}$. At the image frequency, $f=f_{im}$, the attenuation in amplitude is $\alpha=1/|H|=\sqrt{1+Q^2\rho^2}$ with $$\rho=\frac{f_{im}}{f_s}-\frac{f_s}{f_{im}}.$$ For $f_{IF}\ll f_s$, $\rho\approx4f_{IF}/f_s$, so $\alpha\approx4Qf_{IF}/f_s$: rejection grows with both $Q$ and the IF. More tuned circuits in cascade multiply the attenuation (in dB they add).

This explains why the IF is chosen high enough: a larger $f_{IF}$ pushes the image further away, making the preselector’s job easy. But a larger IF makes the IF filter less selective (for a given $Q$ the bandwidth is $f_{IF}/Q$) and harder to build. For AM broadcast the compromise is 455 kHz, for FM broadcast 10.7 MHz, for TV about 38.9 MHz (35.75 MHz in some systems), for satellite receivers 950–2150 MHz (first IF) then 479 MHz.

Worked examples

GATE 2021 Q.50. Receiver tuned to 600 kHz, LO at 1000 kHz. Then $f_{IF}=1000-600=400$ kHz and $f_{im}=f_{LO}+f_{IF}=1000+400=\mathbf{1400}$ kHz (or $f_s+2f_{IF}=600+800$).

Why high-side injection? AM band 540–1600 kHz, IF 455 kHz. High-side: LO tunes 995–2055 kHz, a frequency ratio of 2.07, so the oscillator’s tuning capacitor must change by $(2.07)^2=\mathbf{4.27}\!:\!1$, easy. Low-side: LO tunes 85–1145 kHz, ratio 13.5, capacitance ratio 181:1, which is impossible.

Image rejection. $f_s=1$ MHz, $f_{IF}=455$ kHz, $Q=50$: $f_{im}=1.91$ MHz, $\rho=1.91-0.524=1.386$, $\alpha=\sqrt{1+2500\cdot1.92}=69.3$ ($36.8$ dB).

Other spurious responses

A real mixer is not a perfect multiplier; its non-linearity creates products $m f_x\pm n f_{LO}$ for all integers $m,n$. An unwanted signal at $f_x$ is received if $|mf_x\pm nf_{LO}|=f_{IF}$. The classic cases:

  • Image ($m=n=1$): $f_x=f_{LO}+f_{IF}$.
  • Half-IF spur ($m=n=2$): $2f_x-2f_{LO}=\pm f_{IF}\Rightarrow f_x=f_{LO}\pm f_{IF}/2$. Example: FM receiver tuned to 100 MHz, IF $10.7$ MHz, $f_{LO}=110.7$ MHz: a strong signal at $f_{LO}-f_{IF}/2=105.35$ MHz produces a second-harmonic product that falls on the IF.
  • IF feedthrough: a strong signal at $f_{IF}$ itself leaks through the mixer; defended by an IF trap (a notch) at the antenna input.
  • Intermodulation (below) and blocking: a very strong adjacent signal compresses the front end and desensitises the receiver even though it is not at the tuned frequency.

Derivation · third-order intercept, 1 dB compression and spurious-free dynamic range

No amplifier or mixer is perfectly linear. Model a weakly non-linear stage as $y=a_1x+a_3x^3$ (odd terms only; $a_3<0$ for compression). Two-tone test: $x=A\cos\omega_1t+A\cos\omega_2t$ with $\omega_1,\omega_2$ close. The cubic term gives $3A^3\cos^2\omega_1t\cos\omega_2t\ni\frac32A^3\cos2\omega_1t\cos\omega_2t=\frac34A^3[\cos(2\omega_1+\omega_2)t+\cos(2\omega_1-\omega_2)t]$, and likewise for the mirror image. So there are third-order intermodulation products at $2f_1-f_2$ and $2f_2-f_1$, right next to the wanted tones (the others at $2f_1+f_2$ are far away and filtered), with amplitude $\frac34|a_3|A^3$, against the wanted $a_1A$.

The fundamentals grow as $A$ (slope 1 dB/dB) and the IM3 products as $A^3$ (slope 3 dB/dB): extrapolated, the lines meet at the third-order intercept point $A_{IP3}^2=\frac43|a_1/a_3|$ (a fictitious point: the stage compresses long before). Writing powers in dBm and gain $G$ in dB: $$\boxed{\,P_{IM3,\rm out}=3P_{\rm in}+G-2\,\mathrm{IIP3},\qquad P_{\rm IM3}-P_{\rm fund}=-2(\mathrm{IIP3}-P_{\rm in})\,}$$ For each dB that the tones are lowered, the IM3 products fall by 3 dB: relative to the signals they fall by 2 dB. For a single tone, gain compression $20\log\big(1+\frac{3a_3}{4a_1}A^2\big)=-1$ dB at $A^2=0.109A_{IP3}^2$, i.e. $$P_{1\rm dB}\approx\mathrm{IIP3}-9.6\ \mathrm{dB}.$$ The spurious-free dynamic range is the range of input power over which the signal is above the noise floor $N_{\rm in}=-174+10\log B+NF$ and the IM3 products are below it: the IM3 reaches the floor when $3P-2\mathrm{IIP3}=N_{\rm in}$, i.e. at $P=\frac{2\mathrm{IIP3}+N_{\rm in}}3$, so $$\boxed{\,\mathrm{SFDR}=\tfrac23\big(\mathrm{IIP3}-N_{\rm in}\big)\ \mathrm{dB}\,}$$ For a cascade the input intercept is $\dfrac1{\mathrm{IIP3}_{\rm tot}}=\dfrac1{\mathrm{IIP3}_1}+\dfrac{G_1}{\mathrm{IIP3}_2}+\dfrac{G_1G_2}{\mathrm{IIP3}_3}+\cdots$ (linear units): the opposite of Friis: noise wants gain early, linearity wants gain late. Receiver design is the trade-off between these two; AGC and switchable front-end attenuators move the operating point.

Worked example · dynamic range of an FM front end

$B=200$ kHz, $NF=5$ dB: noise floor $N_{\rm in}=-174+53+5=-116$ dBm. LNA with $\mathrm{IIP3}=-10$ dBm: $\mathrm{SFDR}=\frac23(-10+116)=70.7$ dB. Two strong stations at $-40$ dBm each (adjacent channels $400$ and $800$ kHz away) produce IM3 products $2(\mathrm{IIP3}-P_{\rm in})=2(30)=60$ dB below themselves, i.e. at $-100$ dBm: well above the $-116$ dBm floor and enough to wreck reception of a weak station at $-90$ dBm on the third frequency. This is the real reason for “front-end overload” in crowded bands: moving to an LNA with $\mathrm{IIP3}=0$ dBm or adding 10 dB of attenuator improves the IM3 relative level by $20$ dB (but costs 10 dB of sensitivity if the attenuator is ahead of the LNA).

Noise in analog receivers: what SNR do we get out?

We now ask the key performance question of analog communication: with a given transmitted power and a channel adding white noise of density $N_0/2$, how good is the demodulated signal? To compare schemes fairly we use the benchmark of sending the message directly (baseband) with the same power $P_T$ over a channel of the same bandwidth $W$: $$\gamma\;\triangleq\;\frac{P_T}{N_0W}\quad(\text{“channel SNR” or baseband SNR}),\qquad\text{figure of merit (FOM) }=\frac{\mathrm{SNR}_o}{\gamma}.$$ (Another popular variable is the carrier-to-noise ratio in the predetection bandwidth, $\mathrm{CNR}=\dfrac{P_T}{N_0B_T}=\gamma\dfrac W{B_T}$. For baseband it equals $\gamma$.) The receiver model is always the same: a band-pass filter of width $B_T$ around $f_c$ (the noise there is narrowband noise $n(t)=n_c\cos-n_s\sin$ with power $N_0B_T$, Chapter 5), then the demodulator, then a low-pass filter of width $W$.

Coherent systems: DSB-SC and SSB

Derivation · DSB-SC and SSB output SNR

DSB-SC: $B_T=2W$, input noise power $N_0\cdot2W$. Multiply the sum $A_cm\cos\omega_ct+n_c\cos\omega_ct-n_s\sin\omega_ct$ by $\cos\omega_ct$ and low-pass: output $=\frac12[A_cm(t)+n_c(t)]$, since the $n_s\sin\cos$ term has no baseband part. Signal power $\frac{A_c^2}4P_m$; noise power $\frac14\cdot N_0\cdot2W=\frac{N_0W}2$. Since $P_T=\frac{A_c^2P_m}2$: $$\mathrm{SNR}_o=\frac{A_c^2P_m/4}{N_0W/2}=\frac{A_c^2P_m}{2N_0W}=\gamma.$$ The quadrature noise $n_s$ is rejected by the synchronous detector; the other half of the input noise is thrown away. SSB: $P_T=\frac{A_c^2P_m}4$, $B_T=W$; the same detector gives output $\frac14A_cm+\frac12n_c$ with $n_c$ of power $N_0W$: signal $\frac{A_c^2P_m}{16}$, noise $\frac{N_0W}4$, so $\mathrm{SNR}_o=\frac{A_c^2P_m}{4N_0W}=\gamma$ again.

So DSB-SC and SSB are as good as baseband transmission: figure of merit 1 (0 dB). SSB gets there with half the signal power and half the bandwidth; DSB-SC wastes bandwidth but its quadrature noise cancels. Nothing can beat this without spending more bandwidth.

AM with an envelope detector

Derivation · AM output SNR and the threshold

The detector input is $[A_c(1+\mu m)+n_c]\cos\omega_ct-n_s\sin\omega_ct$, whose envelope is $$r=\sqrt{[A_c(1+\mu m)+n_c]^2+n_s^2}.$$ Strong carrier ($A_c\gg$ noise): $r\approx A_c(1+\mu m)+n_c+\dfrac{n_s^2}{2A_c}\approx A_c+A_c\mu m+n_c$: after removing the DC, the signal is $A_c\mu m$ (power $A_c^2\mu^2P_m$), the noise is $n_c$ (power $N_0B_T=2N_0W$), and $P_T=\frac{A_c^2}2(1+\mu^2P_m)$ includes the wasted carrier. Hence $$\mathrm{SNR}_o=\frac{A_c^2\mu^2P_m}{2N_0W}=\frac{\mu^2P_m}{1+\mu^2P_m}\,\gamma=\eta\,\gamma\ \le\ \tfrac13\gamma .$$ For a sinusoid with $\mu=1$: FOM $=\frac13$ ($-4.8$ dB); at $\mu=0.5$: $\frac{1/8}{9/8}=\frac19$ ($-9.5$ dB). The AM FOM is just the transmission efficiency $\eta$ of Chapter 6: the carrier carries no information, but costs power. Weak carrier ($A_c\ll$ noise): the envelope is dominated by the noise envelope $r\approx r_n+\dfrac{A_c(1+\mu m)\cos\psi_n}{1}$ where $\psi_n$ is the random noise phase: the message multiplies a random factor $\cos\psi_n$ and cannot be recovered; the output SNR then falls quadratically with the input SNR (a 1 dB drop in $\gamma$ costs 2 dB). The knee is around $\mathrm{CNR}\approx$ 10 dB. A synchronous detector for AM has no threshold, since it never forms the non-linear envelope.

FM: bandwidth bought as SNR

Derivation · FM output SNR, the $f^2$ noise spectrum and the figure of merit

Receiver input: $A_c\cos(\omega_ct+\phi(t))+n_c\cos\omega_ct-n_s\sin\omega_ct$. In complex-envelope form the sum is $A_ce^{j\phi}+n_c+jn_s$. For a strong carrier ($|n|\ll A_c$) the angle of the sum is $\phi(t)+\dfrac{n_s'(t)}{A_c}$, where $n_s'$ is the component of the noise in quadrature with the instantaneous carrier phasor (statistically the same as $n_s$, since rotating white-noise quadratures leaves their statistics unchanged). So the noise acts as a phase error $\varphi_n\approx n_s/A_c$.

An ideal discriminator outputs the instantaneous frequency, i.e. the derivative of the phase: $\frac1{2\pi}\dot\phi+\frac1{2\pi}\dot\varphi_n$. Differentiation multiplies the amplitude spectrum by $2\pi f$ (Chapter 2), the power spectrum by $(2\pi f)^2$, so the output noise PSD (in Hz$^2$/Hz) is $$S_{n_o}(f)=\frac{N_0}{A_c^2}\,f^2,\qquad|f|\le W.$$ (Input: $S_{n_s}=N_0$ in the band; output: $\frac{1}{(2\pi)^2}(2\pi f)^2\frac{N_0}{A_c^2}$.) It is parabolic, quiet near 0 and strongest at the band edge. Integrating over $|f|\le W$: noise power $=\dfrac{2N_0W^3}{3A_c^2}$. The demodulated signal $k_fm(t)$ has power $k_f^2P_m$. Therefore $$\mathrm{SNR}_o=\frac{3A_c^2k_f^2P_m}{2N_0W^3}=3\,\frac{k_f^2P_m}{W^2}\,\gamma,\qquad\gamma=\frac{A_c^2/2}{N_0W}.$$ With the deviation ratio $D=\frac{k_fm_p}{W}$ ($m_p$ the peak of $m$): $\ \mathrm{SNR}_o=3D^2\dfrac{P_m}{m_p^2}\gamma$. For a sinusoidal message $P_m/m_p^2=\frac12$, and $$\boxed{\ \mathrm{SNR}_o=\tfrac32\beta^2\gamma\ \ (\text{FM tone}),\qquad \mathrm{SNR}_o=k_p^2P_m\gamma\ \ (\text{PM}).\ }$$

Look at what this says: the output SNR grows with the square of $\beta$, and by Carson’s rule $B_T\approx2W(\beta+1)$ the bandwidth grows only linearly. FM at $\beta=5$ (broadcast) beats DSB-SC/SSB by $\frac32\cdot25=37.5$ times, which is 15.7 dB, and beats AM ($\mu=1$) by 20.5 dB, at the price of 6× the bandwidth. This is Armstrong’s insight and the first demonstration of a theme that returns in Chapter 11: bandwidth can be traded for SNR (Shannon later proved exactly how far).

Derivation · the FM threshold

The linearisation above assumes the noise phasor never swamps the carrier. As the carrier-to-noise ratio $\mathrm{CNR}=A_c^2/(2N_0B_T)$ falls, the resultant phasor occasionally swings around the origin: each time the phase jumps by $\pm2\pi$, the discriminator emits a spike of area $2\pi$ (a “click”), and these impulses, which are broadband (white), add to the parabolic noise. Rice’s analysis shows the click rate rises exponentially when $\mathrm{CNR}$ drops below roughly 10 dB. Since $\gamma=\mathrm{CNR}\cdot B_T/W=\mathrm{CNR}\cdot2(\beta+1)$, a threshold at $\mathrm{CNR}_{th}=10$ means $$\gamma_{th}\approx20(\beta+1)\qquad(\text{ratio, i.e. }10\log[20(\beta+1)]\ \mathrm{dB}).$$ The larger the deviation, the higher the threshold in $\gamma$ terms, because noise in the wide predetection band is larger. Below threshold the SNR collapses at several dB per dB; which means the trade of bandwidth for SNR only works for signals strong enough: $\beta$ must be chosen with the available $\gamma$ in mind. Threshold-extension receivers use a PLL or “FM feedback” demodulator (Chapter 7), whose tracking filter is narrower than the signal, to push the threshold down by 3–6 dB.

And the other famous FM property: the capture effect (derived in Chapter 7). When two FM stations share a frequency, the receiver locks onto the stronger one and suppresses the other almost completely (not so in AM, where you hear both, plus a heterodyne whistle). Both effects come from the same cause: a limiter and a discriminator respond only to the phase of the dominant phasor.

Pre-emphasis and de-emphasis, quantitatively

Because the discriminator noise PSD $\propto f^2$ and music/speech PSD falls with $f$, boosting the high frequencies before transmission with $H_{pe}$ and attenuating them back with $H_{de}=1/H_{pe}$ at the receiver reduces output noise at no cost to the message.

Derivation · the pre-emphasis improvement factor

With $H_{de}(f)=\dfrac1{1+jf/f_0}$, $f_0=\frac1{2\pi RC}$, the noise power with de-emphasis is $\int_{-W}^{W}\frac{N_0}{A_c^2}f^2|H_{de}|^2df=\frac{2N_0f_0^3}{A_c^2}\big[\tfrac Wf_0-\arctan\tfrac W{f_0}\big]$ (use $\frac{f^2}{1+f^2/f_0^2}=f_0^2-\frac{f_0^2}{1+f^2/f_0^2}$), against $\frac{2N_0W^3}{3A_c^2}$ without. The improvement is the ratio: $$I=\frac{(W/f_0)^3}{3\,[\,W/f_0-\arctan(W/f_0)\,]}.$$ For broadcast audio $W=15$ kHz and 75 μs ($f_0=2.12$ kHz): $I=20.9$ ($13.2$ dB). For the 50 μs standard used in India and Europe ($f_0=3.18$ kHz): $I=10.4$ ($10.2$ dB). The caveat: the boosted highs must not over-deviate the carrier; broadcasters apply a limiter, so real programme gets slightly less than the full benefit.

Engineering practice · why FM stereo is noisier than mono

In stereo the $L-R$ signal lives on a 38 kHz subcarrier occupying 23–53 kHz, where the discriminator noise is much higher than in the 0–15 kHz mono band. The noise power in 23–53 kHz relative to 0–15 kHz is $\frac{53^3-23^3}{15^3}=40.5$ ($16$ dB), and the DSB-SC subchannel is also transmitted at roughly half the amplitude of the main channel; together, stereo reception is about 20–23 dB noisier than mono at the same field strength. This is why a weak FM station “drops to mono” (automatic blend/switch) in a car: a clean mono signal beats a hissy stereo one. The same $f^2$ law explains why FM subcarriers (RDS at 57 kHz) are kept at very low injection levels, and why SCA/background-music subcarriers at 67 kHz sound poor.

Two lessons. First, within the linear (AM-family) schemes you cannot buy SNR with bandwidth: it is capped at the baseband value. Second, angle modulation can, at a cost in threshold. In Chapter 11 we shall see that this is a special case of a universal law: the capacity of an AWGN channel, $C=B\log_2(1+S/N)$, grows linearly with bandwidth while only logarithmically with SNR, so spending bandwidth is by far the cheaper way to buy performance. FM’s output SNR improves as $B^2$ above threshold, which is faster than the best digital schemes can do analog-wise at moderate bandwidth, but FM operates far from the Shannon limit: PCM with coding does much better (Chapters 12 and 17).

Real world · a link in numbers

An FM station delivers $-80$ dBm to a receiver with noise figure 5 dB and a 200 kHz channel. Noise floor: $-174+10\log(200\,\mathrm{k})+5=-116$ dBm. Input SNR $=36$ dB, comfortably above the FM threshold ($\approx13$–$20$ dB), so the formulas above hold and audio SNR is $36+15.7\approx52$ dB (plus the 10–13 dB pre-emphasis gain, less the audio/CNR bandwidth ratio): studio-quality. If the signal fades to $-100$ dBm the SNR falls to 16 dB, near threshold: the audio suddenly turns into a rough hiss with clicks. That abrupt cliff is why a car FM radio “drops out” rather than fading gracefully.

The sensitivity at the threshold. With $\mathrm{CNR}_{th}=10$ dB, $B_T=180$ kHz and $NF=5$ dB: $P_{\min}=-174+52.6+5+10=-106$ dBm. A typical broadcast receiver quotes about $-100$ dBm (≈ 2–3 µV across 75 Ω) for “usable sensitivity”, consistent with this estimate.

Set A · Concept checks

Select all correct statements about the superheterodyne receiver.
  1. The IF filter provides most of the adjacent-channel selectivity.
  2. The preselector needs to be sharp enough to remove the IF.
  3. A larger IF moves the image further from the wanted signal.
  4. The image and the wanted signal are symmetric about the local oscillator frequency.

(B) is false: the preselector must remove the image, which is $2f_{IF}$ away. A, C, D are as derived above.

Compared with a superheterodyne, a direct-conversion receiver
  1. has a stronger image problem
  2. needs no image filter but is vulnerable to DC offset and I/Q imbalance
  3. needs a more selective IF filter
  4. cannot demodulate FM

With the LO at the carrier, the image is the signal’s own mirror and is handled by I/Q processing; the price is DC offset, LO leakage and I/Q mismatch. (B).

When the input SNR of an FM receiver falls below the threshold, the output SNR
  1. continues to fall at 1 dB per dB
  2. improves because of the limiter
  3. falls much faster than 1 dB per dB because of click noise
  4. becomes equal to the baseband SNR

Below threshold the noise phasor occasionally encircles the carrier phasor, each time producing a $2\pi$ phase jump (a click); output SNR collapses rapidly.

Set B · Numerical practice

An AM broadcast superhet with $f_{IF}=455$ kHz (high-side injection) is tuned to 1000 kHz. The image frequency in kHz is ______.

$f_s+2f_{IF}=1000+910=1910$ kHz.

An AM receiver covers 540–1600 kHz with $f_{IF}=455$ kHz and high-side injection. The tuning capacitor of the local oscillator must have a maximum-to-minimum capacitance ratio of about
  1. 2.07
  2. 4.27
  3. 8.5
  4. 181

LO range 995–2055 kHz; $f\propto1/\sqrt{LC}$ so $C_{\max}/C_{\min}=(2055/995)^2=4.27$.

A single-tuned preselector has $Q=50$. The receiver is tuned to 1 MHz with $f_{IF}=455$ kHz. The image rejection in dB is ______.

$f_{im}=1.91$ MHz, $\rho=1.386$, $\alpha=\sqrt{1+(50\cdot1.386)^2}=69.3\to36.8$ dB.

A single-tone AM system with $\mu=0.5$ is demodulated by an envelope detector at high SNR. Its output SNR relative to the baseband benchmark $\gamma=P_T/(N_0W)$, in dB, is ______.

$\eta=\frac{\mu^2/2}{1+\mu^2/2}=\frac{0.125}{1.125}=\frac19\to-9.54$ dB.

Single-tone FM with $\beta=5$ is demodulated above threshold. The ratio of output SNR to $\gamma$ in dB is ______.

$\frac32\beta^2=37.5\to10\log_{10}37.5=15.74$ dB.

An FM receiver tuned to 100 MHz has $f_{IF}=10.7$ MHz and high-side injection. A strong signal at the half-IF spur frequency below the LO is received. That frequency in MHz is ______.

$f_{LO}=110.7$ MHz; spur at $f_{LO}-f_{IF}/2=110.7-5.35=\mathbf{105.35}$ MHz (second harmonic mixing: $2f_x-2f_{LO}=-f_{IF}$).

Two equal tones at $-40$ dBm each enter an amplifier whose input third-order intercept is $-10$ dBm. The third-order intermodulation products are below the tones by (dB) ______.

$2(\mathrm{IIP3}-P_{\rm in})=2(-10+40)=\mathbf{60}$ dB.

A receiver has noise bandwidth 200 kHz, noise figure 5 dB and $\mathrm{IIP3}=-10$ dBm. Its spurious-free dynamic range in dB is ______.

$N_{\rm in}=-174+10\log(2\times10^5)+5=-174+53+5=-116$ dBm. $\mathrm{SFDR}=\frac23(-10+116)=\mathbf{70.7}$ dB.

A tone-modulated FM system with $\beta=5$ is received with channel SNR $\gamma=30$ dB (above threshold). The output SNR in dB is ______.

$30+10\log(1.5\times25)=30+15.74=\mathbf{45.7}$ dB.

The FM threshold in channel SNR $\gamma$ for $\beta=5$ is about (dB) ______.

$\gamma_{th}=20(\beta+1)=120\to10\log120=\mathbf{20.8}$ dB (CNR at threshold $=10$ dB, and $B_T/W=12$ gives $+10.8$ dB).

An FM receiver has $B_T=180$ kHz, noise figure 5 dB and operates at its threshold CNR of 10 dB. Its minimum input power in dBm is ______.

$-174+10\log(1.8\times10^5)+5+10=-174+52.6+5+10=\mathbf{-106.4}$ dBm.

With 50 µs de-emphasis ($f_0=3.18$ kHz) and $W=15$ kHz, the improvement in output SNR (dB) is ______.

$r=W/f_0=4.717$, $I=\dfrac{r^3}{3(r-\arctan r)}=\dfrac{104.96}{3(4.717-1.361)}=10.4\to\mathbf{10.2}$ dB.

Set C · Exam-style problems

Consider a superheterodyne receiver tuned to 600 kHz. If the local oscillator feeds a 1000 kHz signal to the mixer, the image frequency (integer) is ______ kHz.

$f_{IF}=400$ kHz, image $=f_{LO}+f_{IF}=1400$ kHz.

Which of the following have a figure of merit (output SNR / $P_T/N_0W$) equal to 1?
  1. SSB-SC with coherent detection
  2. AM ($\mu=1$, tone) with envelope detection
  3. DSB-SC with coherent detection
  4. FM with $\beta=1$

SSB and DSB-SC both give 1. AM gives $\frac13$. FM with $\beta=1$ gives $\frac32$.

A superheterodyne receiver (high-side injection, $f_{LO}=f_s+f_{IF}$) tunes the range 58–68 MHz. The minimum $f_{IF}$ (MHz) such that every image frequency falls outside the 58–68 MHz band is ______.

Image $=f_s+2f_{IF}$; the lowest tuned frequency $58$ MHz gives the lowest image $58+2f_{IF}$, which must exceed 68 MHz: $f_{IF}>5$ MHz. Minimum $=\mathbf5$ MHz. (A larger IF buys image rejection but costs IF selectivity.)

A superheterodyne receiver has $f_{IF}=15$ MHz and $f_{LO}=3.5$ GHz. The received signal frequency is greater than the LO frequency. The image frequency (MHz) is ______.

Now $f_s=f_{LO}+f_{IF}=3515$ MHz (a high-side received signal: low-side injection). The image is on the other side of the LO: $f_{im}=f_{LO}-f_{IF}=f_s-2f_{IF}=\mathbf{3485}$ MHz. General rule: the image is the mirror of the wanted signal about the LO.

Chapter 8 in one breath

  • Superhet: $f_{IF}=|f_{LO}-f_s|$; high-side injection $f_{LO}=f_s+f_{IF}$; image $f_{im}=f_s+2f_{IF}$; preselector rejects it: $\alpha=\sqrt{1+Q^2\rho^2}$. AM IF = 455 kHz; FM IF = 10.7 MHz. Half-IF spur at $f_{LO}\pm f_{IF}/2$. Direct-conversion has no image but DC/IQ problems.
  • Non-linearity: IM3 at $2f_1-f_2$; relative level $-2(\mathrm{IIP3}-P_{\rm in})$; $P_{1\rm dB}=\mathrm{IIP3}-9.6$; $\mathrm{SFDR}=\frac23(\mathrm{IIP3}-N_{\rm in})$; cascade: noise wants gain early, linearity late.
  • Benchmark $\gamma=P_T/N_0W$. DSB-SC & SSB: $\mathrm{SNR}_o=\gamma$. AM: $\eta\gamma$ (≤⅓). FM: $\frac32\beta^2\gamma$ (tone), $3D^2\frac{P_m}{m_p^2}\gamma$ (general); PM: $k_p^2P_m\gamma$.
  • FM discriminator noise PSD $\propto f^2$ (phase noise differentiated); pre/de-emphasis improves SNR by 10–13 dB; stereo costs ~20 dB.
  • FM threshold: CNR ≈ 10 dB, i.e. $\gamma_{th}\approx20(\beta+1)$; capture effect.
  • Bandwidth ↔ SNR trade-off: preview of Shannon.
