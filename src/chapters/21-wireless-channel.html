History · from Marconi’s letter S to Bell Labs’ BLAST

On 12 December 1901 Guglielmo Marconi, in a hut on Signal Hill, Newfoundland, listened for three dots, the letter S, sent from Poldhu in Cornwall 3500 km away: proof that radio waves follow the Earth’s curvature (via the ionosphere, which nobody yet understood). Fifty years later radio engineering was still empirical. Harald Friis of Bell Labs gave the transmission formula that bears his name in 1946 and, in the same year, the cascade noise formula (Chapter 5). Yoshihisa Okumura drove through Tokyo in 1968 with a field-strength meter in a van to measure what cities do to signals; Masaharu Hata fitted his data with the formula that is still in every planning tool. In 1968 Clarke described the random fading of a moving receiver, and William Jakes made it a simulation recipe in 1974. For two decades fading was “the enemy” to be beaten with diversity. Then in 1995 Emre Telatar proved, and Gerard Foschini and Jack Salz at Bell Labs argued, that with several antennas at both ends rich scattering multiplies capacity; Siavash Alamouti (1998) found a two-antenna code of stunning simplicity. The enemy became a resource, and MIMO is now in every Wi-Fi router and phone.

Real world

A Wi-Fi router in the next room and a 5G small cell down the street live on the same physics as a satellite 36 000 km away: received power from the Friis equation, a margin for shadowing, a fading margin, and enough diversity to make the margin affordable. The 3GPP channel models used to design 4G and 5G (TDL-A…E, CDL, UMa/UMi/InH) are the sophisticated descendants of this chapter’s models. Every number in “coverage”, “range” and “link budget” on a data sheet comes out of the equations below.

The link budget

Derivation · the Friis transmission equation

A transmitter radiating power $P_t$ isotropically spreads it over a sphere; at distance $d$ the power flux density is $P_t/(4\pi d^2)$ W/m². A directive antenna with gain $G_t$ (relative to isotropic) in the direction of the receiver multiplies this by $G_t$. A receiving antenna of effective aperture $A_e$ collects $P_r=A_e\cdot P_tG_t/(4\pi d^2)$. Antenna theory (reciprocity and the equivalence of a matched receiving antenna to an absorbing area: $A_e=\lambda^2G/4\pi$) gives for the receiver of gain $G_r$: $$\boxed{\ P_r=P_tG_tG_r\Big(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\Big)^2=\frac{P_tG_tG_r}{L_{\rm fs}},\qquad L_{\rm fs}=\Big(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\Big)^2\ }$$ In dB: $L_{\rm fs}=20\log_{10}\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}$; with $d$ in km and $f$ in MHz, $$L_{\rm fs}\,[\mathrm{dB}]=32.44+20\log_{10}d_{\rm km}+20\log_{10}f_{\rm MHz}.$$ It grows by 6 dB per doubling of distance and of frequency. Examples: 1 km at 2.4 GHz: $32.44+0+67.6=100.0$ dB; at 28 GHz: 121.4 dB; a geostationary satellite ($d=35\,786$ km) at 12 GHz: $32.44+91.1+81.6=205.1$ dB. Why frequency appears. The $\lambda^2$ does not come from nature’s absorption of high frequencies: it is the aperture of an isotropic antenna. Re-write with apertures, $G=4\pi A_e/\lambda^2$: $$P_r=\frac{P_t\,A_t\,A_r}{\lambda^2d^2}.$$ For antennas of fixed physical size, received power rises as $f^2$. That is why dishes get small at high frequency, why a phased array of 256 patch elements fits in a phone at 28 GHz, and why millimetre-wave links are viable if (and only if) both ends use beamforming to restore the antenna gain that the fixed-gain isotropic assumption took away. (Real mm-wave links lose extra to rain, oxygen at 60 GHz and blockage.)

The link is analysed as a link budget: add gains and subtract losses in dB, from the transmitter to the detector. $$P_r\,[\mathrm{dBm}]=P_t+G_t+G_r-L_{\rm path}-L_{\rm other}-M,$$ with $P_t$ the transmit power (dBm), $G$ antenna gains (dBi), $L_{\rm path}$ the path loss, $L_{\rm other}$ cable, polarisation, atmospheric, pointing and body losses, and $M$ the margin for shadowing and fading below. The receiver is characterised by its sensitivity, the weakest signal it can decode: $$P_{\min}=-174+10\log_{10}B+F+\mathrm{SNR}_{\min}\quad[\mathrm{dBm}]$$ ($-174$ dBm/Hz from Chapter 5, $B$ the bandwidth in Hz, $F$ the noise figure, $\mathrm{SNR}_{\min}$ the SNR at the decision point for the chosen modulation and code; for example Wi-Fi at 20 MHz, $F=6$ dB, 6 Mbit/s BPSK ½ needing about 5 dB: $-174+73+6+5=-90$ dBm in the ideal case; the standard specifies $-82$ dBm, leaving 8 dB for implementation loss and interference). The system works where $P_r\ge P_{\min}$. For the free-space model this gives the maximum range $$d_{\max}=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\sqrt{\frac{P_tG_tG_r}{P_{\min}}}.$$ Example (Wi-Fi at 50 m). $P_t=20$ dBm, $G_t=G_r=2$ dBi, 2.4 GHz: $L_{\rm fs}=32.44+20\log0.05+67.6=74.0$ dB; $P_r=20+4-74.0=-50$ dBm: 40 dB above the $-90$ dBm sensitivity. Free space would give a range of $50\times10^{40/20}=5$ km; walls and furniture bring the real figure below 100 m, so the model needs the sections that follow.

Engineering practice · the digital-link equation in $E_b/N_0$ form

For a symbol-rate-limited link it is convenient to write the budget directly in $E_b/N_0$: $$\frac{E_b}{N_0}=\frac{P_r}{kT_sR_b}=\mathrm{EIRP}\,[\mathrm{dBW}]-L+\Big(\frac GT\Big)[\mathrm{dB/K}]-10\log_{10}k-10\log_{10}R_b,\qquad-10\log k=228.6\ \mathrm{dBW/K/Hz},$$ with the EIRP $=P_tG_t$ and the receiver’s figure of merit $G/T_s$ (antenna gain over system noise temperature). Required $E_b/N_0$ (from the modulation and code, Chapters 15, 17, 18) plus a margin $M$ sets $R_b$ or the range. Chapter 23 works full budgets with this equation for a satellite-TV dish, a Wi-Fi link, a cellular uplink, a deep-space probe and an optical line (the GPS signal is analysed in Chapter 19).

Large-scale propagation

Derivation · the two-ray ground-reflection model and the $d^{-4}$ law

Over flat ground a receiver at height $h_r$ gets the direct ray (length $d_1$) and a ray reflected from the ground (length $d_2$) from a transmitter of height $h_t$ at horizontal distance $d\gg h_t,h_r$. The ground reflection coefficient is $\approx-1$ for grazing incidence. The path difference is $$\Delta d=d_2-d_1=\sqrt{d^2+(h_t+h_r)^2}-\sqrt{d^2+(h_t-h_r)^2}\approx\frac{2h_th_r}{d}.$$ The received complex amplitude is the free-space amplitude times $\big|1-e^{-j2\pi\Delta d/\lambda}\big|=2\big|\sin(\pi\Delta d/\lambda)\big|$. For $\Delta d\ll\lambda$ (large $d$) this is $\approx2\pi\Delta d/\lambda=4\pi h_th_r/(\lambda d)$, so the power is $$P_r\approx P_tG_tG_r\Big(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\Big)^2\Big(\frac{4\pi h_th_r}{\lambda d}\Big)^2=P_tG_tG_r\frac{h_t^2h_r^2}{d^4}.$$ Beyond the breakpoint distance $d_b\approx4h_th_r/\lambda$ the received power falls at 40 dB per decade (12 dB per octave), independent of frequency. (Below $d_b$ the two rays alternate between constructive and destructive interference with $d$.) Example: $h_t=30$ m, $h_r=1.5$ m at 900 MHz ($\lambda=0.33$ m): $d_b=540$ m. Doubling the antenna height gives $+6$ dB, which is why base-station towers are tall.

Real environments fall between $d^{-2}$ and $d^{-4}$; the empirical log-distance model summarises them: $$\mathrm{PL}(d)=\mathrm{PL}(d_0)+10\,n\log_{10}\frac d{d_0}+X_\sigma,\qquad X_\sigma\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\ \mathrm{dB}.$$

The random term $X_\sigma$ is shadowing: a slow variation, over tens of metres, caused by buildings and terrain obstructing the path, log-normal because the many independent attenuations multiply (their logs add: the central limit theorem in dB). Its correct handling requires a shadow-fade margin: to make the signal exceed the threshold at the cell edge with probability $1-P_{\rm out}$, the median received power must be $z\sigma$ above it, where $Q(z)=P_{\rm out}$: $z=1.28$ for $P_{\rm out}=10\%$ (margin $10.3$ dB at $\sigma=8$ dB), $z=1.64$ for 5% (13.2 dB). A margin of 10 dB with $n=3.5$ costs $10^{10/35}=1.9\times$ in range.

Engineering practice · the Okumura–Hata formula

The urban median path loss of Hata (valid for 150–1500 MHz, base-station height $h_b=30$–200 m, mobile height $h_m=1$–10 m, range 1–20 km) is $$L_{\rm urban}=69.55+26.16\log f_{\rm MHz}-13.82\log h_b-a(h_m)+(44.9-6.55\log h_b)\log d_{\rm km},$$ $a(h_m)=(1.1\log f-0.7)h_m-(1.56\log f-0.8)$ for a small/medium city. For $f=900$ MHz, $h_b=30$ m, $h_m=1.5$ m the slope is $44.9-6.55\log30=35.2$ dB/decade, an exponent $n=3.5$; at 1 km $L=126.4$ dB and at 5 km $L=151.0$ dB, compared to free space 105.5 dB at 5 km: 45 dB of extra loss from the city. Suburban and open areas subtract 10 to 30 dB. COST 231 extends it to 2 GHz; 3GPP’s UMa and UMi models extend to 100 GHz. Indoor: add wall penetration losses (a few dB per light wall, 10–15 dB for brick/concrete, 20+ for metallised glass), floor losses (15–20 dB each). Millimetre waves: add absorption (≈15 dB/km from oxygen at 60 GHz; 5–10 dB/km in heavy rain at 28 GHz) and human-body blockage (20–40 dB).

Small-scale fading

Over distances of a wavelength or less the signal changes by tens of dB. At the receiver, $L$ scattered waves with random amplitudes $\alpha_l$, delays $\tau_l$ and arrival angles $\theta_l$ add as phasors; moving $\lambda/2$ changes the relative phases by $\pi$ and turns constructive into destructive interference. With many scatterers and no dominant path the sum is a complex Gaussian (central limit theorem): the envelope is Rayleigh, $p(r)=\frac r{\sigma^2}e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}$, the power $\gamma\propto r^2$ is exponential, $p(\gamma)=\frac1{\bar\gamma}e^{-\gamma/\bar\gamma}$ (Chapter 4). With a dominant (line-of-sight) component of power ratio $K$ to the scattered power the envelope is Rician ($K=0$: Rayleigh; $K\to\infty$: no fading). Two independent questions then decide what the receiver sees: how fast does the channel change in time (Doppler) and how selective is it in frequency (delay spread).

Time selectivity: Doppler spread and coherence time

Derivation · Doppler, Clarke’s spectrum and the coherence time

A receiver moving at speed $v$ at angle $\theta$ to the direction of an incoming plane wave sees the carrier shifted by $f_D=\dfrac vc f_c\cos\theta=f_m\cos\theta$, with maximum Doppler $f_m=vf_c/c$ (100 km/h at 2.1 GHz: $f_m=194$ Hz; 350 km/h at 2.6 GHz: 842 Hz; walking at 1.4 m/s at 5 GHz: 23 Hz). In Clarke’s isotropic model the arrival angle is uniform, $\theta\sim U(0,2\pi)$. The Doppler shift $f=f_m\cos\theta$ has density $p(f)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{f_m^2-f^2}}$ ($|f|coherence time $T_c\approx0.24/f_m$ to $0.42/f_m$ depending on the definition: a channel is essentially constant over a time shorter than about a quarter of the Doppler period. At 100 km/h and 2.1 GHz: $T_c\approx1.2$ ms. Spacing for fade independence: the first zero of $J_0(2\pi\Delta x/\lambda)$ is at $\Delta x\approx0.38\lambda$, so antennas about 5 cm apart at 2.4 GHz see nearly independent fading.

The signal bandwidth versus Doppler spread decides fast vs slow fading: if the symbol time $T_s\ll T_c$ (i.e. $B_s\gg f_m$) the channel is constant during a symbol (slow fading: the usual case, and the one assumed in all channel-estimation schemes); if $T_s>T_c$ (rare: slow data rate, fast vehicle) the channel changes within a symbol (fast fading), causing an irreducible error floor. Fades are deep but short: for Rayleigh fading the level-crossing rate (crossings per second of a threshold $\rho=R/R_{\rm rms}$ in the downward direction) and average fade duration are $$N_R=\sqrt{2\pi}\,f_m\rho\,e^{-\rho^2},\qquad\bar\tau=\frac{e^{\rho^2}-1}{\rho f_m\sqrt{2\pi}}.$$ For $f_m=100$ Hz and a $-20$ dB threshold ($\rho=0.1$): 24.8 crossings per second, each lasting on average 0.4 ms: deep fades are brief, which is why interleaved coding can bridge them.

Frequency selectivity: delay spread and coherence bandwidth

The multipath components arrive over a range of delays; the power-delay profile $P(\tau)$ has mean delay $\bar\tau$ and rms delay spread $\tau_{\rm rms}=\sqrt{\overline{\tau^2}-\bar\tau^2}$ (typical values: indoor 20–100 ns; urban 0.3–2 µs; hilly 3–10 µs; Chapter 20’s cyclic prefixes exist for these). The Fourier transform of $P(\tau)$ gives the frequency correlation of the channel; two frequencies are strongly correlated (0.5) if they differ by less than the coherence bandwidth $$B_c\approx\frac{1}{5\tau_{\rm rms}}\quad(0.5\ \text{correlation}),\qquad\approx\frac1{50\tau_{\rm rms}}\ (0.9).$$ $\tau_{\rm rms}=1$ µs gives $B_c\approx200$ kHz. A signal with bandwidth $B\ll B_c$ undergoes flat fading (a single complex gain; symbol time $T_s\gg\tau_{\rm rms}$: no ISI); with $B\gtrsim B_c$ it is frequency-selective: ISI, the problem OFDM and equalisers solve.

What Rayleigh fading does to the error rate

Derivation · average BER in Rayleigh fading

In flat slow fading the received SNR $\gamma$ is constant over a bit but random from bit to bit, exponentially distributed with mean $\bar\gamma$. The average BER for coherent BPSK is the AWGN error probability averaged over $\gamma$. Use Craig’s form $Q(x)=\frac1\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{-x^2/(2\sin^2\theta)}d\theta$ with $x=\sqrt{2\gamma}$ and integrate over $\gamma$ first (the moment generating function of the exponential, $\int_0^\infty e^{-s\gamma}\frac1{\bar\gamma}e^{-\gamma/\bar\gamma}d\gamma=\frac{1}{1+s\bar\gamma}$): $$P_b=\frac1\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{1+\bar\gamma/\sin^2\theta}=\frac12\Big[1-\sqrt{\frac{\bar\gamma}{1+\bar\gamma}}\Big]\ \ \xrightarrow{\ \bar\gamma\gg1\ }\ \ \frac1{4\bar\gamma}.$$ The error probability falls only as the inverse of the SNR, not exponentially (Chapter 15): 10 dB buys a factor 10, not a factor $10^{4}$. Numbers: $\bar\gamma=10$ dB: $P_b=0.023$ (AWGN: $3.9\times10^{-6}$); $20$ dB: $2.5\times10^{-3}$; $30$ dB: $2.5\times10^{-4}$. To reach $10^{-5}$ requires $\bar\gamma=25\,000=44$ dB compared with 9.6 dB for AWGN: a fading penalty of 34 dB. The mechanism: the average is dominated by the rare deep fades. The probability that $\gamma<\gamma_0$ is $1-e^{-\gamma_0/\bar\gamma}\approx\gamma_0/\bar\gamma$ — linear in $1/\bar\gamma$ — and when a bit falls in such a fade it is wrong with probability ½. Raising power cannot fix this economically; diversity, coding with interleaving, and adaptive modulation can.

Diversity

If the receiver gets $L$ independent copies of the signal (branches) fading independently, the probability that all of them are in a deep fade at once is the product of the individual probabilities: $\approx(\gamma_0/\bar\gamma)^L$. The slope of the BER curve on a log–log plot, the diversity order, becomes $L$. Sources: space (antennas $\gtrsim\lambda/2$ apart, in rich scattering), polarisation, time (coding plus interleaving over more than $T_c$), frequency (OFDM subcarriers, wideband Rake, frequency hopping over more than $B_c$), and angle.

Derivation · maximal-ratio combining (MRC)

Branch $l$ receives $y_l=h_lx+n_l$, with independent noise $n_l$ of variance $N_0/2$ per real dimension and known complex gains $h_l$. Form $z=\sum_lw_ly_l=x\sum_lw_lh_l+\sum_lw_ln_l$. The SNR is $\dfrac{|\sum w_lh_l|^2E_s}{(N_0)\sum|w_l|^2}$; by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality it is maximised by $w_l=h_l^{*}$ (match each branch’s weight to its own channel: strong branches count more, phases are aligned), giving $$\gamma_{\rm MRC}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_l,\qquad\gamma_l=\frac{|h_l|^2E_s}{N_0}.$$ The SNRs add. For independent Rayleigh branches with equal mean $\bar\gamma$ each, the sum of $L$ exponentials is a gamma (chi-square with $2L$ degrees of freedom) variable with $p(\gamma)=\dfrac{\gamma^{L-1}e^{-\gamma/\bar\gamma}}{\bar\gamma^L(L-1)!}$, and the BER of BPSK is $$P_b=\Big[\frac{1-\mu}2\Big]^L\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\binom{L-1+k}{k}\Big[\frac{1+\mu}2\Big]^k,\qquad\mu=\sqrt{\frac{\bar\gamma}{1+\bar\gamma}}\ \xrightarrow{\ \bar\gamma\gg1\ }\ \binom{2L-1}{L}\Big(\frac1{4\bar\gamma}\Big)^L.$$ (For $L=1$ this is the previous formula.) Diversity order $L$: the curve falls at $L$ decades per 10 dB. Numbers (per-branch average SNR needed for $10^{-5}$): $L=1$: 44.0 dB; $L=2$: 21.3 dB; $L=4$: 10.0 dB, already within 0.4 dB of the AWGN requirement. There is in addition an array gain: the total mean SNR is $L\bar\gamma$ (3 dB for $L=2$).

Selection combining simply picks the branch with the largest SNR: the CDF of the maximum of $L$ i.i.d. exponentials is $(1-e^{-\gamma/\bar\gamma})^L$ and the mean SNR is $\bar\gamma\sum_{k=1}^L1/k$ ($1.5\bar\gamma$ for $L=2$: 1.76 dB; MRC would give $2\bar\gamma$, 3 dB). It needs no phase information or co-phasing and uses one RF chain: attractive in cheap receivers (the Wi-Fi “antenna diversity” of 2000s routers). Equal-gain combining co-phases and adds with equal weights (within 1 dB of MRC for $L$ small).

Transmit diversity: the Alamouti code

Derivation · the Alamouti space-time block code (2 transmit, 1 receive antenna)

Mobiles cannot easily carry two antennas, but base stations can: can transmit diversity be had with no feedback of channel state? Alamouti (1998) sends two symbols $s_1,s_2$ over two time slots from two antennas: $$\text{slot 1: antenna 1 }s_1,\ \text{antenna 2 }s_2;\qquad\text{slot 2: antenna 1 }-s_2^*,\ \text{antenna 2 }s_1^*.$$ With the channel constant over the two slots ($h_1,h_2$ from antennas 1 and 2), the receiver gets $r_1=h_1s_1+h_2s_2+n_1$, $r_2=-h_1s_2^*+h_2s_1^*+n_2$. Combine $$\tilde s_1=h_1^*r_1+h_2r_2^*=(|h_1|^2+|h_2|^2)s_1+h_1^*n_1+h_2n_2^*,$$ $$\tilde s_2=h_2^*r_1-h_1r_2^*=(|h_1|^2+|h_2|^2)s_2+h_2^*n_1-h_1n_2^*,$$ because the cross terms cancel: the code’s $2\times2$ matrix is orthogonal ($\mathbf G^H\mathbf G=(|s_1|^2+|s_2|^2)I$). Each symbol is detected independently with SNR $(|h_1|^2+|h_2|^2)E_s/(2N_0)$ — the sum of two branch SNRs, with $E_s/2$ per antenna for a fixed total transmit power: diversity order 2, rate 1, no channel knowledge at the transmitter, trivial linear decoding. Its BER is that of two-branch MRC at $\bar\gamma/2$ per branch (a 3 dB penalty compared with receive MRC for the same total power, because no array gain at the transmitter without CSI). It is the only complex orthogonal STBC with rate 1; it appears in 3G (WCDMA transmit diversity), WiMAX, LTE (SFBC), Wi-Fi 11n.

MIMO: using the scattering

With $N_t$ transmit and $N_r$ receive antennas and a narrowband (flat) channel, the received vector is $$\mathbf y=\mathbf H\mathbf x+\mathbf n,\qquad\mathbf H\in\mathbb C^{N_r\times N_t},\quad H_{ij}\ \text{the gain from transmit antenna }j\text{ to receive antenna }i.$$ When scatterers are plentiful the entries of $\mathbf H$ are nearly independent complex Gaussians; with no scattering (line of sight, antennas close together) $\mathbf H$ is nearly rank 1.

Derivation · MIMO capacity by the SVD

Write the singular value decomposition $\mathbf H=\mathbf U\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf V^H$ with unitary $\mathbf U,\mathbf V$ and $\boldsymbol\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1\ge\dots\ge\sigma_r\ge0)$, $r=\mathrm{rank}\,\mathbf H\le\min(N_t,N_r)$. Precode $\mathbf x=\mathbf V\tilde{\mathbf x}$ and decode $\tilde{\mathbf y}=\mathbf U^H\mathbf y$. Since $\mathbf U^H\mathbf U=\mathbf I$ and unitary transformations preserve white Gaussian noise, $$\tilde y_i=\sigma_i\tilde x_i+\tilde n_i,\qquad i=1,\dots,r:$$ the MIMO channel is equivalent to $r$ parallel, non-interfering scalar channels with gains $\sigma_i^2$ (the eigenvalues of $\mathbf H\mathbf H^H$). With total transmit power $P$ divided equally among the $N_t$ antennas (no channel knowledge at the transmitter) and noise power $N_0B$, per-antenna SNR $\rho/N_t$ with $\rho=P/(N_0B)$: $$\boxed{\ C=\log_2\det\Big(\mathbf I_{N_r}+\frac{\rho}{N_t}\mathbf H\mathbf H^H\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\log_2\Big(1+\frac{\rho}{N_t}\sigma_i^2\Big)\ \ \text{bit/s/Hz}\ }$$ With channel knowledge at the transmitter the optimal power allocation across the eigenmodes is water-filling (Chapter 11). Scaling. For an i.i.d. Rayleigh channel with $N_t=N_r=N$ the $N$ eigenvalues are of the same order and at high SNR $C\approx N\log_2\rho+\text{const}$: capacity grows linearly with the number of antennas, with no extra power or bandwidth, versus $\log_2\rho$ for one antenna. The extra degrees of freedom are “spatial streams”. Numbers (mean over fading, 20 dB SNR): $1\times1$: 5.9; $2\times2$: 11.3; $4\times4$: 22.1; $8\times8$: 44.0 bit/s/Hz. Special cases. $N_t=1$, $N_r=N$ (SIMO): $C=\log_2(1+\rho\|\mathbf h\|^2)$: the SNR gain $N$ (MRC) is only a log-gain. $N_t=N$, $N_r=1$ (MISO) without CSI: no array gain; with CSI, beamforming $\mathbf x=\mathbf h^H/\|\mathbf h\|\,s$ gives the full array gain $N$.

The three cannot all be maximised: the Zheng–Tse diversity–multiplexing trade-off says that at multiplexing gain $r$ (rate $r\log\rho$) the best diversity order is $(N_t-r)(N_r-r)$ (piecewise-linear interpolation between $N_tN_r$ and 0 as $r$ goes from 0 to $\min(N_t,N_r)$): a system sends many streams at high SNR and one robust stream (or beamformed) at low SNR, and rank adaptation in LTE/5G chooses between them every few milliseconds on the basis of channel reports. Multi-user MIMO serves several users on the same time–frequency resource by spatial separation (zero-forcing precoding); massive MIMO (64–256 base-station antennas, many users) exploits “channel hardening” and nearly orthogonal user channels so that simple linear processing approaches capacity.

Engineering practice · MIMO in the standards, and what spoils it

LTE downlink: up to 8 layers (2×2 and 4×4 in the field), codebook-based precoding; Wi-Fi 802.11n/ac/ax: up to 4 (n), 8 (ac wave 2, ax) spatial streams, with a 2×2 router doubling the peak rate of a 1×1 phone; 5G NR sub-6 GHz: 64T64R massive MIMO radios with beam-sweeping in mmWave. Impairments: line-of-sight with closely-spaced antennas gives a rank-one channel (high $K$-factor); antenna spacing below $\lambda/2$ or correlated scatterers reduce the effective rank (the Kronecker model: $\mathbf H=\mathbf R_r^{1/2}\mathbf H_w\mathbf R_t^{1/2}$); the SNR must be high enough ($>15$–20 dB) to use more than one stream; the channel must be known: pilot overhead scales with the number of transmit antennas, and in fast fading the CSI is out of date (the feedback delay vs $T_c$). Hence smartphones show 2 streams most of the time and 4 only at high SNR near the cell centre.

GATE pattern · wireless channels
  • Link budget arithmetic in dB (Friis; EIRP; noise power $kTB$; sensitivity; $E_b/N_0$ link equation); FSPL dB formula; two-ray $d^{-4}$; path-loss exponent.
  • Doppler $f_m=vf_c/c$; coherence time $\approx1/(2f_m)$ or $0.423/f_m$; coherence bandwidth $\approx1/(5\tau_{\rm rms})$; classification flat/selective, slow/fast; rms delay spread of a given profile.
  • Rayleigh facts: envelope and power distributions; $P(\gamma<\gamma_0)=1-e^{-\gamma_0/\bar\gamma}$; BPSK BER $\approx1/4\bar\gamma$; diversity order from the slope; MRC SNR sum; Alamouti decoding.
  • MIMO capacity $\log_2\det(\mathbf I+\frac\rho{N_t}\mathbf H\mathbf H^H)$ for a given $\mathbf H$; rank and number of streams.

Set A · Concept checks

In free space with fixed antennas, doubling both the distance and the carrier frequency changes the received power by
  1. $-6$ dB
  2. $-12$ dB
  3. $-3$ dB
  4. $-24$ dB

$L_{\rm fs}\propto d^2f^2$: each doubling costs 6 dB; two doublings: $12$ dB. (B).

In the two-ray ground-reflection model, for distances beyond the breakpoint, the received power falls with distance $d$ as
  1. $d^{-1}$
  2. $d^{-2}$
  3. $d^{-4}$
  4. $d^{-6}$

$P_r\propto h_t^2h_r^2/d^4$: 40 dB per decade. (C).

Which statements about flat Rayleigh fading are correct?
  1. The received power (SNR) is exponentially distributed
  2. The average BPSK bit error probability falls only as $1/(4\bar\gamma)$ at high SNR
  3. A 10 dB increase in average SNR reduces the BER by roughly a factor of 10
  4. The envelope has a Gaussian distribution

(A), (B), (C) true. (D) false: the real and imaginary parts are Gaussian; the envelope is Rayleigh. A, B, C.

A signal of bandwidth 5 MHz is transmitted in an environment with rms delay spread 1 µs. The channel is
  1. flat fading, since 5 MHz is a narrow band
  2. fast fading
  3. flat, with no ISI
  4. frequency-selective, since $B\gg B_c\approx200$ kHz

$B_c\approx1/(5\tau_{\rm rms})=200$ kHz $\ll5$ MHz: frequency-selective, needing an equaliser or OFDM. (D).

The Alamouti scheme with two transmit antennas and one receive antenna achieves
  1. diversity order 2 and rate 1 without channel knowledge at the transmitter
  2. diversity order 2 and rate 2
  3. an array gain of 3 dB at the transmitter
  4. diversity order 1 and rate 2

Orthogonal $2\times2$ code: two symbols in two slots (rate 1), decoded with SNR $\propto|h_1|^2+|h_2|^2$ (order 2), with no CSI at the transmitter. (A).

Which statements about MIMO capacity are correct for an $N\times N$ i.i.d. Rayleigh channel at high SNR?
  1. It increases as $\log_2N$
  2. It increases approximately linearly with $N$ at fixed total transmit power
  3. It requires a line-of-sight channel
  4. It requires a scattering-rich channel with full-rank $\mathbf H$

$C\approx N\log_2\rho$: linear in $N$ (B); needs rank $N$, hence scattering (D); a pure LOS channel has rank 1 (C false). B, D.

Set B · Numerical practice

The free-space path loss at 5 GHz over 100 m is ______ dB.

$32.44+20\log_{10}0.1+20\log_{10}5000=32.44-20+73.98=86.4$ dB.

A transmitter radiates 30 dBm into an antenna of gain 10 dBi; the receive antenna is 0 dBi; the path loss is 100 dB. The received power is ______ dBm.

$30+10+0-100=-60$ dBm.

The receiver noise floor for a bandwidth of 20 MHz and a noise figure of 6 dB is ______ dBm.

$-174+10\log_{10}(2\times10^7)+6=-174+73+6=-95$ dBm.

A 2.4 GHz link has $P_t=20$ dBm, 0 dBi antennas and receiver sensitivity $-90$ dBm. The maximum free-space range is ______ km.

Allowed loss $20+90=110$ dB. $20\log d_{\rm km}=110-32.44-67.60=9.96$, $d=10^{0.498}=3.15$ km.

Base station height 50 m, mobile height 2 m, carrier 1.8 GHz. The two-ray breakpoint distance $4h_th_r/\lambda$ is ______ m.

$\lambda=0.1667$ m; $4\times50\times2/0.1667=2400$ m.

Log-normal shadowing has $\sigma=6$ dB. The margin needed so that the signal exceeds the threshold at 95% of locations ($z=1.645$) is ______ dB.

$1.645\times6=9.87$ dB.

A car travels at 108 km/h and the carrier is 1.8 GHz. The maximum Doppler shift is ______ Hz.

$v=30$ m/s; $f_m=30\times1.8\times10^9/3\times10^8=180$ Hz. Coherence time $\approx0.24/f_m=1.3$ ms.

A channel has rms delay spread 2 µs. The coherence bandwidth, using $B_c\approx1/(5\tau_{\rm rms})$, is ______ kHz.

$1/(5\times2\times10^{-6})=10^5$ Hz $=100$ kHz.

BPSK in flat Rayleigh fading with average SNR 20 dB: the average bit error probability, $\frac12[1-\sqrt{\bar\gamma/(1+\bar\gamma)}]$, is ______ (decimal).

$\bar\gamma=100$: $\frac12(1-\sqrt{100/101})=\frac12(1-0.99504)=0.00248$ (the approximation $1/(4\bar\gamma)=0.0025$).

In Rayleigh fading the probability that the instantaneous SNR is more than 10 dB below its mean ($\gamma<0.1\bar\gamma$) is ______.

$P=1-e^{-0.1}=0.0952$: the channel is 10 dB below its mean about 1 time in 10.

To limit outage (SNR below the decoding threshold) to 1% in flat Rayleigh fading, the mean SNR must exceed the threshold by ______ dB.

$1-e^{-\gamma_{\rm th}/\bar\gamma}=0.01\Rightarrow\bar\gamma/\gamma_{\rm th}=99.5=20.0$ dB: a fading margin of 20 dB.

Three-branch MRC receives with each branch having an average SNR of 5 dB. The mean SNR at the combiner output is ______ dB.

The SNRs add: $3\times10^{0.5}=9.49\ (9.77\ \text{dB})$.

With channel gains $h_1=0.5+0.5j$ and $h_2=1-0.5j$ in an Alamouti $2\times1$ system, the effective gain $|h_1|^2+|h_2|^2$ multiplying each decoded symbol is ______.

$|h_1|^2=0.5$, $|h_2|^2=1.25$; sum $=1.75$.

A $2\times2$ MIMO channel has $\mathbf H=\mathbf I_2$ and SNR $\rho=15$ dB (31.6) with equal power on the two antennas. The capacity $\log_2\det(\mathbf I+\frac\rho2\mathbf H\mathbf H^H)$ is ______ bit/s/Hz.

$2\log_2(1+15.81)=2\times4.07=8.14$.

A $2\times2$ channel with all entries equal to 1 (a perfectly correlated, rank-1 channel) at $\rho=10$ dB (10): the capacity is ______ bit/s/Hz.

$\mathbf H\mathbf H^H=\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\2&2\end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues 4 and 0: $C=\log_2(1+\frac{10}{2}\cdot4)=\log_2 21=4.39$. (The identity channel at the same SNR gives $2\log_2(1+5)=5.17$: the correlated channel loses the second stream.)

The array gain of an 8-antenna transmit beamformer with perfect channel knowledge is ______ dB.

$10\log_{10}8=9.03$ dB.

Set C · Exam-style problems

A geostationary satellite at 36 000 km transmits at 12 GHz with an EIRP of 50 dBW. A ground dish has a gain of 40 dBi. Using only free-space loss, the received power is ______ dBW.

$L_{\rm fs}=32.44+91.13+81.58=205.15$ dB. $P_r=50+40-205.15=-115.2$ dBW ($=-85.2$ dBm).

For the downlink above add 2 dB of atmospheric and pointing loss, a receiver figure of merit $G/T=15$ dB/K and a data rate of 36 Mbit/s. Using $E_b/N_0=\mathrm{EIRP}-L+G/T+228.6-10\log R_b$ (dB), the $E_b/N_0$ is ______ dB.

$50-(205.15+2)+15+228.6-75.56=10.9$ dB. (Enough for a rate-¾ QPSK link with coding, Chapters 15 and 18.)

BPSK with 2-branch MRC in independent Rayleigh fading, each branch having mean SNR 20 dB. Using the exact formula, the BER is ______ $\times10^{-5}$.

$\bar\gamma=100$, $\mu=0.99504$: $\big(\frac{1-\mu}2\big)^2\big[1+2\frac{1+\mu}2\big]=(0.00248)^2(2.995)=1.84\times10^{-5}$. (Single branch: $2.5\times10^{-3}$: a 135-fold improvement; the asymptote $\binom32(1/400)^2=1.9\times10^{-5}$.)

A vehicle at 100 km/h uses a 900 MHz carrier and a 200 kHz bandwidth data link in an urban area with $\tau_{\rm rms}=0.5$ µs. The channel is best described as
  1. fast and frequency-selective
  2. slow and flat
  3. fast and flat
  4. slow and frequency-selective

$f_m=83$ Hz, $T_c\approx0.24/f_m\approx2.9$ ms $\gg T_s=5$ µs (slow). $B_c=1/(5\times0.5\ \mu\text{s})=400$ kHz $>200$ kHz (flat). (B).

Chapter 21 in one breath

  • Friis: $P_r=P_tG_tG_r(\lambda/4\pi d)^2$; $L_{\rm fs}=32.44+20\log d_{\rm km}+20\log f_{\rm MHz}$; $=P_tA_tA_r/\lambda^2d^2$ (aperture view: higher $f$ needs beamforming, not higher loss per se). Link budget: $P_r=P_t+G_t+G_r-L-M$ vs sensitivity $-174+10\log B+F+\mathrm{SNR}_{\min}$; $E_b/N_0=\mathrm{EIRP}-L+G/T+228.6-10\log R_b$.
  • Two-ray: $P_r\propto(h_th_r)^2/d^4$ beyond $d_b=4h_th_r/\lambda$. Log-distance $n=2..5$ plus log-normal shadowing ($z\sigma$ margin); Hata for cities.
  • Small-scale: Rayleigh; $f_m=vf_c/c$, Jakes spectrum, $R(\Delta t)=J_0(2\pi f_m\Delta t)$, $T_c\approx0.24/f_m$; $B_c\approx1/5\tau_{\rm rms}$; flat/selective, slow/fast; LCR $\sqrt{2\pi}f_m\rho e^{-\rho^2}$.
  • BPSK in Rayleigh: $\frac12[1-\sqrt{\bar\gamma/(1+\bar\gamma)}]\approx1/4\bar\gamma$: 34 dB penalty at $10^{-5}$.
  • Diversity order $L$: MRC (weights $h^*$, SNRs add), SC ($\bar\gamma\sum1/k$), Alamouti (2 Tx, rate 1, no CSIT). BER $\approx\binom{2L-1}L(4\bar\gamma)^{-L}$.
  • MIMO: $C=\log_2\det(\mathbf I+\frac\rho{N_t}\mathbf H\mathbf H^H)=\sum\log_2(1+\frac\rho{N_t}\sigma_i^2)$ via SVD: linear in $\min(N_t,N_r)$; beamforming array gain; diversity–multiplexing trade-off.
