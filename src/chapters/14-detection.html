History · a filter born in the radar war

In 1943, at RCA Laboratories, D. O. North asked a question that had no answer yet: what is the best possible linear filter for detecting a known pulse buried in noise? His classified report showed that the best filter has an impulse response equal to the pulse time-reversed: the filter is "matched" to the signal. The result was published openly only in 1963, by which time Van Vleck and Middleton had independently named it the matched filter (1946) and V. A. Kotelnikov had built the whole theory of optimum reception in his 1947 doctoral thesis. In 1965 John Wozencraft and Irwin Jacobs wrote the textbook that taught engineers to think of signals as points in a space, which is the viewpoint of this chapter.

Real world · hearing a whisper below the noise

A GPS satellite's signal reaches the ground at about $-130$ dBm. The receiver's own thermal noise in the 2 MHz signal bandwidth is about $-111$ dBm: the signal is 19 dB below the noise, invisible on any oscilloscope. Yet your phone locks on in seconds, because it correlates the received waveform with a stored copy of the satellite's 1023-chip code, a matched filter whose energy gain $10\log_{10}1023=30$ dB lifts the signal 11 dB above the noise. The same idea is used in radar "pulse compression", RFID readers, CDMA phones, and every Wi-Fi packet preamble.

The decision problem

One of $M$ messages is sent, using signal $s_i(t)$, $0\le t\le T$. The receiver observes $r(t)=s_i(t)+n(t)$ with $n(t)$ white Gaussian noise of PSD $N_0/2$. It must output a guess $\hat\imath$. The probability of error $P_e$ depends on the decision rule, and the best rule is simple to state.

Two decision rules

MAP (maximum a posteriori): choose the $i$ that maximises the posterior $P(s_i\mid r)\propto P_i\,f(r\mid s_i)$. It minimises the probability of error. ML (maximum likelihood): choose the $i$ that maximises the likelihood $f(r\mid s_i)$. It is MAP with equal priors.

The picture for one real number $r=\pm A+n$ with $n\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$: two bell curves, one centred at $+A$ (weighted by $P_1$) and one at $-A$ (weighted by $P_0$). The decision threshold sits where the weighted curves cross. Everything to the right is declared "1". The error is the area of each bell that falls on the wrong side: $$P_e=P_0\,Q\!\Big(\frac{A+\tau}{\sigma}\Big)+P_1\,Q\!\Big(\frac{A-\tau}{\sigma}\Big),\qquad\tau^*=\frac{\sigma^2}{2A}\ln\frac{P_0}{P_1}.$$ For equal priors $\tau^*=0$ and $P_e=Q(A/\sigma)$. If $P_1>P_0$, $\tau^*<0$: the threshold moves towards the unlikely symbol, enlarging the region of the likelier one.

Two weighted pdfs and a threshold. Dragging the threshold changes the shaded error areas; the total is smallest exactly at the MAP threshold (green marker). With equal priors this is the midpoint; with unequal priors it shifts. Try setting the threshold slider to the value shown in the readout as τ*.
Worked example · GATE 2019 Q.47

$X=-1$ w.p. 0.2, $X=+1$ w.p. 0.8; $Y=X+N$ with $N\sim U[-2,2]$. Decide $+1$ if $Y>\theta$, $\theta\in[-1,1]$. Then $P(\text{err}\mid X=+1)=P(1+N\le\theta)=\frac{\theta+1}4$, $P(\text{err}\mid X=-1)=P(-1+N>\theta)=\frac{1-\theta}4$. $P_e=0.8\frac{\theta+1}4+0.2\frac{1-\theta}4=\frac{1+0.6\theta}4$, minimised at $\theta=-1$: $P_e=\frac{0.4}{4}=\mathbf{0.1}$. (Uniform noise, so the "tails" are linear, and the optimum threshold goes to the extreme: decide "$+1$" always, which costs only the prior of $-1$ weighted by the overlap.)

Worked example · GATE 2014 Set 4 Q.49, Q.50

$P(X=+1)=0.75$, $P(X=-1)=0.25$, $Y=X+Z$: the optimum threshold is $\frac{\sigma^2}2\ln\frac{0.25}{0.75}<0$: strictly negative (option C). The Z-channel question: input 0 or 1 equally likely; 0→0 with prob. 1, 1→1 with prob. 0.75, 1→0 with prob. 0.25. $P(\text{out}=0)=0.5+0.125=0.625$; $P(\text{in}=0\mid\text{out}=0)=\frac{0.5}{0.625}=\mathbf{0.8}$ (Bayes, Chapter 1).

Signals as points: the geometry of detection

Here is the idea that turns waveform detection into high-school geometry. Choose a set of orthonormal functions $\phi_1(t),\ldots,\phi_N(t)$ that span all the signals ($\int\phi_j\phi_k=\delta_{jk}$; Gram–Schmidt will construct them). Then each signal is a coefficient vector $\mathbf s_i=(s_{i1},\ldots,s_{iN})$, $s_{ij}=\int s_i(t)\phi_j(t)dt$, and $$\text{energy}\ E_i=\|\mathbf s_i\|^2,\qquad\text{inner product}\int s_is_j\,dt=\mathbf s_i\cdot\mathbf s_j,\qquad\text{distance }d_{ij}=\|\mathbf s_i-\mathbf s_j\|.$$ The noise projected onto each $\phi_j$ is an independent $\mathcal N(0,N_0/2)$ (white noise is "equally strong in every direction", which is why it is white). So the receiver sees a point $\mathbf r=\mathbf s_i+\mathbf n$: the transmitted point plus a round Gaussian cloud of variance $\sigma^2=N_0/2$ per axis. The likelihood is $f(\mathbf r\mid\mathbf s_i)\propto\exp(-\|\mathbf r-\mathbf s_i\|^2/N_0)$, so:

ML detection = minimum-distance decoding

With equal priors, choose the signal point nearest to the received point. The decision regions are the Voronoi cells (bounded by perpendicular bisectors). With unequal priors, MAP slides the boundary between $\mathbf s_i$ and $\mathbf s_j$ along the line joining them, towards the less probable point, by $\dfrac{N_0}{2d_{ij}}\ln\dfrac{P_i}{P_j}$ from the midpoint (for antipodal $\pm A$ with $\sigma^2=N_0/2$ this is the $\frac{\sigma^2}{2A}\ln$ formula above).

Choose a constellation. The coloured regions are the ML decision cells; dots are 500 noisy receptions. Raise the noise: dots spill across the boundaries and the measured symbol-error rate goes up. Compare with the exact theoretical value in the readout. Closer points (smaller $d_{\min}$) make errors more likely: the whole game of modulation design is to place points as far apart as possible for a given average energy.

The key formula

For two equally likely signals at distance $d$, the error happens when the noise component along the line joining them (a single $\mathcal N(0,N_0/2)$ variable) exceeds half the distance: $$\boxed{\,P_e=Q\!\Big(\frac{d/2}{\sqrt{N_0/2}}\Big)=Q\!\Big(\frac{d}{\sqrt{2N_0}}\Big)\,}$$ Writing $d^2=\|\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0\|^2=E_1+E_0-2\rho\sqrt{E_0E_1}$ gives the three classic cases (with $E_b$ the average energy per bit, $\gamma=E_b/N_0$):
SchemeSignal points$d^2$$P_e$
Antipodal (BPSK, polar baseband)$\pm\sqrt{E_b}$$4E_b$$Q\big(\sqrt{2\gamma}\big)$
Orthogonal (coherent BFSK)$(\sqrt{E_b},0),(0,\sqrt{E_b})$$2E_b$$Q\big(\sqrt{\gamma}\big)$  (3 dB worse)
On–off (OOK, ASK)$0,\ \sqrt{2E_b}$$2E_b$ (average energy $E_b$)$Q\big(\sqrt{\gamma}\big)$
The comparison tells you why BPSK is the benchmark: for a given average energy, antipodal signals are as far apart as possible. Orthogonal signals are a factor $\sqrt2$ closer, hence 3 dB worse.

Worked examples · real questions

GATE 2019 Q.34. Choose $p=\varphi_1,\ q=-\varphi_1$ (antipodal): $d^2=4$. If $p=\varphi_1$ and $q=\sqrt E\,\varphi_2$ (orthogonal, energy $E$): $d^2=1+E$. Same BER ⇔ same $d$: $1+E=4\Rightarrow E=\mathbf3$.

GATE 2014 Set 2 Q.50. Coherent orthogonal BFSK, $s_{1,2}=\alpha\cos2\pi f_{1,2}t$, $\alpha=4$ mV, $N_0/2=0.5\times10^{-12}$ W/Hz ($N_0=10^{-12}$), data rate 500 kbit/s ($T_b=2\,\mu$s). $E_b=\frac{\alpha^2T_b}2=\frac{16\times10^{-6}\cdot2\times10^{-6}}2=16\times10^{-12}$ J. $P_e=Q(\sqrt{E_b/N_0})=Q(\sqrt{16})=\mathbf{Q(4)}$.

GATE 2014 Set 1 Q.51. BPSK gives $Q(\sqrt\gamma)$ in the paper's notation. The receiver now sees the sum of the outputs of two independent identical AWGN channels: the signal adds coherently (amplitude ×2, power ×4) while the independent noises add in power (×2): SNR doubles, so $P_e=Q(\sqrt{2\gamma})$, $b=\sqrt2=\mathbf{1.41}$. (This is "diversity combining".)

GATE 2014 Set 4 Q.52. Noise is signal-dependent: $Z\sim\mathcal N(\beta X,1)$, so $Y=X(1+\beta)+N$. With $\beta=-0.3$ the effective amplitude is $0.7a$. Given $Q(a)=10^{-8}$ and the stated approximation $Q(v)\approx e^{-v^2/2}$: $a^2/2=\ln10^8=18.42$; $Q(0.7a)\approx e^{-0.49\times18.42}=1.2\times10^{-4}$ ⇒ closest to $\mathbf{10^{-4}}$ (key C). (Using the exact $Q$ would give $10^{-5}$, which isn't an option: use the approximation the question hands you.)

$M$ symbols on a line: GATE's favourite multi-level detection

For $M$ equally likely points on a line separated by $d$, the two end points have one neighbour each, interior points two. With noise standard deviation $\sigma$ and thresholds midway ($d/2$ from each point): $$P_e=\frac{2(M-1)}{M}\,Q\!\Big(\frac{d}{2\sigma}\Big).$$ GATE 2024 Q.45: $s\in\{-4,0,4\}$, $\sigma^2=4$ ⇒ $d=4$, $d/2\sigma=1$: $P_e=\frac{2\cdot2}{3}Q(1)=\mathbf{\tfrac43Q(1)}$ (option B). GATE 2025 Q.41: $s\in\{-2,0,2\}$, thresholds at $\pm1$: $P_e=\frac43P(N>1)$, so $\alpha=\mathbf{4/3}$ (option D). GATE 2020 Q.48: symbols $-3,-1,+1,+2$, unit-variance noise, thresholds $-2,0,1.5$. $P_e(s_1)=Q(1)=0.159$; $P_e(s_2)=2Q(1)=0.317$; $P_e(s_3)=Q(1)+Q(0.5)=0.467$; $P_e(s_4)=Q(0.5)=0.309$. The highest is at $i=3$ (the symbol with neighbours at distances 2 and 1 has the thinnest cell: width 1.5).

Worked example · GATE 2022 Q.63: non-Gaussian ML

$f_{Y|A}(y)=e^{-(y+1)}u(y+1)$, $f_{Y|B}(y)=e^{y-1}[1-u(y-1)]$. ML means: decide for the larger likelihood. For $y<-1$ only $B$ has support, for $y>1$ only $A$. In $-1e^{y-1}\iff y<0$. So decide $A$ on $(-1,0)\cup(1,\infty)$, $B$ on $(-\infty,-1)\cup(0,1)$. Given $A$ (equally likely): error iff $y\in(0,1)$: $\int_0^1e^{-(y+1)}dy=e^{-1}(1-e^{-1})=0.2325$. Given $B$: error iff $y\in(-1,0)$: same $0.2325$. $P_e=\mathbf{0.23}$ (key 0.22–0.25). Lesson: ML is "pick the taller curve at the observed $y$", whatever the curves look like.

The matched filter

For a known pulse $s(t)$ of energy $E$ in white noise, we want the linear filter $h(t)$ that makes the output, sampled at $t=T$, as large as possible compared with the noise. The output signal is $\int h(\tau)s(T-\tau)d\tau$; the output noise variance is $\frac{N_0}2\int h^2d\tau$. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, $$\Big(\int h(\tau)s(T-\tau)d\tau\Big)^2\le\int h^2\,d\tau\cdot\int s^2\,dt=E\!\int h^2\,d\tau,$$ with equality iff $h(\tau)=k\,s(T-\tau)$. So $$\boxed{\,h_{\rm opt}(t)=s(T-t)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H(f)=S^*(f)e^{-j2\pi fT},\qquad\mathrm{SNR}_{\max}=\frac{2E}{N_0}\,}$$ The maximum SNR depends only on the signal's energy, not its shape: that is why long, low-amplitude pulses (spread-spectrum, radar chirps, Barker codes) detect as well as short, high-amplitude ones. The filter output at $T$ equals $\int_0^Tr(t)s(t)dt$: the correlation of the received waveform with the template, so matched filter ≡ correlator (the same number, computed by multiplying and integrating instead of convolving and sampling). For a rectangular pulse the matched filter is simply an integrate-and-dump: integrate $r(t)$ over the symbol and reset.

A Barker-13 code pulse (13 chips, each of height $\pm1$) is buried in noise at an unknown delay. Top: what the antenna sees. Bottom: the output of the matched filter. At $\sigma\approx2$ (6 dB below the noise) the signal is invisible in the raw samples, yet the correlator output has a clear spike (height 52, in the units shown as 1) of width 1 chip: a 17 dB processing gain ($10\log_{10}52$). The little side-lobes are the code's autocorrelation sidelobes: Barker codes make them as small as possible ($\le1$ chip out of 13).
Worked example

Rectangular pulse $A=2$ V, $T=1\,\mu$s, $N_0/2=10^{-9}$ W/Hz. $E=A^2T=4\times10^{-6}$ J. $\mathrm{SNR}_{\max}=\frac{2E}{N_0}=\frac{8\times10^{-6}}{2\times10^{-9}}=4000$ ($36$ dB). The matched filter's output is a triangle of peak $A^2T$ at $t=T$.

The optimum receiver for two signals: correlate with each template, subtract the half-energy biases, and pick the larger. For equiprobable signals of equal energy the biases cancel: just compare the two correlator outputs. With unequal priors add $\frac{N_0}2\ln P_i$.
GATE pattern · detection (the highest-yield topic in digital)
  • Nearly every year contains a "symbol received with Gaussian noise; find the error probability or the optimum threshold" question: 2014 (×4), 2019, 2020, 2022, 2024, 2025, 2026. Method: draw the points, put thresholds midway (or at the MAP location), count neighbours, use $Q(\text{half-distance}/\sigma)$.
  • Know which $\sigma$ is given: variance (square-root it), PSD $N_0/2$ ($\sigma^2=N_0/2$ per correlator output with unit-energy basis).
  • BPSK $Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})$; BFSK $Q(\sqrt{E_b/N_0})$; matched-filter SNR $2E/N_0$.
  • Expect "unequal priors ⇒ threshold direction" (2014) and non-Gaussian ML (2022) occasionally.

$X\in\{-0.5,+0.5\}$ with probabilities $\frac14,\frac34$. The observation is $Y=X+Z$ with $Z$ uniform on $(-1,1)$, independent of $X$. The MAP detector outputs $\hat X=-0.5$ if $Y<\alpha$ and $+0.5$ if $Y\ge\alpha$. The value of $\alpha$ is ______.

If $X=-0.5$ then $Y\in(-1.5,0.5)$; if $X=+0.5$, $Y\in(-0.5,1.5)$. For $Y<-0.5$ only $X=-0.5$ is possible; for $Y>0.5$ only $+0.5$. In the overlap $(-0.5,0.5)$ the two likelihoods are equal ($\frac12$ each), so the priors decide: $\frac34>\frac14$ ⇒ choose $+0.5$. The decision switches at $\alpha=\mathbf{-0.5}$. (With uniform noise the MAP boundary sits at the edge of the overlap, not at the midpoint.)

Chapter 10 in one breath

  • MAP maximises $P_if(r|s_i)$; ML is MAP with equal priors. Unequal priors shift the threshold toward the less likely symbol: $\tau^*=\frac{\sigma^2}{2A}\ln\frac{P_0}{P_1}$.
  • Signal space: orthonormal basis; noise per axis $\mathcal N(0,N_0/2)$; ML = nearest point (Voronoi); $P_e^{\rm bin}=Q\big(d/\sqrt{2N_0}\big)$.
  • BPSK $Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})$; BFSK/OOK $Q(\sqrt{E_b/N_0})$ (3 dB worse). $M$ points on a line: $\frac{2(M-1)}MQ\big(\frac d{2\sigma}\big)$.
  • Matched filter $h(t)=s(T-t)$ ≡ correlator; $\mathrm{SNR}_{\max}=2E/N_0$ depends only on energy. Integrate-and-dump for rectangular pulses.
