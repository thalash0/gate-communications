1. Heat, noise and the price of a bit

Why is noise $kT$? Statistical mechanics says that in thermal equilibrium every independent way a system can store energy (a "degree of freedom") holds on average $\tfrac12kT$. A resistor's electrons jiggle, and Nyquist's 1928 argument imagined the resistor connected to a long transmission line: the line has standing-wave modes, each is a degree of freedom, and in equilibrium each carries energy $kT$ per mode (one for the electric and one for the magnetic field, $\tfrac12kT$ each). The power flowing into a matched load per hertz is then exactly $kT$: Johnson noise is thermodynamics, not a defect of resistors. This is why noise power $kTB$ is independent of what the resistor is made of.

But it cannot be white forever. Planck's quantum law replaces $kT$ by $\dfrac{hf}{e^{hf/kT}-1}$, which equals $kT$ for $hf\ll kT$ and decays for $hf\gg kT$ (otherwise the total noise power $\int kT\,df$ would be infinite, Rayleigh and Jeans' "ultraviolet catastrophe"). At 290 K the crossover is at $kT/h=6$ THz, a million times above your radio's band, which is why "white" is an excellent approximation here, but at optical frequencies and at low temperatures the quantum form matters.

Available thermal noise power per hertz (relative to $kT$) against frequency, classical (dashed flat) versus quantum (Planck). The white-noise assumption of Chapter 3 holds until the curve bends at $f\approx kT/h$. Cool the resistor and the bend moves down: radio-astronomy receivers cooled to 4 K are quantum-limited at $\sim$80 GHz.
The Shannon limit and Landauer's principle

The second law connects information and energy. Gibbs' entropy $S=-k_B\sum p_i\ln p_i$ is Shannon's formula times $k_B$ (Shannon was not copying: both formulas count the same logarithm of possibilities). Maxwell's demon (1867), a tiny being who sorts fast molecules from slow without doing work, seemed to violate the second law until Rolf Landauer (IBM, 1961) showed that erasing one bit of memory must dissipate at least $$E_{\rm erase}\ge k_BT\ln2\approx2.9\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm{J}\quad(T=300\ \mathrm{K}).$$ Now recall Chapter 7: the Shannon limit for reliable communication is $E_b/N_0\ge\ln2$, and for thermal noise $N_0=kT$. Multiply out: $E_b\ge kT\ln2$, the same number. The minimum energy to communicate one bit and the minimum energy to erase one bit are both $kT\ln2$. Modern computing sits about $10^{3}$–$10^{4}$ times above Landauer's bound; wireless receivers are within a few dB of Shannon's.

2. Fourier is everywhere: quantum mechanics, light, crystals, DNA

Uncertainty. The result of Chapter 2, "narrow in time means wide in frequency", is the same mathematics as Heisenberg's principle. A particle's wavefunction in position space and in momentum space ($p=\hbar k$) are a Fourier-transform pair, so $\sigma_x\sigma_p\ge\hbar/2$. For signals, the rms durations satisfy $\sigma_t\sigma_f\ge\frac1{4\pi}$, with equality for the Gaussian pulse (the one that is its own transform). Quantum "uncertainty" is partly just Fourier uncertainty applied to matter waves.

Diffraction. In the Fraunhofer (far-field) limit, the amplitude of light diffracted by an aperture is the Fourier transform of the aperture's transmission function, with spatial frequency $u=\sin\theta/\lambda$. A single slit of width $a$ is a $\rect$ function, so the pattern is $\sinc^2(a\sin\theta/\lambda)$: Chapter 2's rect ↔ sinc pair, on a screen. Two slits are two impulses (a cosine fringe pattern); a grating is an impulse train, whose transform is another impulse train, sharp bright orders at $\sin\theta=m\lambda/d$: sampling, as light.

Top: the aperture (open = height 1). Bottom: the far-field intensity, which is $|\mathcal F\{\text{aperture}\}|^2$. Narrowing the slit widens the pattern (time-bandwidth tradeoff); more slits sharpen the peaks (a longer pulse train has a narrower spectrum). Grating spacing sets the order angles ($d\sin\theta=m\lambda$): exactly the "sampling at interval $d$ replicates the spectrum every $1/d$" of Chapter 2.
History · a helix, in Fourier space

In 1952 Raymond Gosling and Rosalind Franklin recorded the X-ray diffraction pattern of DNA, the famous "Photo 51": a distinct X of spots. X-ray scattering gives the Fourier transform of the electron density. Cochran, Crick and Vand had just worked out that the transform of a helix is a set of crossing diagonal streaks, so an X in the pattern meant a helix; the spacing of the layers told the pitch. Watson and Crick's double helix (1953) was read, literally, out of a Fourier transform, the same corkscrew you rotated in Chapter 2, seen from the other side. Crystals are sampling in 3-D: a lattice is a sum of impulses, its transform is the "reciprocal lattice", and aliasing between zones is what solid-state physicists call Umklapp scattering.

3. Resonance: one equation in many worlds

A series RLC circuit, a mass on a spring with friction, a pendulum, a quartz crystal, an atom radiating light and a laser cavity all obey the same second-order equation. When "kicked", the response is a decaying cosine $e^{-t/\tau}\cos2\pi f_0t$; the frequency response is its Fourier transform, a peak of width $$\Delta f=\frac{f_0}{Q}=\frac1{\pi\tau}\ \ (\text{power half-width}),\qquad Q=\pi f_0\tau,$$ a Lorentzian (Chapter 2: $e^{-at}u(t)\leftrightarrow\frac1{a+j2\pi f}$). In the superheterodyne receiver of Chapter 6, the preselector's $Q$ decides image rejection. In the atom, the excited state's finite lifetime $\tau$ gives the spectral line its natural width $1/2\pi\tau$. A quartz crystal oscillator has $Q\sim10^5$ (hence the stable clocks and carriers of Chapters 5 and 6); a good optical cavity $Q\sim10^{10}$. The mathematics of selectivity is the mathematics of memory: a sharper resonance rings longer.

A resonator of centre frequency 1 (arbitrary units). Left: its ring-down after a kick. Right: its power frequency response and half-power bandwidth $f_0/Q$. High $Q$: narrow and long-ringing. This is the preselector (circuit), the atomic line (physics), the tuning fork (mechanics).

Devices and analog circuits. The mixer works because real devices are non-linear: the diode current $I=I_s(e^{V/V_T}-1)$ expands into $V$, $V^2$, $V^3,\dots$; the $V^2$ term contains the product $\cos\omega_1t\cos\omega_2t$ of Chapter 4 (GATE 2018 asked exactly this with $v_o=av_i+bv_i^2$). The MOSFET's square-law $I_D\propto(V_{GS}-V_T)^2$ does the same. A varactor (junction capacitance varying with reverse bias) is the voltage-controlled capacitor of the FM modulator. Shot noise (Schottky 1918, $\overline{i_n^2}=2qIB$) is the other fundamental noise, from the discreteness of charge, and the Poisson statistics of Chapter 1; transistors also have $1/f$ (flicker) noise. An oscillator is an amplifier with positive feedback satisfying Barkhausen's criterion (loop gain 1, phase 0), which is also what makes Armstrong's regenerative receiver work.

4. Waves, antennas and link budgets

Everything above the antenna is electromagnetics. Maxwell's equations give: the antenna must be a fraction of a wavelength ($\lambda=c/f$); transmission lines reflect when impedances mismatch (an echo: the time-domain cousin of multipath and ISI); waveguides have cutoff frequencies; radio waves spread, so the received power falls with distance. The Friis transmission equation and noise from Chapter 3 combine into the link budget: $$P_r=P_tG_tG_r\Big(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\Big)^2\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{C}{N_0}=\underbrace{P_t+G_t+G_r}_{\text{dB}}-\underbrace{20\log_{10}\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}}_{\text{free-space loss}}-10\log_{10}(kT_{\rm sys}),\qquad\frac{E_b}{N_0}=\frac C{N_0}-10\log_{10}R_b.$$ This one line joins electromagnetics (path loss), noise (Chapter 3), digital detection (the $E_b/N_0$ required for a target BER, Chapter 11) and information theory (maximum rate). Voyager 1 sits almost exactly at the threshold: its 160 bit/s data rate was chosen to make the budget close.

Doppler and relativity. A transmitter moving at velocity $v$ towards you shifts the frequency by $\Delta f=\frac vcf_c$. A low-orbit satellite at 7.5 km/s on a 2 GHz carrier is Doppler-shifted by $\pm50$ kHz; a bullet train at 300 km/h on 900 MHz, by 250 Hz. A coherent receiver must track this shift, otherwise it sees the "frequency error" beating of Chapter 4. And the multipath fading of mobile radio is a sum of many random phasors: the same Rayleigh-envelope mathematics as the narrowband noise of Chapter 3. (GPS satellites must also correct for special- and general-relativistic clock offsets, about 38 μs per day, or position errors would grow by ~10 km per day.)

5. Light as a channel, and a gravitational-wave story

Optical fibre. Light in a glass fibre is a carrier at ~200 THz, with bandwidth orders of magnitude above radio. On–off keying of a laser is ASK. A photodiode converts photons to current with responsivity $R=\dfrac{\eta\,\lambda(\mu\mathrm m)}{1.24}$ A/W (GATE 2019 asked this in the Electron Devices section). Photon arrivals are Poisson (Chapter 1), so even a perfect receiver makes errors: for OOK with $\bar n$ photons in a "1" and none in a "0", a "1" is missed with probability $e^{-\bar n}$ and $P_e=\tfrac12e^{-\bar n}$. To reach $10^{-9}$ you need $\bar n=20$, i.e. 10 photons per bit on average: the quantum limit of direct detection. Wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) is FDM at optical frequencies.

History · September 14, 2015

Two black holes of about 36 and 29 solar masses spiralled together 1.3 billion light-years away and merged. The ripple in spacetime reached Earth and moved LIGO's 4-km mirrors by about $10^{-18}$ m, a thousandth of the size of a proton, far below the instrument's noise at every sample. The signal was found by matched filtering: the LIGO pipeline correlates the data with a bank of thousands of precomputed general-relativity "chirp" templates. The best match had a matched-filter SNR of about 24, the same $\sqrt{2E/N_0}$-style statistic that Chapter 10 derived, and the discovery earned the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics. Penzias and Wilson, a half-century earlier (Chapter 1), found the Big Bang in the hiss; LIGO found black holes in it.

6. Digital design and DSP in disguise

  • Shift registers. The CRC divider of Chapter 12 is a linear-feedback shift register (LFSR): flip-flops plus XOR gates. The same hardware generates scrambling sequences and the Gold codes of GPS and CDMA (two 10-stage LFSRs, 1023-chip codes), whose sharp autocorrelation makes the correlator of Chapter 10 work.
  • Gray code. The Karnaugh map labels its rows and columns in Gray order (adjacent cells differ in one variable) so that logic minimisation is geometric; the constellation labelling of Chapter 11 uses the same code so that adjacent points differ in one bit. Same idea, different subject.
  • Counters are frequency dividers: the ÷N block of the PLL synthesizer. Flip-flops have setup/hold times, and clock jitter and metastability show up in receivers as sampling-time error (Chapter 9's eye diagram).
  • ADC/DAC architectures (flash, SAR, delta-sigma) realise Chapter 8. A delta-sigma converter is delta modulation plus noise shaping, running at a high oversampling rate.
  • OFDM (Wi-Fi, 4G/5G, DSL, DVB-T) is Chapter 2 weaponised. A multipath channel is a convolution, hard to undo; but after a cyclic prefix, it becomes circular convolution, which the FFT diagonalises into independent multiplications on each subcarrier: one tap per subcarrier instead of an equaliser. Wi-Fi 20 MHz uses a 64-point FFT: 312.5 kHz spacing, useful symbol 3.2 μs, plus a 0.8 μs cyclic prefix. LTE uses 15 kHz spacing: 66.7 μs + 4.7 μs. Orthogonality of subcarriers is the zero-crossing property of Chapter 9.

7. Control, linear algebra and learning

Feedback. The PLL (Chapter 5), the AGC (Chapter 6) and adaptive equalisers (Chapter 9) are control loops. Their analysis is the Bode/root-locus/Nyquist-stability toolkit of your Control Systems syllabus (a second theorem of Harry Nyquist, 1932): the PLL's loop bandwidth trades tracking speed against noise, and "cycle slipping" is loss of lock. Norbert Wiener's optimal filter of Chapter 3 (1942) was extended by Rudolf Kálmán (1960) to time-varying systems; the Kalman filter navigated Apollo to the Moon and sits in every GPS receiver.

Linear algebra. Signal space (Chapter 10) is linear algebra: orthonormal bases, projection, Gram–Schmidt, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (which proved the matched filter optimal). Parity-check codes (Chapter 12) are linear algebra over the field GF(2); syndromes are matrix products.

Machine learning. A receiver is a classifier. The MAP rule of Chapter 10 is the Bayes-optimal classifier; a linear threshold detector is logistic regression; the correlator bank is a template-matching network. "End-to-end learned" transceivers replace the hand-designed blocks of this book with neural networks, but they are judged against the same benchmark curves ($Q$-function BER, Shannon capacity).

8. Nature already uses all of this

  • The genetic code is a 4-letter alphabet (2 bits per base), read three bases at a time (6 bits) to specify one of ~20 amino acids (4.4 bits): the 64 codons carry redundancy, and mutations in the third base often leave the protein unchanged: a built-in error-correcting code.
  • Hearing: the cochlea is a mechanical Fourier analyser: different places on the basilar membrane resonate at different frequencies (Békésy, Nobel 1961). MP3 exploits the ear's masking to discard what you cannot hear: source coding with a psychoacoustic model.
  • Neurons transmit information as spike trains well modelled by Poisson processes: noise-limited, rate-coded channels, analysed with the entropy and mutual information of Chapter 7.

Practice: connecting problems (original, exam-style)

Landauer's principle gives the minimum energy $k_BT\ln2$ to erase one bit. At $T=300$ K, with $k_B=1.38\times10^{-23}$ J/K, this equals ______ $\times10^{-21}$ J.

$1.38\times10^{-23}\times300\times0.693=2.87\times10^{-21}$ J.

At $T_0=290$ K the Shannon limit $E_b/N_0\ge\ln2$ with $N_0=kT_0$ implies a minimum energy per bit of ______ $\times10^{-21}$ J for reliable communication over an AWGN channel.

$E_b\ge kT_0\ln2=1.38\times10^{-23}\times290\times0.693=2.77\times10^{-21}$ J.

The free-space path loss (dB) over $d=10$ km at 900 MHz ($c=3\times10^8$ m/s) is ______.

$\lambda=0.333$ m; $20\log_{10}(4\pi d/\lambda)=20\log_{10}(3.77\times10^5)=111.5$ dB.

A Wi-Fi link at 2.4 GHz transmits 20 dBm with 0 dBi antennas over 100 m (free space; path loss 80 dB). The receiver has noise figure 6 dB and bandwidth 1 MHz (thermal noise floor $-114$ dBm before the noise figure). The SNR in dB is ______.

$P_r=20-80=-60$ dBm. Noise $=-114+6=-108$ dBm. SNR $=48$ dB (Shannon capacity $\approx16$ Mbit/s in 1 MHz).

A satellite approaching at 7.5 km/s transmits a 2 GHz carrier. The Doppler shift seen by a ground receiver directly in its path (kHz) is ______. ($c=3\times10^8$ m/s)

$\Delta f=\frac vcf_c=\frac{7.5\times10^3}{3\times10^8}\times2\times10^9=50$ kHz.

An ideal optical OOK receiver counts photons (Poisson). A "1" has mean $\bar n$ photons; a "0" has none. The error probability is $\frac12e^{-\bar n}$. The value of $\bar n$ needed for $P_e=10^{-9}$ is ______.

$e^{-\bar n}=2\times10^{-9}\Rightarrow\bar n=\ln(5\times10^8)=20.0$ photons per "1" (10 per bit on average).

Light of wavelength 500 nm passes through a single slit of width 0.1 mm. The angle of the first diffraction minimum, in milliradians, is ______.

The slit is a rect of width $a$; its transform is a sinc with first zero at $u=1/a$, i.e. $\sin\theta=\lambda/a=5\times10^{-3}$: 5 mrad.

A resonant circuit has $f_0=1$ MHz and $Q=50$. Its 3-dB bandwidth in kHz is ______.

$\Delta f=f_0/Q=20$ kHz.

A 20 MHz Wi-Fi OFDM system uses a 64-point FFT. The useful (FFT) symbol duration in microseconds, excluding the cyclic prefix, is ______.

Subcarrier spacing $=20\text{ MHz}/64=312.5$ kHz; useful symbol $=1/312.5\text{ kHz}=3.2\ \mu$s.
