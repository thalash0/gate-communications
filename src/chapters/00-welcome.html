Everything in this book is an answer to one question: how can I get a message from here to there, reliably, with limited power and limited bandwidth, when the universe adds noise to everything?

I want to start with a confession. When I first learned this subject, I memorised it: formulas for $\mu$, $\beta$, $Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})$, Carson's rule, Huffman's algorithm. I could pass the exam. And I understood nothing, because nobody had told me the story: that every one of those formulas is a small, clever answer to a problem somebody once found very annoying.

So this book does something different. In every chapter you will meet the person who got stuck, the picture that unstuck them, and only then the equation, and then you will see where the equation comes from: every result that matters is derived in a purple box, step by step, with its assumptions stated, so that you know when it stops being true. Almost every figure moves; most have a slider. Play with them. If a formula feels arbitrary, change the slider until it stops feeling arbitrary. That is how the formulas stay in your head on exam day.

The book has two ambitions that turn out to be the same ambition. It is a GATE guide: I downloaded the official question papers of 2014 and 2019–2026 and read every communication question; 95 of them are reproduced, solved and explained in these pages with their exam year, each chapter closes with a "GATE pattern" box saying what to expect, and every problem set climbs from concept checks to numericals to exam-style questions. And it is an engineering guide: the real-world numbers are not parked at the end but woven into the theory, in teal Engineering practice boxes (why Wi-Fi uses a 0.8 µs guard interval, how GPS recovers a signal 19 dB below the noise, what is inside a CD, a GSM call, an LTE cell). The last chapters turn the toolkit into design: link budgets, fading and diversity, MIMO, multiple access, cellular planning, and a chapter that sizes six real systems from scratch.

What GATE actually asks: the pattern from real papers

How this analysis was done

I downloaded the official GATE Electronics & Communication (EC) question papers from the organising institutes' websites: 2014 (all four sets), 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025 and 2026, together with the official answer keys for 2014 and 2021–2026 (the 2021 key is a scanned image, read by OCR). For 2015–2018, whose official files I could not find online, I used a chapter-wise solved-papers collection: I read only the Communication unit, to see which questions appeared and what they asked, and wrote my own solutions (its stated answers were used as a cross-check, which caught one disagreement: for 2019 Q.34 the collection prints (B), but the geometry gives $E=3$, i.e. (D); I trust the mathematics). The 2019–2021 papers are scanned images, so I read them with OCR and by eye; official keys for 2019 and 2020 were not available, so those answers are my own derivations (checked numerically). I then went through every question and kept the ones belonging to the Communications syllabus: random processes, analog, information theory, digital and coding. This is my classification; borderline questions (e.g. ADC SNR, which sits between Signals and Communications) are counted here.

Communication has been worth roughly 7 to 14 marks out of 100, and the weight has grown: papers of 2015–2018 (which had several sets, so I averaged per set) carried about 7–8 marks of Communication; the eight papers 2019–2026 carried about 11.5 on average (range 8–14). The split between 1-mark and 2-mark questions is roughly even. Here is where the marks went:

Marks of Communication questions by topic (my classification of the official papers). Use the "view" menu for totals, a single year, or a comparison with the older papers. Notice the stability: information theory, AM/FM, PCM/ADC and detection are present nearly every year; random-process questions are in 7 of 8; coding in 6 of 8 and rising.
Almost every year
  • Random process / noise through a filter (7 of 8 years): $S_Y=|H|^2S_X$, white noise through RC, validity of an autocorrelation/PSD, WSS or not.
  • Entropy / capacity / mutual information (7 of 8): max entropy, BSC/BEC/DMC capacity, Shannon–Hartley, Huffman.
  • ML/MAP detection with Gaussian noise (7 of 8): midpoint thresholds, $Q(d/2\sigma)$, 3-level symbols.
  • AM or FM numerical (8 of 8): $\mu$, sideband power, instantaneous frequency, Carson, pre-emphasis.
Most years
  • PCM / ADC / delta modulation (5 of 8): $6.02n+1.76$, bit rate $nf_s$, DM step.
  • Hamming / CRC / block codes (6 of 8 since 2019): syndrome, $d_{\min}$, $P_{\rm correct}$, CRC division.
  • Digital modulation rates and BER (3 of 8 + 2014): bits/symbol, bandwidth with raised cosine, $Q$-function BER.
Rare
  • Superheterodyne (once: 2021), ISI/Nyquist criterion (2024), noise figure (never in this sample, but cheap to learn).
Honest caveats

(1) Eight (or twelve) papers is a small sample; a topic being absent does not make it impossible. 2015–2018 numbers are approximate: each year had 2–3 sets with different questions, the collection may not list every question, and I divided pooled marks by the number of sets. (2) I could not retrieve official keys for 2019 and 2020: I solved those myself and checked numerically, but treat them as "derived". (When I compared my 2021 answers to the OCR'd key, one needed fixing: Q.52 asks for the SQNR as a ratio, not in dB. Always read the units the question wants.) (3) GATE sometimes updates the syllabus; the book covers the full GATE communication syllabus and goes well beyond it. (4) The questions here are reproduced from the public papers for study; for the real thing, always cross-check with the official files (links in Chapter 24).

Engineering course · one semester

Read the whole book in order, skipping nothing in the derivation boxes. Part I (3 weeks) is the toolkit; Part II (2) analog; Part III (2); Part IV (4) the heart of digital communication; Part V (3): spread spectrum and OFDM in week 1, wireless channel and cellular in week 2, case studies in week 3. Use the widgets as laboratory exercises: each has a question hidden in its caption. Work Set B and C of every chapter; use Chapter 23 as the template for a design project.

Reference reader · browse by need

Need a link budget? Chapter 21, then 23. Choosing a code? Chapters 17–18 (the table “Choosing a code” in Ch 17). Why does Wi-Fi look the way it does? Chapters 20 and 22. How does GPS work? Chapters 16, 19 and 23. What is a PLL? Chapter 7, then 16. Formulas in a hurry? Chapter 25.

Exam technique for this subject. (i) Draw the picture: constellation, spectrum, or block diagram: it usually gives the answer in two lines. (ii) Keep a mental table of $Q(1)=0.159$, $Q(2)=0.0228$, $Q(3)=0.00135$, $Q(4)=3.2\times10^{-5}$. (iii) In every NAT, check units (dB vs ratio; Hz vs rad/s; one-sided vs two-sided PSD $N_0$ vs $N_0/2$). (iv) Spend 2 minutes per 1-mark question, 3–4 per 2-mark; this is a "fast-formula" subject, so be quick on the easy ones and bank the time.

Conventions used throughout

  • $\sinc(x)=\sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)$; frequencies $f$ in Hz; $\omega=2\pi f$.
  • AWGN has two-sided PSD $N_0/2$; thermal noise $N_0=kT$; $\sigma^2=N_0/2$ per orthonormal dimension.
  • $Q(x)=\int_x^\infty\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^2/2}dt=\frac12\erfc(x/\sqrt2)$. $\log$ means $\log_2$ in information theory.
  • $E_b$ energy per bit, $E_s=E_b\log_2M$ energy per symbol, $\gamma=E_b/N_0$. dB values are $10\log_{10}$ of power ratios.
