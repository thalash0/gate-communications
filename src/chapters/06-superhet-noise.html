History · a trick from the trenches

1918, Paris. Major Edwin Armstrong of the US Signal Corps was trying to detect the faint ignition noise of enemy aircraft at very high frequencies, where the available vacuum tubes gave almost no gain. The problem: tubes amplify well at low frequencies, and a tuned circuit is sharp (selective) only if it is built for a single fixed frequency. His solution was to move the incoming signal to a fixed lower "intermediate" frequency by mixing it with a locally generated oscillation, then amplify and filter at that fixed frequency with a stack of identical, carefully-tuned stages. He called it the "supersonic heterodyne" ("beats at a frequency above hearing"), now superheterodyne. (A French engineer, Lucien Lévy, filed a very similar patent at nearly the same time; the priority fight ran for a decade.) Nearly every radio built since, from the 1930s tube sets to the chip in your phone, uses some variant of this scheme.

The problem the superhet solves

An early "tuned-radio-frequency" (TRF) receiver amplified the antenna signal directly with several tunable stages. Two difficulties: (i) getting a sharp filter whose centre you can slide from 540 kHz to 1600 kHz is very hard (the ratio of bandwidth to centre frequency, $Q=f_0/B$, changes by 3× as you tune), and (ii) every stage of a tunable amplifier had to be adjusted together, and could oscillate. The superhet's idea: do the hard filtering once, at a fixed frequency.

The superheterodyne receiver. Only the RF preselector and the local oscillator (LO) are tuned (by a single ganged knob). Almost all the gain and the sharp channel-selecting filter live in the fixed IF strip. AGC (automatic gain control) feeds the detected level back to hold the output steady.

The mixer arithmetic

A mixer multiplies the incoming signal by the LO. By the product-of-cosines identity, $\cos(2\pi f_st)\cos(2\pi f_{LO}t)=\tfrac12[\cos2\pi(f_{LO}-f_s)t+\cos2\pi(f_{LO}+f_s)t]$; the IF filter keeps the difference: $$f_{IF}=|f_{LO}-f_s|.$$ Broadcast receivers use high-side injection, $f_{LO}=f_s+f_{IF}$. The reason is practical (below).

The image frequency: the superhet's one weakness

Mixing treats $f_{LO}+f_{IF}$ and $f_{LO}-f_{IF}$ identically: both are exactly $f_{IF}$ away from the LO. With high-side injection the wanted station sits at $f_s=f_{LO}-f_{IF}$, and a second, unwanted station at $$\boxed{\,f_{im}=f_s+2f_{IF}\,}$$ (the "image", the mirror of $f_s$ about $f_{LO}$) also lands precisely on the IF and cannot be separated afterwards. The only defence is to remove it before the mixer, with the RF preselector. For a single tuned circuit of quality factor $Q$ the image rejection is $$\alpha=\sqrt{1+Q^2\rho^2},\qquad\rho=\frac{f_{im}}{f_s}-\frac{f_s}{f_{im}}.$$ This explains why IF is chosen high enough: a larger $f_{IF}$ pushes the image further away, making the preselector's job easy. But a larger IF makes the IF filter less selective and harder to build. For AM broadcast the compromise is 455 kHz, for FM broadcast 10.7 MHz, for TV about 38.9 MHz.

Worked examples

GATE 2021 Q.50. Receiver tuned to 600 kHz, LO at 1000 kHz. Then $f_{IF}=1000-600=400$ kHz and $f_{im}=f_{LO}+f_{IF}=1000+400=\mathbf{1400}$ kHz (or $f_s+2f_{IF}=600+800$).

Why high-side injection? AM band 540–1600 kHz, IF 455 kHz. High-side: LO tunes 995–2055 kHz, a frequency ratio of 2.07, so the oscillator's tuning capacitor must change by $(2.07)^2=\mathbf{4.27}\!:\!1$, easy. Low-side: LO tunes 85–1145 kHz, ratio 13.5, capacitance ratio 181:1, which is impossible.

Image rejection. $f_s=1$ MHz, $f_{IF}=455$ kHz, $Q=50$: $f_{im}=1.91$ MHz, $\rho=1.91-0.524=1.386$, $\alpha=\sqrt{1+2500\cdot1.92}=69.3$ ($36.8$ dB).

Noise in analog receivers: what SNR do we get out?

We now ask the key performance question of analog communication: with a given transmitted power and a channel adding white noise of density $N_0/2$, how good is the demodulated signal? To compare schemes fairly we use the benchmark of sending the message directly (baseband) with the same power $P_T$ over a channel of the same bandwidth $W$: $$\gamma\;\triangleq\;\frac{P_T}{N_0W}\quad(\text{"channel SNR" or baseband SNR}),\qquad\text{figure of merit }=\frac{\mathrm{SNR}_o}{\gamma}.$$ The receiver model is always the same: a band-pass filter of width $B_T$ around $f_c$ (the noise there is narrowband noise $n(t)=n_c\cos-n_s\sin$ with power $N_0B_T$), then the demodulator, then a low-pass filter of width $W$.

Coherent systems: DSB-SC and SSB

DSB-SC: $B_T=2W$, input noise power $N_0\cdot2W$. Multiply by $\cos2\pi f_ct$, low-pass: output $=\frac12[A_cm(t)+n_c(t)]$. Signal power $\frac{A_c^2}4P_m$; noise power $\frac14\cdot2N_0W=\frac{N_0W}2$. Since $P_T=\frac{A_c^2P_m}2$: $\ \mathrm{SNR}_o=\dfrac{A_c^2P_m}{2N_0W}=\gamma$. The quadrature noise $n_s$ is rejected by the synchronous detector; the other half of the input noise is thrown away. SSB gives the same $\mathrm{SNR}_o=\gamma$ (half the signal power but half the noise bandwidth). So DSB-SC and SSB are as good as baseband transmission: figure of merit 1 (0 dB). Nothing can beat this without spending more bandwidth.

AM with an envelope detector

With $\mu\le1$ and a strong carrier, the envelope is $\approx A_c[1+\mu m]+n_c(t)$ (the quadrature noise only adds a second-order term). After removing DC, signal $=A_c^2\mu^2P_m$ and noise $=n_c$ power $=2N_0W$, but now $P_T=\frac{A_c^2}2(1+\mu^2P_m)$ includes the wasted carrier. So $$\mathrm{SNR}_o=\frac{\mu^2P_m}{1+\mu^2P_m}\,\gamma=\eta\,\gamma\ \le\ \tfrac13\gamma .$$ For a sinusoid with $\mu=1$: FOM $=\frac13$ ($-4.8$ dB); at $\mu=0.5$: $\frac{1/8}{9/8}=\frac19$ ($-9.5$ dB). The AM FOM is just the transmission efficiency $\eta$ of Chapter 4: the carrier carries no information, but costs power.

Threshold effect (AM and FM)

The formulas above assume the carrier is much stronger than the noise. Once the noise dominates, an envelope detector output is the envelope of noise, with the message (a small ripple on top) swamped: the output SNR collapses non-linearly. A coherent detector for AM does not show a threshold. FM has a much more dramatic threshold: see below.

FM: bandwidth bought as SNR

For FM with a strong carrier, the noise causes a phase error $\varphi_n\approx n_s(t)/A_c$ (the component of noise in quadrature with the carrier, relative to its amplitude). The discriminator outputs the frequency $\frac1{2\pi}\dot\varphi_n$: it differentiates the phase noise, so the output noise PSD is $$S_{n_o}(f)=\frac{N_0}{A_c^2}f^2\qquad(|f|\le W),$$ parabolic, quiet near 0 and strongest at the band edge. Integrating, noise power $=\frac{2N_0W^3}{3A_c^2}$. The demodulated signal power is $k_f^2P_m$. Therefore $$\mathrm{SNR}_o=\frac{3A_c^2k_f^2P_m}{2N_0W^3}=3\,\frac{k_f^2P_m}{W^2}\,\gamma,\qquad\gamma=\frac{A_c^2/2}{N_0W}.$$ With the deviation ratio $D=\frac{k_fm_p}{W}$ ($m_p$ the peak of $m$): $\ \mathrm{SNR}_o=3D^2\dfrac{P_m}{m_p^2}\gamma$. For a sinusoidal message $P_m/m_p^2=\frac12$, and $$\boxed{\ \mathrm{SNR}_o=\tfrac32\beta^2\gamma\ \ (\text{FM tone}),\qquad \mathrm{SNR}_o=k_p^2P_m\gamma\ \ (\text{PM}).\ }$$ Look at what this says: the output SNR grows with the square of $\beta$, and by Carson's rule $B_T\approx2W(\beta+1)$ the bandwidth grows only linearly. FM at $\beta=5$ (broadcast) beats DSB-SC/SSB by $\frac32\cdot25=37.5$ times, which is 15.7 dB, and beats AM ($\mu=1$) by 20.5 dB, at the price of 6× the bandwidth. This is Armstrong's insight and the first demonstration of a theme that returns in Chapter 7: bandwidth can be traded for SNR (Shannon later proved exactly how far).

And the other famous FM property: the capture effect. When two FM stations share a frequency, the receiver locks onto the stronger one and suppresses the other almost completely (not so in AM, where you hear both, plus a heterodyne whistle). Both effects come from the same cause: a limiter and a discriminator respond only to the phase of the dominant phasor.

Pre-emphasis and de-emphasis, quantitatively

Because the discriminator noise PSD $\propto f^2$ and music/speech PSD falls with $f$, boosting the high frequencies before transmission with $H_{pe}$ and attenuating them back with $H_{de}=1/H_{pe}$ at the receiver reduces output noise at no cost to the message. With $f_0=\frac1{2\pi RC}$ the improvement in output SNR is $$I=\frac{\int_{-W}^{W}f^2\,df}{\int_{-W}^{W}f^2|H_{de}(f)|^2df}=\frac{(W/f_0)^3}{3\,[\,W/f_0-\arctan(W/f_0)\,]}.$$ For broadcast audio $W=15$ kHz and 75 μs ($f_0=2.12$ kHz): $I=20.9$ ($13.2$ dB). For the 50 μs standard used in India and Europe ($f_0=3.18$ kHz): $I=10.4$ ($10.2$ dB).

Real world · a link in numbers

An FM station delivers $-80$ dBm to a receiver with noise figure 5 dB and a 200 kHz channel. Noise floor: $-174+10\log(200\,\mathrm{k})+5=-116$ dBm. Input SNR $=36$ dB, comfortably above the FM threshold ($\approx13$–$20$ dB), so the formulas above hold and audio SNR is $36+15.7\approx52$ dB minus de-emphasis effects: studio-quality. If the signal fades to $-100$ dBm the SNR falls to 16 dB, near threshold: the audio suddenly turns into a rough hiss with clicks. That abrupt cliff is why a car FM radio "drops out" rather than fading gracefully.

Practice

Consider a superheterodyne receiver tuned to 600 kHz. If the local oscillator feeds a 1000 kHz signal to the mixer, the image frequency (integer) is ______ kHz.

$f_{IF}=400$ kHz, image $=f_{LO}+f_{IF}=1400$ kHz.

An AM broadcast superhet with $f_{IF}=455$ kHz (high-side injection) is tuned to 1000 kHz. The image frequency in kHz is ______.

$f_s+2f_{IF}=1000+910=1910$ kHz.

An AM receiver covers 540–1600 kHz with $f_{IF}=455$ kHz and high-side injection. The tuning capacitor of the local oscillator must have a maximum-to-minimum capacitance ratio of about
  1. 2.07
  2. 4.27
  3. 8.5
  4. 181

LO range 995–2055 kHz; $f\propto1/\sqrt{LC}$ so $C_{\max}/C_{\min}=(2055/995)^2=4.27$.

A single-tuned preselector has $Q=50$. The receiver is tuned to 1 MHz with $f_{IF}=455$ kHz. The image rejection in dB is ______.

$f_{im}=1.91$ MHz, $\rho=1.386$, $\alpha=\sqrt{1+(50\cdot1.386)^2}=69.3\to36.8$ dB.

A single-tone AM system with $\mu=0.5$ is demodulated by an envelope detector at high SNR. Its output SNR relative to the baseband benchmark $\gamma=P_T/(N_0W)$, in dB, is ______.

$\eta=\frac{\mu^2/2}{1+\mu^2/2}=\frac{0.125}{1.125}=\frac19\to-9.54$ dB.

Single-tone FM with $\beta=5$ is demodulated above threshold. The ratio of output SNR to $\gamma$ in dB is ______.

$\frac32\beta^2=37.5\to10\log_{10}37.5=15.74$ dB.

When the input SNR of an FM receiver falls below the threshold, the output SNR
  1. continues to fall at 1 dB per dB
  2. improves because of the limiter
  3. falls much faster than 1 dB per dB because of click noise
  4. becomes equal to the baseband SNR

Below threshold the noise phasor occasionally encircles the carrier phasor, each time producing a $2\pi$ phase jump (a click); output SNR collapses rapidly.

Which of the following have a figure of merit (output SNR / $P_T/N_0W$) equal to 1?
  1. SSB-SC with coherent detection
  2. AM ($\mu=1$, tone) with envelope detection
  3. DSB-SC with coherent detection
  4. FM with $\beta=1$

SSB and DSB-SC both give 1. AM gives $\frac13$. FM with $\beta=1$ gives $\frac32$.

A superheterodyne receiver (high-side injection, $f_{LO}=f_s+f_{IF}$) tunes the range 58–68 MHz. The minimum $f_{IF}$ (MHz) such that every image frequency falls outside the 58–68 MHz band is ______.

Image $=f_s+2f_{IF}$; the lowest tuned frequency $58$ MHz gives the lowest image $58+2f_{IF}$, which must exceed 68 MHz: $f_{IF}>5$ MHz. Minimum $=\mathbf5$ MHz. (A larger IF buys image rejection but costs IF selectivity.)

A superheterodyne receiver has $f_{IF}=15$ MHz and $f_{LO}=3.5$ GHz. The received signal frequency is greater than the LO frequency. The image frequency (MHz) is ______.

Now $f_s=f_{LO}+f_{IF}=3515$ MHz (a high-side received signal: low-side injection). The image is on the other side of the LO: $f_{im}=f_{LO}-f_{IF}=f_s-2f_{IF}=\mathbf{3485}$ MHz. General rule: the image is the mirror of the wanted signal about the LO.
