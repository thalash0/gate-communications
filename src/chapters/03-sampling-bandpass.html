History · Nyquist, Kotelnikov, Shannon and a lot of people in between

Harry Nyquist (Bell Labs, 1928) showed that a telegraph channel of bandwidth $B$ can carry at most $2B$ distinct pulses per second. Vladimir Kotelnikov proved the sampling theorem in 1933 in the Soviet Union, and Claude Shannon gave it its modern form in 1949 (E. T. Whittaker had a version in 1915). In 1937 Alec Reeves, working at the International Telephone and Telegraph laboratories in Paris, conceived pulse-code modulation, sampling speech and sending the samples as numbers; he was thirty years too early, since nobody could build the electronics. Not until 1962 did Bell’s T1 system put 24 digitised telephone calls on one pair of copper wires, and with that the analog world began to retreat.

Real world · where the sampling theorem lives

An audio CD stores $44\,100$ samples per second per channel because humans hear up to $\approx20$ kHz, and $44.1>2\times20$ leaves room for a realisable anti-alias filter. Telephone speech (0.3–3.4 kHz) is sampled at $8$ kHz. A Wi-Fi 20 MHz channel is sampled at $20$–$40$ Msps; a cellular base-station receiver digitises an IF band at hundreds of Msps; a software-defined radio samples an entire band of the radio spectrum and then chooses the station in software. All of them are the same theorem.

Sampling: multiplying by a comb

The spectrum of a sampled signal

To sample a signal every $T_s$ seconds is, in the idealised mathematical model, to multiply it by the impulse train $\sum_n\delta(t-nT_s)$: $$x_s(t)=x(t)\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-nT_s)=\sum_nx(nT_s)\,\delta(t-nT_s).$$ The comb is periodic, so by Chapter 2 it has a Fourier series with coefficients $c_k=\frac1{T_s}$ for all $k$: $\sum_n\delta(t-nT_s)=\frac1{T_s}\sum_ke^{j2\pi kf_st}$ with $f_s=1/T_s$.

Derivation · replicas of the spectrum

Multiply $x(t)$ by the series term by term and use the frequency-shift property ($x(t)e^{j2\pi kf_st}\leftrightarrow X(f-kf_s)$): $$\boxed{\,X_s(f)=\frac1{T_s}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}X(f-kf_s)=f_s\sum_{k}X(f-kf_s)\,}$$ Sampling creates an infinite sequence of copies of the original spectrum, centred at every multiple of $f_s$, each scaled by $f_s$. Everything about sampling follows from this picture.

  • If $X(f)=0$ for $|f|>W$ and $f_s\ge2W$, the copies do not overlap. An ideal low-pass filter of gain $T_s$ and cut-off between $W$ and $f_s-W$ isolates the $k=0$ copy and returns $x(t)$ exactly. That is the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem; $f_s=2W$ is the Nyquist rate and $f_s/2$ the Nyquist (folding) frequency. (Strictly $f_s>2W$ when $X$ contains an impulse at $\pm W$, e.g. $\sin2\pi Wt$ sampled at its zero crossings gives all zeros.)
  • If $f_s<2W$ the tails of neighbouring copies overlap and add: aliasing. The overlap cannot be undone afterwards: information is lost. A tone at $f$ becomes indistinguishable from a tone at $|f-nf_s|$, with $n$ chosen to land in $[0,f_s/2]$.

Practical samplers

Flat-top (sample-and-hold) sampling. Real circuits hold each sample for a time $\tau$ (so the capacitor can be charged and the ADC can convert). This is equivalent to impulse sampling followed by a filter with impulse response $\rect\big((t-\tau/2)/\tau\big)$, i.e. frequency response $H_{\rm ap}(f)=\tau\sinc(f\tau)e^{-j\pi f\tau}$. The spectrum of the baseband copy is therefore multiplied by $\sinc(f\tau)$: an aperture effect (droop) that attenuates the high frequencies. For a zero-order-hold DAC the hold time equals the sample period, $\tau=T_s$, and at the band edge $f=f_s/2$ the droop is $\sinc(\tfrac12)=\tfrac2\pi=0.637=-3.9$ dB. An equaliser with response $1/\sinc(f/f_s)$ (analog, or an FIR filter before the DAC) cancels it.

Derivation · how clock jitter limits the SNR of an ADC

Sample a sinusoid $A\sin2\pi ft$ at instants that are late or early by a random $\epsilon$ (rms $\sigma_t$). The sampling error is $\approx\frac{d}{dt}(A\sin2\pi ft)\cdot\epsilon=2\pi fA\cos(2\pi ft)\,\epsilon$, with mean-square value $\frac12(2\pi fA\sigma_t)^2$, against a signal power $\frac12A^2$: $$\mathrm{SNR}_{\rm jitter}=\frac1{(2\pi f\sigma_t)^2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathrm{SNR}_{\rm dB}=-20\log_{10}(2\pi f\sigma_t).$$ The error grows with signal frequency and amplitude, because fast-moving signals are most sensitive to timing. For $\sigma_t=1$ ps, a 100 MHz input gives $64$ dB (about 10.4 bits); $\sigma_t=10$ ps gives only $44$ dB (7 bits). High-speed ADCs and IF-sampling radios live and die by clock quality.

Engineering practice · anti-alias and oversampling

Any energy above $f_s/2$ at the ADC input aliases onto the band of interest, including noise and interference you did not know was there. So an anti-alias filter always precedes an ADC. Because a steep analog filter is expensive (Chapter 1: the $n=16$ Butterworth example), designers instead oversample by a factor $L=f_s/2W$ so that a gentle filter suffices, then filter and decimate digitally. A bonus: quantisation noise (Chapter 12) of fixed total power is spread over $f_s/2$, and only the fraction $1/L$ falls in the signal band, an SNR gain of $10\log_{10}L$ dB (3 dB per doubling). Sigma–delta converters push this to the extreme: a 1-bit quantiser sampled at a few MHz yields a 16–24-bit audio ADC.

Sampling a bandpass signal: undersampling on purpose

The condition $f_s\ge2f_{\max}$ is sufficient but wasteful for a signal that occupies only a band $f_L\le|f|\le f_H$ of width $B=f_H-f_L$. The spectrum then consists of two islands, at $[f_L,f_H]$ and $[-f_H,-f_L]$, and sampling places copies of both islands at every multiple of $f_s$. The copies can interleave without overlap, in which case the signal can be recovered with $f_s$ as small as $2B$.

Derivation · allowed sampling rates for a bandpass signal

Divide the frequency axis into Nyquist zones of width $f_s/2$: zone $m$ is $[m\tfrac{f_s}2,(m+1)\tfrac{f_s}2]$, $m=0,1,2,\dots$. Sampling folds every zone onto $[0,f_s/2]$ (zone 0 straight, zone 1 mirrored, zone 2 straight, …) and the negative-frequency mirror of each positive zone onto $[f_s/2,f_s]$. A band lying inside one zone therefore folds without touching its own mirror image, while a band that straddles a multiple of $f_s/2$ is folded over itself. So the signal survives sampling iff for some integer $m\ge0$ $$m\frac{f_s}2\le f_L\quad\text{and}\quad f_H\le(m+1)\frac{f_s}2.$$ Write $k=m+1$ and rearrange: $$\boxed{\ \frac{2f_H}{k}\ \le\ f_s\ \le\ \frac{2f_L}{k-1},\qquad k=1,2,\dots,\Big\lfloor\frac{f_H}{B}\Big\rfloor\ }$$ (for $k=1$ the right-hand side is infinite: ordinary low-pass sampling, $f_s\ge2f_H$). The upper limit on $k$ comes from requiring the window to be non-empty, which forces $f_s\ge2B$. The largest $k$ gives the lowest rate; exactly $f_s=2B$ is reachable only if $f_H/B$ is an integer.

Choose the band edges of a bandpass signal (blue: positive-frequency island, orange: its negative-frequency mirror) and the sampling rate. The chart shows where all copies fall once folded into the interval $[0,f_s)$. Whenever blue and orange overlap (red), the band is aliased and cannot be recovered. Try the band 30–38 MHz: you will find allowed windows $19$–$20$, $25.3$–$30$, $38$–$60$ and $\ge76$ MHz. Notice how narrow the lowest window is.
Worked example · the minimum sampling rate for a 30–38 MHz IF signal

$f_L=30$, $f_H=38$, $B=8$ MHz. $\lfloor f_H/B\rfloor=\lfloor4.75\rfloor=4$. For $k=4$: $\frac{2\cdot38}4=19\le f_s\le\frac{2\cdot30}3=20$ MHz. So $f_s=19$ MHz suffices, compared with $76$ MHz from the low-pass theorem. Check by folding: $30\bmod19=11$, $38\bmod19=0$ (i.e. $19$), so the positive island lands on $[11,19]$ and the negative on $[0,8]$: disjoint ✓. Practical designs avoid the window edges (guard bands, finite filter slopes), so they pick $f_s\approx19.5$ MHz.

Engineering practice · IF sampling and the “$f_s=4f_c$” trick

A software-defined radio samples a band at its intermediate frequency and does the down-conversion numerically. The cheapest scheme sets $f_s=4f_{\rm IF}$: then $\cos(2\pi f_{\rm IF}nT_s)=\cos(\pi n/2)=1,0,-1,0,\dots$ and $\sin(\pi n/2)=0,1,0,-1,\dots$, so the multiplications by cosine and sine reduce to choosing and sign-flipping alternate samples: no multiplier at all. The in-phase samples are $x[0],-x[2],x[4],\dots$ and the quadrature samples $-x[1],x[3],-x[5],\dots$. A subsequent low-pass/decimate stage rejects the $2f_{\rm IF}$ images (the demodulation of the next section).

The Hilbert transform and the analytic signal

The Hilbert transform $\hat x(t)$ of a signal is what you get when you delay every frequency component by a quarter of a cycle ($-90^\circ$) without changing its amplitude. In the frequency domain: $$\hat X(f)=-j\,\sgn(f)\,X(f),\qquad H(f)=-j\sgn(f)=\begin{cases}-j,&f>0\\+j,&f<0\end{cases}$$ (a phase of $-90^\circ$ at positive frequencies and $+90^\circ$ at negative frequencies, which keeps the output real).

Derivation · its impulse response is $1/\pi t$

Chapter 2 gave $\sgn(t)\leftrightarrow\frac1{j\pi f}$. By duality ($X(t)\leftrightarrow x(-f)$), $\frac1{j\pi t}\leftrightarrow\sgn(-f)=-\sgn(f)$. Multiply by $j$: $\frac1{\pi t}\leftrightarrow-j\,\sgn(f)$. Hence $$\hat x(t)=x(t)*\frac1{\pi t}=\frac1\pi\,\mathrm{P.V.}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{x(\tau)}{t-\tau}\,d\tau.$$ The impulse response is non-causal and decays slowly ($1/t$), so an ideal Hilbert transformer cannot be built exactly; FIR approximations with an odd symmetric impulse response are used in practice (a type III linear-phase filter, with group delay of half its length).

Immediate consequences, all easy to verify with $H(f)=-j\sgn f$:

  • $\widehat{\cos2\pi f_0t}=\sin2\pi f_0t$ and $\widehat{\sin2\pi f_0t}=-\cos2\pi f_0t$ (for $f_0>0$).
  • Applying it twice gives $-1$: $\hat{\hat x}=-x$ (since $(-j\sgn f)^2=-1$).
  • $|H|=1$ except at $f=0$: it preserves energy, $\int\hat x^2=\int x^2$; and $x$ is orthogonal to $\hat x$: $\int x\hat x\,dt=\int j\sgn(f)|X(f)|^2df=0$ (odd integrand).

The analytic signal: a real signal without its redundant negative half

A real signal wastes half its spectrum on negative frequencies, which are mirror images of the positive ones. Build the analytic signal (pre-envelope) $$x_+(t)=x(t)+j\hat x(t)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X_+(f)=X(f)\,[1+\sgn f]=2X(f)\,u(f).$$ The negative frequencies cancel and the positive ones double. For $x=\cos2\pi f_0t$, $x_+=\cos+j\sin=e^{j2\pi f_0t}$: a point spinning on a circle, i.e. a helix when drawn against time. The real signal is the helix’s shadow on the real axis, $\hat x$ its shadow on the imaginary axis. Write $x_+(t)=a(t)e^{j\theta(t)}$ in polar form: $$\text{envelope }a(t)=|x_+(t)|=\sqrt{x^2+\hat x^2},\quad\text{phase }\theta(t)=\arg x_+,\quad\text{instantaneous frequency }f_i(t)=\frac1{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{dt}.$$ If the amplitude of the cosine wobbles (AM) the helix’s radius wobbles; if its frequency wobbles (FM) the helix tightens and loosens. These are definitions that make “instantaneous amplitude and frequency” precise for any real signal, and they are the soul of the next chapters.

The analytic signal $x_+(t)$ as a corkscrew in (Re, Im, time). Its shadow on the red–time wall is $x(t)$; its shadow on the blue–time wall is $\hat x(t)$. Choose a tone, an AM tone, or an FM tone, and drag to rotate.

Single-sideband (SSB) modulation is produced by keeping only $X_+(f)$ (or its conjugate) and shifting it: $s_{\rm SSB}(t)=m(t)\cos2\pi f_ct\mp\hat m(t)\sin2\pi f_ct$. Chapter 6 proves it.

Bandpass signals and the complex envelope

A transmitted signal lives near a carrier frequency $f_c$: it is a bandpass (narrowband) signal if its bandwidth is much smaller than $f_c$. Carrier cycles are fast and carry no information by themselves; what matters is how the amplitude and phase change slowly relative to the carrier. The complex envelope isolates exactly that.

Derivation · from analytic signal to I/Q
  1. Take the bandpass real signal $x(t)$, spectrum around $\pm f_c$. Form $x_+(t)$: spectrum $2X(f)u(f)$ around $+f_c$ only.
  2. Shift it to baseband: $\tilde x(t)=x_+(t)\,e^{-j2\pi f_ct}$, so $\tilde X(f)=X_+(f+f_c)=2X(f+f_c)u(f+f_c)$. This is a low-pass complex signal, the complex envelope.
  3. Undo the shift and keep the real part: $\boxed{x(t)=\mathrm{Re}\{\tilde x(t)\,e^{j2\pi f_ct}\}}$.
  4. Write $\tilde x=x_I+jx_Q=a(t)e^{j\phi(t)}$. Expanding: $x(t)=x_I\cos2\pi f_ct-x_Q\sin2\pi f_ct=a(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct+\phi(t))$. Explicitly, from $x_+=x+j\hat x$: $$x_I=x\cos2\pi f_ct+\hat x\sin2\pi f_ct,\qquad x_Q=\hat x\cos2\pi f_ct-x\sin2\pi f_ct.$$

$x_I$ (“in-phase”) multiplies the cosine and $x_Q$ (“quadrature”) the sine: the signal is a sum of two amplitude-modulated carriers in phase quadrature, which are orthogonal over a carrier period. That is why two independent messages can share one carrier (QAM, Chapters 6 and 15). The complex envelope is the baseband “movie”; the carrier is just the projector’s spinning light bulb.

  • AM: $\phi=0$, $a(t)=A_c[1+\mu m(t)]$: the tip moves along a radial line.
  • PM/FM: $a$ constant, $\phi(t)$ varies: the tip moves on a circle.
  • QPSK/QAM: the tip jumps between a few points of the plane: the “constellation”.
Left: the passband waveform. Right: the tip of $\tilde x(t)$ in the I–Q plane (with its trail). Increasing $\mu$ makes the tip stretch radially; increasing $\beta$ makes it swing in angle. Same carrier, two ways to hide information.

The I/Q demodulator

Derivation · recovering $x_I$ and $x_Q$ with two multipliers and two filters

Multiply $x(t)=x_I\cos\omega_ct-x_Q\sin\omega_ct$ by $2\cos\omega_ct$ and by $-2\sin\omega_ct$ and use the product-to-sum identities: $$2x\cos\omega_ct=x_I(1+\cos2\omega_ct)-x_Q\sin2\omega_ct\ \xrightarrow{\rm LPF}\ x_I,$$ $$-2x\sin\omega_ct=-x_I\sin2\omega_ct+x_Q(1-\cos2\omega_ct)\ \xrightarrow{\rm LPF}\ x_Q.$$ The terms at $2f_c$ are removed by the low-pass filters (bandwidth $\ge$ the message bandwidth, $\ll2f_c$). This pair of mixers and filters is the front end of every modern receiver: the output is the complex baseband signal $x_I+jx_Q$, ready for the ADC. If the local oscillator has a phase error $\varphi$, the outputs are rotated: $\tilde x\to\tilde xe^{-j\varphi}$ (a rotation of the constellation); a frequency error $\Delta f$ makes the constellation spin at $\Delta f$ turns per second (Chapter 16).

Power and energy of bandpass signals

By Parseval, $\int|x_+|^2dt=\int4|X|^2u(f)\,df=2E_x$ (only the positive half of the symmetric $|X|^2$ survives, doubled in amplitude, quadrupled in power), and $|\tilde x|=|x_+|$, so $$E_x=\tfrac12\int|\tilde x(t)|^2dt,\qquad P_x=\tfrac12\,\overline{|\tilde x(t)|^2}=\tfrac12\overline{x_I^2+x_Q^2}.$$ A carrier $A\cos2\pi f_ct$ has complex envelope $A$ and power $A^2/2$. Forget the $\tfrac12$ and every power in the next chapters is off by 2.

Bandpass systems have equivalent baseband models

Derivation · a bandpass filter acting on a bandpass signal

Let $y=x*h$ with real bandpass $h$. In the frequency domain $Y=HX$, and for positive frequencies $Y_+(f)=2Y(f)u(f)=H(f)\cdot2X(f)u(f)=H(f)X_+(f)$. Shift by $f_c$: $\tilde Y(f)=H(f+f_c)\tilde X(f)$ for $f>-f_c$. Defining the equivalent low-pass frequency response $H_l(f)=H(f+f_c)\,u(f+f_c)$, $$\tilde Y(f)=H_l(f)\,\tilde X(f).$$ The whole bandpass system acts on the complex envelope as an ordinary (complex) low-pass filter with the same shape, moved to $f=0$. Real bandpass simulation therefore needs only a sample rate set by the message bandwidth, not by the carrier frequency: simulating a 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi link takes tens of MHz of sample rate rather than billions. If $H_l$ is not conjugate-symmetric (i.e. $|H|$ not symmetric about $f_c$, as with an SSB filter or a multipath channel), $h_l$ is complex and mixes $x_I$ and $x_Q$: I/Q crosstalk. If $H_l(f)=|H_l|e^{-j2\pi ft_g}$ (linear phase) then $\tilde y(t)=|H_l|\tilde x(t-t_g)$: the envelope is simply delayed, which is exactly the group-delay result of Chapter 1.

Engineering practice · direct conversion and I/Q imbalance

“Zero-IF” (direct-conversion) receivers are the standard in phones: one local oscillator at $f_c$ drives two mixers in quadrature, two low-pass filters and two ADCs deliver $x_I$ and $x_Q$. Compared with a superhet (Chapter 8) there are no image filters or IF stages, so the chip is tiny. The price is I/Q imbalance: if the two arms have gain mismatch $\varepsilon$ and phase error $\varphi$, the output contains the desired $\tilde x$ plus an unwanted mirror-image copy of strength $\approx\frac12\sqrt{\varepsilon^2+\varphi^2}$; a $1^\circ$ phase error alone gives an image at $20\log(\tfrac12\cdot0.0175)=-41$ dBc, which is why high-order QAM (Chapter 15) demands calibration. The same maths explains “DC offset” and LO leakage in these receivers.

A signal $m(t)$ is bandlimited to 4 kHz. The minimum sampling rate that allows $m^2(t)$ to be recovered from its samples is
  1. 4 kHz
  2. 8 kHz
  3. 16 kHz
  4. 32 kHz

Squaring doubles the bandwidth (convolution of $M$ with itself: support $[-8,8]$ kHz), so Nyquist rate $=16$ kHz.

A bandpass signal occupies 30 MHz to 38 MHz. The minimum sampling rate (MHz) that permits perfect reconstruction is ______.

$k_{\max}=\lfloor38/8\rfloor=4$: $2f_H/k=19\le f_s\le2f_L/(k-1)=20$. Minimum: 19 MHz.

An ADC samples a 100 MHz sine wave with an rms clock jitter of 10 ps. The jitter-limited SNR in dB is ______.

  3. band-pass signal
  4. notch (band-stop) signal

  • Sampling $=$ multiplication by a comb ⇒ $X_s(f)=f_s\sum_kX(f-kf_s)$: spectral copies at every $kf_s$. No overlap iff $f_s>2W$. Aliases at $|f-nf_s|$. Product widens (bandwidths add); $m^2$ needs $4W$.
  • Reconstruction: ideal LPF = sinc interpolation, $x(t)=\sum x(nT_s)\sinc((t-nT_s)/T_s)$. Hold circuits droop as $\sinc(f/f_s)$; jitter limits SNR to $-20\log(2\pi f\sigma_t)$; oversampling relaxes the anti-alias filter and gains $10\log L$ dB.
  • Bandpass sampling: $\frac{2f_H}k\le f_s\le\frac{2f_L}{k-1}$, $k\le\lfloor f_H/B\rfloor$; minimum approaches $2B$.
  • Hilbert: $H=-j\sgn f$, $h=1/\pi t$, energy-preserving, $\hat{\hat x}=-x$. Analytic signal $x_+=x+j\hat x$, spectrum $2Xu(f)$; envelope $\sqrt{x^2+\hat x^2}$; $f_i=\frac1{2\pi}\dot\theta$.
  • Bandpass: $x=\mathrm{Re}\{\tilde xe^{j2\pi f_ct}\}=x_I\cos-x_Q\sin$; recover with $2\cos$ and $-2\sin$ mixers + LPFs; power $\frac12\overline{|\tilde x|^2}$; bandpass systems act as $H(f+f_c)$ on $\tilde x$.
