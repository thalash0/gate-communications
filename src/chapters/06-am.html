History · Christmas Eve, 1906

Guglielmo Marconi had sent Morse dots and dashes across the Atlantic in 1901, but a spark transmitter can only be on or off. Reginald Fessenden, a Canadian physicist, wanted the human voice. His idea: generate a pure, continuous high-frequency wave and let the microphone vary its amplitude. On Christmas Eve 1906, from a mast at Brant Rock, Massachusetts, he broadcast a violin solo (“O Holy Night”), a Bible reading and a greeting. Radio operators on United Fruit Company ships, accustomed to hearing only beeps in their headphones, heard music and speech from the sky.

The enabling technology arrived the same year: Lee de Forest’s audion triode (1906), the first device that could amplify. By 1920, KDKA in Pittsburgh was running scheduled broadcasts, and by the 1930s AM radio was in most homes. In 1915 John Renshaw Carson, an AT&T engineer, showed mathematically that single-sideband transmission needs only half the bandwidth (the principle behind carrier telephony, in service on long-distance lines from 1918); in 1922 he also concluded that frequency modulation offers no bandwidth advantage over AM, a conclusion Armstrong would overturn in 1933 by using wide deviation to buy noise immunity (Chapter 7).

Real world · AM is not dead

All Indian medium-wave broadcasting (All India Radio, 531–1602 kHz, 9 kHz channels) is AM. Every conversation between a civil pilot and air-traffic control (118–137 MHz) is AM, chosen partly because, when two pilots transmit at once, controllers hear a squeal rather than one signal silently overriding the other (FM’s “capture effect” would hide the weaker aircraft). Ham-radio, marine and military HF use SSB. The picture in analog TV was VSB. Your phone’s digital modulation (QAM) is amplitude-modulation of two carriers at once. Even the humble remote-control and key-fob transmitter is amplitude modulation in its simplest form: on–off keying.

Why modulate at all?

  1. Antenna size. An efficient antenna is a sizeable fraction of a wavelength, $\lambda/4=c/4f$. For a 3 kHz audio signal that is $\frac{3\times10^8}{4\cdot3000}=25$ km of wire. At 1 MHz it is 75 m (an AM radio mast), at 100 MHz 0.75 m (the stub on your FM radio), at 2.4 GHz 3 cm (inside your phone).
  2. Sharing. All voices are in the same band (0–4 kHz). Shifting each to a different carrier lets thousands of stations share the same air: frequency-division multiplexing (Chapter 9).
  3. Channel matching and noise. Physical channels pass only certain bands (ionosphere, optical fibre, coax); modulation moves the signal to where the medium is friendly and, for angle modulation (Chapter 7), trades bandwidth for noise immunity.

All the analog schemes of this chapter are linear modulation: the message $m(t)$ multiplies, or is added to, a carrier, so the modulated spectrum is a shifted copy of $M(f)$ and superposition holds (two messages added at the input give two spectra added at the output). Angle modulation, the next chapter, is non-linear, with a very different spectrum.

DSB-SC: multiplying by a cosine

The simplest idea: multiply the message $m(t)$ (bandwidth $W$) by the carrier. From Chapter 2, multiplication by $\cos2\pi f_ct$ copies the spectrum to $\pm f_c$ at half height: $$s(t)=A_c\,m(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct)\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad S(f)=\tfrac{A_c}2\big[M(f-f_c)+M(f+f_c)\big].$$ This is double-sideband suppressed-carrier (DSB-SC). The part of the spectrum above $f_c$ is the upper sideband (USB), the mirror image below is the lower sideband (LSB). There is no carrier line in $S(f)$; the bandwidth is $B_T=2W$; all of the transmitted power carries information. The average power is $$P_s=\overline{A_c^2m^2\cos^2\omega_ct}=\frac{A_c^2}{2}\,\overline{m^2}=\frac{A_c^2}{2}P_m,$$ the factor $\frac12$ again coming from the carrier’s $\cos^2$. To recover $m(t)$ multiply again by a synchronised local carrier and low-pass filter: $$s(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct)=\tfrac{A_c}2m(t)+\tfrac{A_c}2m(t)\cos(4\pi f_ct)\ \xrightarrow{\text{LPF}}\ \tfrac{A_c}2m(t).$$ The second term sits at $2f_c$ and is removed by the filter. This is coherent (synchronous) detection.

DSB-SC system. The multiplier is a “balanced” or “ring” modulator in hardware (four diodes), which cancels the carrier by symmetry. The receiver must regenerate the carrier with the exact frequency and phase: that is the price of suppressing it.

What happens when the local carrier is wrong?

Derivation · phase and frequency error in a coherent detector

Let the receiver multiply by $\cos(2\pi(f_c+\Delta f)t+\varphi)$. By the product-to-sum rule, $$A_cm(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct)\cdot\cos(2\pi(f_c+\Delta f)t+\varphi)=\tfrac{A_c}2m(t)\big[\cos(2\pi\Delta f\,t+\varphi)+\cos(2\pi(2f_c+\Delta f)t+\varphi)\big].$$ After the LPF the output is $y(t)=\frac{A_c}2m(t)\cos(2\pi\Delta f\,t+\varphi)$.

  • Phase error only ($\Delta f=0$): $y=\frac{A_c}2m(t)\cos\varphi$. The message keeps its shape but fades as $\cos\varphi$, vanishing at $\varphi=90^\circ$ (the “quadrature null”). Output signal power falls as $\cos^2\varphi$: $60^\circ$ error costs $6$ dB.
  • Frequency error only ($\varphi=0$): $y=\frac{A_c}2m(t)\cos(2\pi\Delta f\,t)$: the message is multiplied by a slow cosine and beats, rising and falling in loudness $\Delta f$ times a second. Even $\Delta f=1$ Hz is intolerable for speech.
Message: a two-tone signal. With a phase error the recovered waveform keeps its shape but shrinks by $\cos\varphi$. With a frequency error it is multiplied by a slow oscillation. Both are fatal in a real receiver, so DSB-SC receivers use a Costas loop or squaring loop to lock a local oscillator onto the (hidden) carrier (Chapter 16).

Modulators: building a multiplier

An ideal analog multiplier is expensive, wideband and noisy; practical designs use a trick.

Derivation · the switching (ring) modulator

Instead of multiplying by $\cos\omega_ct$, multiply by a square wave $p(t)=\pm1$ of the same fundamental frequency, which a diode ring does by reversing the polarity of $m(t)$ at the carrier rate. The Fourier series of the square wave (Chapter 2) is $p(t)=\frac4\pi\big[\cos\omega_ct-\frac13\cos3\omega_ct+\frac15\cos5\omega_ct-\cdots\big]$, so $$m(t)p(t)=\frac4\pi m(t)\cos\omega_ct-\frac{4}{3\pi}m(t)\cos3\omega_ct+\cdots$$ A band-pass filter centred on $f_c$ (width $2W$) keeps only the first term: a DSB-SC signal with $A_c=4/\pi$. The unwanted terms at $3f_c,5f_c,\dots$ lie far outside the passband. Because the switching action is insensitive to the amplitude of the carrier, this modulator is robust, efficient and simple: the heart of every mixer and of most DSB-SC and I/Q modulators.

Balanced modulators. Two conventional-AM modulators fed with $+m$ and $-m$ and subtracted give $A_c[1+\mu m]\cos\omega_ct-A_c[1-\mu m]\cos\omega_ct=2A_c\mu m\cos\omega_ct$: the carrier cancels exactly because of the symmetry. Square-law modulator. Feed $x=m(t)+\cos\omega_ct$ through $y=ax+bx^2$: the $bx^2$ term contains $2b\,m(t)\cos\omega_ct$, the product we want, plus $bm^2$, $a\,m$ and carrier terms that a band-pass filter at $f_c$ largely removes; the filter keeps $a\cos\omega_ct+2bm\cos\omega_ct=a[1+\frac{2b}{a}m]\cos\omega_ct$: conventional AM with $\mu=2b/a\cdot|m|_{\max}$ (GATE 2018 below).

Conventional AM: send the carrier too

Fessenden’s trick for cheap receivers: add a big carrier so that the receiver doesn’t have to regenerate it. Transmit $$s(t)=A_c\big[1+\mu\,m(t)\big]\cos(2\pi f_ct),\qquad |m(t)|\le1,$$ where $\mu$ is the modulation index (“depth”). As long as $\mu\le1$, the factor $1+\mu m(t)$ is never negative, so the envelope of $s(t)$ is a faithful copy of the message (plus DC) and a simple diode circuit can detect it, without synchronising to anything. For $\mu>1$ the envelope flips sign, the envelope no longer equals $1+\mu m$, and the detector output is distorted: over-modulation (it also splatters spectrum into adjacent channels, which regulators forbid).

Derivation · spectrum, power and efficiency of AM

For a single tone $m=\cos\omega_mt$: $s=A_c\cos\omega_ct+\mu A_c\cos\omega_mt\cos\omega_ct=A_c\cos\omega_ct+\frac{\mu A_c}2\cos(\omega_c+\omega_m)t+\frac{\mu A_c}2\cos(\omega_c-\omega_m)t$: a carrier and two sidebands each of amplitude $\mu A_c/2$, at spacing $f_m$. From the envelope $A_c(1+\mu\cos\omega_mt)$: $A_{\max}=A_c(1+\mu)$, $A_{\min}=A_c(1-\mu)$, so $$\mu=\frac{A_{\max}-A_{\min}}{A_{\max}+A_{\min}}=\frac{A_m}{A_c},\qquad B_T=2f_m.$$ Powers (across $1\,\Omega$; powers of different-frequency terms add, Chapter 1): carrier $P_c=\dfrac{A_c^2}2$; each sideband $\dfrac12\Big(\dfrac{\mu A_c}2\Big)^2=\dfrac{\mu^2}4P_c$; total $P_t=P_c\Big(1+\dfrac{\mu^2}2\Big)$. For a general zero-mean message with $|m|\le1$ and power $P_m=\overline{m^2}$, the same algebra gives $P_t=P_c(1+\mu^2P_m)$ (the cross term $2\mu m$ averages to zero), and $$\eta=\frac{P_{SB}}{P_t}=\frac{\mu^2P_m}{1+\mu^2P_m}\ \overset{\text{tone}}=\ \frac{\mu^2}{2+\mu^2}\le\frac13\quad(\text{at }\mu=1).$$ Antenna current rises as $I_t=I_c\sqrt{1+\mu^2/2}$ (power $\propto I^2$ in a fixed resistance). For several tones, the sideband powers add, so $\mu_{\rm total}=\sqrt{\mu_1^2+\mu_2^2+\cdots}$.

The painful fact: at best, two-thirds of the transmitter power is wasted in the carrier, and at $\mu=0.5$ (typical speech) the efficiency is only $0.25/2.25=11\%$. Speech has a large peak-to-rms ratio ($P_m\approx0.05$–$0.1$ even when the peaks reach $|m|=1$), so the efficiency in practice is a few percent. That is the price of receiver simplicity. (The economics: one transmitter, millions of cheap receivers.) Broadcasters therefore use audio compression and peak limiting to raise $P_m$ while keeping $|m|\le1$: it makes the station sound louder and raises the useful fraction of transmitted power.

Worked example

An AM envelope has maximum 3 V and minimum 1 V: $\mu=\frac{3-1}{3+1}=0.5$ (GATE 2014 Set 2). Doubling $\mu$ multiplies the ratio (sideband power)/(carrier power) $=\mu^2/2$ by 4 (GATE 2014 Set 4). For several tones, the powers add, so $\mu_{\rm total}=\sqrt{\mu_1^2+\mu_2^2+\cdots}$.

Worked example · GATE 2024 Q.28, solved in two lines

The AM output is $s(t)=A\cos400t+B\cos360t+B\cos440t$ (carrier amplitude $A$, two sidebands of amplitude $B$). Carrier power $A^2/2=50\Rightarrow A=10$. Sideband power $=2\cdot\frac{B^2}2=B^2$. “Sideband/total $=\frac19$”: $\dfrac{B^2}{50+B^2}=\dfrac19\Rightarrow B^2=6.25\Rightarrow B=2.5$ V. The key accepted 2.49–2.51.

The envelope detector

The cheapest demodulator ever invented: a diode followed by a parallel RC. The diode lets the capacitor charge to each carrier peak; between peaks the capacitor discharges through $R$ with time constant $RC$. For it to follow the envelope but not the carrier ripple we need $RC$ in a window.

Derivation · the $RC$ design window

Lower limit (ripple). Between two carrier peaks, a time $1/f_c$ apart, the capacitor voltage decays by the factor $e^{-1/(f_cRC)}\approx1-\frac1{f_cRC}$. The ripple is small only if $RC\gg1/f_c$.

Upper limit (diagonal clipping). When the envelope $e(t)=A_c(1+\mu\cos\omega_mt)$ is falling, the capacitor discharges exponentially, at slope $-v/RC\approx-e(t)/RC$. The output keeps up only if the discharge is at least as steep as the envelope’s fall at every instant: $\dfrac{e(t)}{RC}\ge\Big|\dfrac{de}{dt}\Big|=A_c\mu\omega_m\sin\omega_mt$. With $\theta=\omega_mt$, $$RC\le\frac{1+\mu\cos\theta}{\mu\omega_m\sin\theta}\quad\forall\theta.$$ Minimise the right side: setting its derivative to zero gives $\cos\theta=-\mu$, where the value is $\frac{1-\mu^2}{\mu\omega_m\sqrt{1-\mu^2}}$. Therefore $$\boxed{\ \frac1{f_c}\ \ll\ RC\ \le\ \frac{\sqrt{1-\mu^2}}{\mu\,\omega_m}\ }$$ (for $\mu\to1$ the upper limit tends to zero: a 100%-modulated signal needs a vanishing time constant, one reason broadcasters keep $\mu\lesssim0.9$ at peaks). Too small an $RC$: carrier ripple at the output. Too large: the capacitor cannot discharge fast enough and the output stays above the envelope: diagonal clipping. Find the sweet spot:

Diode envelope detector, simulated sample by sample. Sweep the time constant $RC$ from too small (ripple) to too large (diagonal clipping). A coupling capacitor afterwards would remove the DC term $A_c$.
Negative-peak clipping: why the AC load matters

The detector output has a DC level $A_c$ and an audio part $\mu A_c\cos\omega_mt$. A coupling capacitor passes the audio to the next stage, whose input resistance $R_L$ is in parallel with $R$ for audio but not for DC. The DC load is $R$, the AC load $R_{ac}=R\parallel R_L$. At the negative peak the diode must still conduct: the audio swing across $R_{ac}$ cannot exceed the DC across $R$: $\mu A_c\cdot\frac{R_{ac}}{R}\le A_c$... precisely, the audio current peak $\mu A_c/R_{ac}$ must not exceed the DC current $A_c/R$, so $$\mu\le\frac{R_{ac}}{R_{dc}}.$$ A badly coupled detector clips the troughs of the audio at a modulation depth lower than 100% even though the transmitted signal is perfect: increase $R_L$ or add a buffer.

Engineering practice · AGC, diode drop and square-law operation

A silicon diode needs $\approx0.6$ V to conduct, so for small signals the envelope detector degenerates into a square-law detector ($i\propto v^2$), which produces output $\propto A_c^2[1+\mu m]^2\approx A_c^2[1+2\mu m+\mu^2m^2]$ with second-harmonic distortion $\mu/4$ relative to the wanted term, and an output that is proportional to input power rather than amplitude. Practical receivers precede the detector with an IF amplifier whose gain is controlled by automatic gain control (AGC): the DC component of the detector output ($\propto A_c$) is fed back to hold the carrier amplitude constant at the detector input, which both linearises the diode and keeps the audio level steady when a signal fades (the reason an AM radio needs no volume adjustment as you drive under a bridge, within limits).

Transmitters: low-level vs high-level modulation

There are two ways to build the transmitter. Low-level modulation: make the AM signal at low power and amplify it with linear amplifiers (class A/B), which are inefficient (30–60%). High-level modulation: amplify the unmodulated carrier in a highly efficient class-C amplifier (conduction angle <180°, efficiency 70–80%) and modulate its supply voltage by an audio amplifier (“collector/plate modulation”), so the supply varies as $V_{CC}[1+\mu m(t)]$ and the output amplitude follows. Broadcast transmitters of hundreds of kilowatts use this approach (or its modern successors) because the electricity bill matters.

Worked example · carrier, average and peak envelope power

A $100\,$kW (carrier) medium-wave transmitter modulated 100% by a tone: $P_t=100(1+\tfrac12)=150$ kW average; the sidebands carry $50$ kW (33%). The peak envelope power is that at the envelope’s crest, $A_c^2(1+\mu)^2/2=P_c(1+\mu)^2=400$ kW: the final amplifier and the antenna must withstand four times the carrier power. For $\mu=0.8$: $P_t=132$ kW and PEP $=324$ kW. By contrast an SSB transmitter’s rating is its PEP, and its average power for speech is 10 to 20 dB lower.

Real world · why AM stations are heard farther away at night

Medium waves travel two ways. Ground wave follows the curvature of the earth and attenuates with distance and conductivity (good over sea, poor over dry land): the daytime service range of a 100 kW station is $100$–$200$ km. At night the D-layer of the ionosphere (which absorbs MF in the day) disappears, and the E/F layers reflect sky waves hundreds of kilometres: stations suddenly appear from far away, and interfere with the local ones. Regulators therefore require many medium-wave stations to reduce power or change antenna patterns at night. The 9 kHz channel spacing ($10$ kHz in the Americas) and the 4.5–5 kHz audio bandwidth of AIR follow from the same crowding.

SSB: stop sending the same information twice

A real message has $M(-f)=M^*(f)$: its two sidebands (above and below the carrier) are mirror images and carry identical information. Single-sideband (SSB) transmits only one of them, halving the bandwidth ($B_T=W$) and, with the carrier also suppressed, spending all power on information.

Derivation · the SSB signal in the time domain

Start with the one-sided spectrum of the message, which is the analytic signal $m_+=m+j\hat m$ (Chapter 3), $M_+(f)=2M(f)u(f)$. The upper sideband signal is the part of $\frac{A_c}2M(f-f_c)$ with $f>f_c$, which is the real part of the complex signal $\frac{A_c}2m_+(t)e^{j2\pi f_ct}$: $$s_{\rm USB}(t)=\mathrm{Re}\Big\{\tfrac{A_c}2\,[m(t)+j\hat m(t)]\,e^{j2\pi f_ct}\Big\}=\tfrac{A_c}2\big[m(t)\cos2\pi f_ct-\hat m(t)\sin2\pi f_ct\big].$$ The lower sideband uses the conjugate (the negative-frequency half of $M$): $s_{\rm LSB}=\frac{A_c}2[m\cos2\pi f_ct+\hat m\sin2\pi f_ct]$. Check with $m=\cos\omega_mt$, $\hat m=\sin\omega_mt$: $\cos\omega_mt\cos\omega_ct-\sin\omega_mt\sin\omega_ct=\cos(\omega_c+\omega_m)t$, the upper sideband only ✓. Power ($m$ and $\hat m$ have equal power $P_m$ and are orthogonal): $P_{\rm SSB}=\big(\frac{A_c}2\big)^2\big[\frac{P_m}2+\frac{P_m}2\big]=\dfrac{A_c^2P_m}4$, half the DSB-SC power $\frac{A_c^2P_m}2$ for the same $A_c$ — and half of the information bandwidth means the same SNR per unit bandwidth (Chapter 8).

Two ways to make it. Filter method: make DSB-SC, then remove one sideband with a very sharp filter. The difficulty is the transition band: telephone speech starts at 300 Hz, so the two sidebands are separated by a 600 Hz gap centred on the carrier; the filter must go from passband to $-40\ldots-60$ dB within $600$ Hz. At a carrier of $10$ MHz that is a fractional transition of $6\times10^{-5}$, which no LC filter can achieve ($Q\approx f_c/\Delta f\approx1.7\times10^4$). So SSB is generated at a low fixed frequency ($100$ kHz–$9$ MHz) using crystal or mechanical filters ($Q\sim10^4$–$10^5$) and then up-converted by mixing to the desired channel. Phase-shift method: use the formula above directly: two multipliers, a Hilbert network on the message (an all-pass $-90^\circ$ phase shifter, accurate over 300–3400 Hz), a quadrature carrier, and a subtractor (USB) or adder (LSB). Its accuracy is limited by the Hilbert network and the quadrature oscillator: a $1^\circ$ phase error leaves the unwanted sideband at $-41$ dBc. The Weaver method avoids wide-band phase shifters by translating the message in two stages.

Phase-shift (Hartley-type) SSB modulator: two multipliers, a $-90^\circ$ Hilbert network on the message and a $-90^\circ$ shift on the carrier. Subtracting gives USB, adding gives LSB.

SSB receivers still need a coherent carrier. A phase error $\varphi$ gives $\frac{A_c}4[m\cos\varphi+\hat m\sin\varphi]$ (phase distortion; tolerable for speech because the ear is insensitive to phase), and a frequency error shifts every component by $\Delta f$: speech turns into the cartoon-duck voice because harmonics lose their integer relationships. Frequency stability needed is $\lesssim10$–$50$ Hz, i.e. $\sim10^{-6}$ at 10 MHz: easily met by crystal oscillators. SSB with a pilot carrier (reduced carrier) lets the receiver lock automatically. A bonus of SSB: it is far less damaged by selective fading, because there is no carrier–sideband phase relationship for the channel to scramble.

Real world · SSB in the telephone network and the amateur bands

Before digital transmission, long-distance telephony used SSB-FDM: each voice channel (300–3400 Hz, with guard bands, 4 kHz spacing) was shifted to its own slot. Twelve channels form a group (60–108 kHz), five groups a supergroup (60 channels), and so on up to a 10,800-channel coaxial system. Radio amateurs use LSB below 10 MHz and USB above by convention; marine and aviation HF links use USB; a single SSB transmitter that is PEP-rated at 100 W can reach round the world. The same bandwidth saving explains why ham operators describe “the end of AM on the bands”.

VSB and QAM: two clever compromises

Vestigial sideband (VSB)

TV video has components down to DC, so the filter method cannot cut the lower sideband sharply. Compromise: keep one full sideband and a “vestige” of the other, shaped so that the two edges add up to a flat response across the transition.

Derivation · the VSB filter condition

Pass the DSB-SC signal $A_cm(t)\cos\omega_ct$ through a filter $H_v(f)$. The output spectrum is $S_v(f)=\frac{A_c}2[M(f-f_c)+M(f+f_c)]H_v(f)$. Demodulate coherently: multiply by $\cos\omega_ct$ (shifts the spectrum by $\pm f_c$ and halves it) and low-pass filter, keeping the terms near $f=0$: $$Y(f)=\frac{A_c}4\,M(f)\,\big[H_v(f-f_c)+H_v(f+f_c)\big],\qquad|f|\le W.$$ For distortionless recovery we need the bracket to be constant over the message band: $$\boxed{\,H_v(f-f_c)+H_v(f+f_c)=\text{const}\quad\text{for }|f|\le W\,}$$ This says $H_v$ has odd symmetry about $f_c$: $H_v(f_c+x)+H_v(f_c-x)=H_v(f_c)\cdot2$ for $|x|\le f_v$ (the vestige width). For an ideal USB filter this is satisfied with a step at $f_c$; any gentle roll-off with odd symmetry (a straight slope or a raised-cosine edge) also works. Bandwidth ends up $\approx W+f_v$.

Analog TV (System B/G, used in India, 7 MHz channel; System M in the USA, 6 MHz): video bandwidth 5 MHz (4.2 MHz in the USA) with a 0.75 MHz vestige, in a channel that also holds the FM sound carrier. A bit of carrier is left in so an inexpensive envelope detector still works; the small quadrature distortion it produces is acceptable. Digital terrestrial TV (ATSC) and cable systems use 8-VSB/QAM with the same logic, but with digital symbols.

Quadrature-carrier multiplexing (analog QAM)

Sine and cosine carriers are orthogonal: you can send two messages in the same band, $s(t)=A_c[m_1(t)\cos2\pi f_ct+m_2(t)\sin2\pi f_ct]$, and separate them at the receiver by multiplying with $\cos$ or $\sin$ and low-pass filtering (the I/Q demodulator of Chapter 3). With a phase error $\varphi$ in the local oscillator the first output becomes $\frac{A_c}2[m_1\cos\varphi+m_2\sin\varphi]$ (the second message leaks in as crosstalk with weight $\sin\varphi$: unlike the single DSB case, the error costs more than level). This is how colour was added to black-and-white TV (the two colour-difference signals on a 4.43 MHz subcarrier in PAL and 3.58 MHz in NTSC) and it is the direct ancestor of digital QAM in Chapter 15. QAM is a reminder that a bandpass signal has two degrees of freedom per Hz: $2W$ of bandwidth carries two real messages of bandwidth $W$, the same efficiency as SSB.

One triangular message spectrum, its positive-frequency half in blue and its negative-frequency half in red, modulated in different ways. A real message has a symmetric magnitude spectrum; the colours are only there to track each half. The USB keeps the upper blue half at $+f_c$ in its original orientation; the LSB’s picture is the mirror image: its red half has moved to $+f$.

Frequency translation: the mixer

Everything above is repeated in receivers as mixing: multiplying a signal by a local-oscillator cosine to move it to a new carrier. $\cos\omega_1t\cdot\cos\omega_2t=\tfrac12[\cos(\omega_1-\omega_2)t+\cos(\omega_1+\omega_2)t]$ produces the difference and the sum frequency; a filter keeps one. A mixer is DSB-SC modulation with a carrier that is not the message; it preserves the information (bandwidth, shape) and merely relocates it, which is why superheterodyne receivers (Chapter 8) can tune any station onto a fixed intermediate frequency. A mixer built from a non-linear device also creates intermodulation terms $mf_1\pm nf_2$ — the origin of the spurious responses discussed in Chapter 8.

Output $\propto m(t)\cos\varphi=0$ at $\varphi=90^\circ$ (the quadrature null). It is not $\hat m$: that arises only in SSB. (B).

Which of the following statements about conventional AM with $\mu\le1$ are true?
  1. An envelope detector recovers the message without a local oscillator.
  2. The carrier carries the message.
  3. At least 2/3 of the power is in the carrier for a single tone.
  4. The bandwidth is $W$.

(A) true. (B) false: the sidebands carry the information. (C) true: $\eta\le\frac13$ at $\mu=1$. (D) false: $2W$.

  2. the diodes cannot work with sinusoids
  3. the fundamental of the square wave does the modulation and a band-pass filter removes the harmonics
  4. the message must be a square wave too

$p(t)=\frac4\pi[\cos\omega_ct-\frac13\cos3\omega_ct+\cdots]$; the filter at $f_c$ keeps only $\frac4\pi m\cos\omega_ct$. (C).

Set B · Numerical practice

An AM transmitter has an unmodulated carrier power of 100 W. It is modulated to $\mu=0.8$ by a single tone. The total transmitted power (watts) is ______.

$P_t=100(1+0.64/2)=132$ W. (Sidebands: 32 W, efficiency $0.64/2.64=24\%$.)

The antenna current of an AM transmitter is 10 A unmodulated and rises to $11.18$ A when modulated by a single tone. The modulation index is ______.

$I_t/I_c=\sqrt{1+\mu^2/2}=1.118\Rightarrow1+\mu^2/2=1.25\Rightarrow\mu^2=0.5$, $\mu=0.707$.

An AM wave is modulated simultaneously by two tones with $\mu_1=0.3$ and $\mu_2=0.4$. The effective modulation index is ______.

$\mu=\sqrt{0.09+0.16}=0.5$.

A DSB-SC receiver's local oscillator has a constant phase error of $60^\circ$. The recovered message power relative to the ideal-phase case is ______.

Amplitude $\propto\cos60^\circ=0.5$; power $\propto\cos^2=0.25$.

A 1 MHz AM signal is modulated by speech up to 5 kHz. For an envelope detector to work without ripple or diagonal clipping, a suitable $RC$ time constant is
  1. 10 ns
  2. 0.1 μs
  3. 20 μs
  4. 5 ms

Need $1/f_c=1\,\mu$s $\ll RC\ll1/W=200\,\mu$s. Only 20 μs fits. (10 ns / 0.1 μs follow the carrier; 5 ms cannot track the envelope.)

Telephone speech occupying 300–3400 Hz is sent as upper-sideband SSB on a 10 MHz carrier. The occupied band is
  1. 9.9966 – 10.0003 MHz
  2. 10.0003 – 10.0034 MHz
  3. 9.9997 – 10.0003 MHz
  4. 9.9966 – 10.0034 MHz

USB: $f_c+300\ \text{Hz}$ to $f_c+3400$ Hz. Bandwidth $3.1$ kHz $\approx W$.

An AM transmitter has an unmodulated carrier power of 100 kW. Its peak envelope power (in kW) when modulated 100% by a single tone is ______.

PEP $=P_c(1+\mu)^2=100\times4=\mathbf{400}$ kW (average power $=150$ kW).

A speech signal normalised to $|m|\le1$ has average power $P_m=0.1$. It AM-modulates a carrier with $\mu=1$. The fraction of the transmitted power that lies in the sidebands is ______ (three decimals).

$\eta=\dfrac{\mu^2P_m}{1+\mu^2P_m}=\dfrac{0.1}{1.1}=\mathbf{0.091}$. More than 90% of the power is spent on the carrier, which is why broadcasters compress the audio to raise $P_m$.

Telephone channels with 4 kHz spacing (including guard bands) are SSB-multiplexed into the band 60–108 kHz (the “group”). The number of channels is ______.

$\dfrac{108-60}{4}=\mathbf{12}$. Five groups form a 60-channel supergroup.

An envelope detector receives an AM wave modulated to $\mu=0.8$ by a 5 kHz audio tone. The largest $RC$ (in microseconds) that avoids diagonal clipping is ______.

$RC\le\dfrac{\sqrt{1-\mu^2}}{\mu\omega_m}=\dfrac{0.6}{0.8\cdot2\pi\cdot5000}=\dfrac{0.6}{25133}=\mathbf{23.9}\ \mu$s.

Set C · Exam-style problems

An amplitude modulator has output (in volts) $s(t)=A\cos(400t)+B\cos(360t)+B\cos(440t)$. The carrier power normalised to $1\,\Omega$ is 50 W. The ratio of the total sideband power to the total power is $1/9$. The value of $B$ (volts, two decimals) is ______.

$A=10$ (since $A^2/2=50$). Sidebands: $2\times B^2/2=B^2$. $\frac{B^2}{50+B^2}=\frac19\Rightarrow B^2=6.25$, $B=\mathbf{2.50}$.

A carrier is amplitude modulated by a single-tone message with modulation index 50%. If the carrier and one of the sidebands are suppressed, the percentage of power saved (one decimal) is ______.

Full AM: $P_c(1+\mu^2/2)=1.125P_c$. One sideband: $\mu^2P_c/4=0.0625P_c$. Saved $=1-\frac{0.0625}{1.125}=\mathbf{94.4\%}$.

A real band-limited process $X(t)$ has two-sided PSD $S_X(f)=10^{-6}(3000-|f|)$ W/Hz for $|f|\le3$ kHz and 0 otherwise. It modulates the carrier $\cos(16000\pi t)$ (i.e. $s(t)=X(t)\cos(2\pi\cdot8000\,t)$) and the result passes through an ideal band-pass filter of unity gain, centre frequency 8 kHz and bandwidth 2 kHz. The output power in watts is ______.

$S_s(f)=\frac14[S_X(f-f_c)+S_X(f+f_c)]$ (modulation theorem on PSDs: factor $\frac14$). The filter passes $7$–$9$ kHz, which is $|u|\le1$ kHz of $S_X$ shifted. Total 2.5 W (key 2.4–2.6).

$m(t)$ is band-limited to $[-W,W]$. DSB-SC: $f_{DSB}=A_cm(t)\cos2\pi f_ct$. AM: $f_{AM}=A_c(1+\mu m(t))\cos2\pi f_ct$. Which statements are correct?

  2. An envelope detector can be used for DSB-SC even if $m(t)$ changes sign.

Carrier amplitude 20 (at 900 Hz), sidebands 5 each (at 800 and 1000 Hz): each sideband is $\mu A_c/2$, so $\mu=\frac{2\cdot5}{20}=\mathbf{0.5}$.

Chapter 6 in one breath

  • Multiply by $\cos2\pi f_ct$ ⇒ spectrum to $\pm f_c$ at half height. DSB-SC: $2W$, all power useful, needs coherent detection ($\cos\varphi$ fade, beat for $\Delta f$); power $\frac{A_c^2}2P_m$. Diode-ring modulator = multiplication by a square wave ($4/\pi$).
  • AM: $A_c[1+\mu m]\cos\omega_ct$; $\mu\le1$; $\mu=\frac{A_{\max}-A_{\min}}{A_{\max}+A_{\min}}$; $P_t=P_c(1+\mu^2P_m)$; $\eta=\frac{\mu^2}{2+\mu^2}\le\frac13$ (tone); PEP $=P_c(1+\mu)^2$.
  • Envelope detector: $1/f_c\ll RC\le\sqrt{1-\mu^2}/\mu\omega_m$; negative-peak clipping unless $\mu\le R_{ac}/R_{dc}$; AGC from the DC term.
  • SSB: bandwidth $W$; $s=\frac{A_c}2[m\cos\omega_ct\mp\hat m\sin\omega_ct]$; power $\frac{A_c^2P_m}4$; filter method needs crystal filters at a low IF; phase method needs a precise Hilbert network.
  • VSB: $H_v(f-f_c)+H_v(f+f_c)=$ const for $|f|\le W$ (odd symmetry about $f_c$). QAM: two messages on cos and sin, crosstalk $\sin\varphi$ under phase error.
  • Powers of modulated PSD: $S_s=\frac14[S_X(f-f_c)+S_X(f+f_c)]$.
