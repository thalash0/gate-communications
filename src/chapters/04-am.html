History · Christmas Eve, 1906

Guglielmo Marconi had sent Morse dots and dashes across the Atlantic in 1901, but a spark transmitter can only be on or off. Reginald Fessenden, a Canadian physicist, wanted the human voice. His idea: generate a pure, continuous high-frequency wave and let the microphone vary its amplitude. On Christmas Eve 1906, from a mast at Brant Rock, Massachusetts, he broadcast a violin solo ("O Holy Night"), a Bible reading and a greeting. Radio operators on United Fruit Company ships, accustomed to hearing only beeps in their headphones, heard music and speech from the sky.

The enabling technology arrived the same year: Lee de Forest's audion triode (1906), the first device that could amplify. By 1920, KDKA in Pittsburgh was running scheduled broadcasts, and by the 1930s AM radio was in most homes.

Real world · AM is not dead

All Indian medium-wave broadcasting (All India Radio, 531–1602 kHz, 9 kHz channels) is AM. Every conversation between a civil pilot and air-traffic control (118–137 MHz) is AM, chosen partly because, when two pilots transmit at once, controllers hear a squeal rather than one signal silently overriding the other. Ham-radio and marine HF use SSB. The picture in analog TV was VSB. Your phone's digital modulation (QAM) is amplitude-modulation of two carriers at once.

Why modulate at all?

  1. Antenna size. An efficient antenna is a sizeable fraction of a wavelength, $\lambda/4=c/4f$. For a 3 kHz audio signal that is $\frac{3\times10^8}{4\cdot3000}=25$ km of wire. At 1 MHz it is 75 m (an AM radio mast), at 100 MHz 0.75 m (the stub on your FM radio), at 2.4 GHz 3 cm (inside your phone).
  2. Sharing. All voices are in the same band (0–4 kHz). Shifting each to a different carrier lets thousands of stations share the same air: frequency-division multiplexing.
  3. Channel matching and noise. Physical channels pass only certain bands (ionosphere, optical fibre, coax); modulation moves the signal to where the medium is friendly and, for angle modulation (Chapter 5), trades bandwidth for noise immunity.

DSB-SC: multiplying by a cosine

The simplest idea: multiply the message $m(t)$ (bandwidth $W$) by the carrier. From Chapter 2, multiplication by $\cos2\pi f_ct$ copies the spectrum to $\pm f_c$ at half height: $$s(t)=A_c\,m(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct)\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad S(f)=\tfrac{A_c}2\big[M(f-f_c)+M(f+f_c)\big].$$ This is double-sideband suppressed-carrier (DSB-SC). There is no carrier line in $S(f)$; the bandwidth is $B_T=2W$; all of the transmitted power carries information. To recover $m(t)$ multiply again by a synchronised local carrier and low-pass filter: $$s(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct)=\tfrac{A_c}2m(t)+\tfrac{A_c}2m(t)\cos(4\pi f_ct)\ \xrightarrow{\text{LPF}}\ \tfrac{A_c}2m(t).$$ The second term sits at $2f_c$ and is removed by the filter. This is coherent (synchronous) detection.

DSB-SC system. The multiplier is a "balanced" or "ring" modulator in hardware (four diodes), which cancels the carrier by symmetry. The receiver must regenerate the carrier with the exact frequency and phase: that is the price of suppressing it.

What happens when the local carrier is wrong?

Let the receiver use $\cos(2\pi f_ct+\varphi)$. Then the LPF output is $\frac{A_c}2m(t)\cos\varphi$: the signal fades as $\cos\varphi$ and vanishes at $\varphi=90^\circ$ (the "quadrature null"). If instead the frequency is off by $\Delta f$, the output is $\frac{A_c}2m(t)\cos(2\pi\Delta f\,t)$: the message is multiplied by a slow cosine and beats, rising and falling in loudness. Try both:

Message: a two-tone signal. With a phase error the recovered waveform keeps its shape but shrinks by $\cos\varphi$. With a frequency error it is multiplied by a slow oscillation. Both are fatal in a real receiver, so DSB-SC receivers use a Costas loop or squaring loop to lock a local oscillator onto the (hidden) carrier.

Conventional AM: send the carrier too

Fessenden's trick for cheap receivers: add a big carrier so that the receiver doesn't have to regenerate it. Transmit $$s(t)=A_c\big[1+\mu\,m(t)\big]\cos(2\pi f_ct),\qquad |m(t)|\le1,$$ where $\mu$ is the modulation index ("depth"). As long as $\mu\le1$, the factor $1+\mu m(t)$ is never negative, so the envelope of $s(t)$ is a faithful copy of the message (plus DC) and a simple diode circuit can detect it, without synchronising to anything. For $\mu>1$ the envelope flips sign, the envelope no longer equals $1+\mu m$, and the detector output is distorted: over-modulation.

Middle: the output of an ideal envelope detector $|1+\mu m|$ minus its DC (green) against the true message (grey): they agree until $\mu>1$, then the bottom of each cycle gets folded up. Bottom: spectrum: one carrier line and two sidebands whose height is $\mu/2$ of the carrier. The readout shows where the power goes.
Single-tone AM: everything you need $$s(t)=A_c\cos\omega_ct+\frac{\mu A_c}{2}\cos(\omega_c+\omega_m)t+\frac{\mu A_c}{2}\cos(\omega_c-\omega_m)t$$ $$\mu=\frac{A_{\max}-A_{\min}}{A_{\max}+A_{\min}}=\frac{A_m}{A_c},\qquad B_T=2f_m .$$

Powers (across $1\,\Omega$): carrier $P_c=\dfrac{A_c^2}2$; each sideband $\dfrac{\mu^2}4P_c$; total $P_t=P_c\Big(1+\dfrac{\mu^2}2\Big)$. Efficiency (fraction of power in the sidebands, the useful part): $$\eta=\frac{P_{SB}}{P_t}=\frac{\mu^2}{2+\mu^2}\le\frac13\quad(\text{at }\mu=1).$$ Antenna current rises as $I_t=I_c\sqrt{1+\mu^2/2}$.

The painful fact: at best, two-thirds of the transmitter power is wasted in the carrier, and at $\mu=0.5$ (typical speech) the efficiency is only $0.25/2.25=11\%$. That is the price of receiver simplicity. (The economics: one transmitter, millions of cheap receivers.)

Worked example

An AM envelope has maximum 3 V and minimum 1 V: $\mu=\frac{3-1}{3+1}=0.5$ (GATE 2014 Set 2). Doubling $\mu$ multiplies the ratio (sideband power)/(carrier power) $=\mu^2/2$ by 4 (GATE 2014 Set 4). For several tones, the powers add, so $\mu_{\rm total}=\sqrt{\mu_1^2+\mu_2^2+\cdots}$, and for a general message $P_{SB}=P_c\mu^2\overline{m^2(t)}$ with $|m|\le1$.

Worked example · GATE 2024 Q.28, solved in two lines

The AM output is $s(t)=A\cos400t+B\cos360t+B\cos440t$ (carrier amplitude $A$, two sidebands of amplitude $B$). Carrier power $A^2/2=50\Rightarrow A=10$. Sideband power $=2\cdot\frac{B^2}2=B^2$. "Sideband/total $=\frac19$": $\dfrac{B^2}{50+B^2}=\dfrac19\Rightarrow B^2=6.25\Rightarrow B=2.5$ V. The key accepted 2.49–2.51.

The envelope detector

The cheapest demodulator ever invented: a diode followed by a parallel RC. The diode lets the capacitor charge to each carrier peak; between peaks the capacitor discharges through $R$ with time constant $RC$. For it to follow the envelope but not the carrier ripple we need $$\frac1{f_c}\ \ll\ RC\ \ll\ \frac1{W}\qquad\text{(more exactly }RC\le\frac{\sqrt{1-\mu^2}}{\mu\,\omega_m}\text{ to avoid diagonal clipping).}$$ Too small an $RC$: carrier ripple at the output. Too large: the capacitor cannot discharge fast enough when the envelope falls and the output stays above it ("diagonal clipping"). Find the sweet spot:

Diode envelope detector, simulated sample by sample. Sweep the time constant $RC$ from too small (ripple) to too large (diagonal clipping). A coupling capacitor afterwards would remove the DC term $A_c$.

Modulators

Any device with a non-linear characteristic can multiply. Feed $x=m(t)+\cos\omega_ct$ through a square-law device $y=ax+bx^2$: the $bx^2$ term contains $2b\,m(t)\cos\omega_ct$, the product we want, plus unwanted terms that a band-pass filter at $f_c$ removes. Switching modulators (a diode ring) are the practical form. The same non-linearity logic appears in GATE 2023's FM question (Chapter 5): non-linear distortion creates new frequency components, and the question is where they land.

SSB: stop sending the same information twice

A real message has $M(-f)=M^*(f)$: its two sidebands (above and below the carrier) are mirror images and carry identical information. Single-sideband (SSB) transmits only one of them, halving the bandwidth ($B_T=W$) and, with the carrier also suppressed, spending all power on information.

Two ways to make it. Filter method: make DSB-SC, then remove one sideband with a very sharp filter (hard when the message goes down to DC; telephone speech conveniently starts at 300 Hz, leaving a 600 Hz guard gap). Phase-shift method: use the Hilbert transform from Chapter 2: $$s_{\rm USB}(t)=\tfrac{A_c}2\big[m(t)\cos2\pi f_ct-\hat m(t)\sin2\pi f_ct\big],\qquad s_{\rm LSB}(t)=\tfrac{A_c}2\big[m(t)\cos2\pi f_ct+\hat m(t)\sin2\pi f_ct\big].$$ Why it works: the signal $m+j\hat m$ has only positive frequencies (it is analytic), so multiplying by $e^{j2\pi f_ct}$ and taking the real part puts it on one side of $f_c$ only. (Check with $m=\cos\omega_mt$: $\cos\omega_mt\cos\omega_ct-\sin\omega_mt\sin\omega_ct=\cos(\omega_c+\omega_m)t$, the upper sideband only ✓.)

Phase-shift (Weaver/Hartley-type) SSB modulator: two multipliers, a $-90^\circ$ Hilbert network on the message and a $-90^\circ$ shift on the carrier. Subtracting gives USB, adding gives LSB.

SSB receivers still need a coherent carrier. A phase error $\varphi$ gives $m\cos\varphi+\hat m\sin\varphi$ (phase distortion; tolerable for speech), and a frequency error shifts every component by $\Delta f$: speech turns into the cartoon-duck voice because harmonics lose their integer relationships.

VSB and QAM: two clever compromises

Vestigial sideband (VSB). TV video has components down to DC, so the filter method cannot cut the lower sideband sharply. Compromise: keep one full sideband and a "vestige" of the other, shaped so that the two edges add up to a flat response across the transition: $H_v(f-f_c)+H_v(f+f_c)=\text{const}$ for $|f|\le W$. Bandwidth ends up $\approx W+f_v$ (analog TV: 4.2 MHz video, 0.75 MHz vestige, in a 6 MHz channel). A bit of carrier is left in so an envelope detector still works.

Quadrature-carrier multiplexing (analog QAM). Sine and cosine carriers are orthogonal: you can send two messages in the same band, $s(t)=A_c[m_1(t)\cos2\pi f_ct+m_2(t)\sin2\pi f_ct]$, and separate them at the receiver by multiplying with $\cos$ or $\sin$ and low-pass filtering. This is how colour was added to black-and-white TV (the two colour-difference signals), and it is the direct ancestor of digital QAM in Chapter 11. A phase error $\varphi$ leaks one channel into the other ("crosstalk").

One triangular message spectrum, its positive-frequency half in blue and its negative-frequency half in red, modulated in different ways. A real message has a symmetric magnitude spectrum; the colours are only there to track each half. The USB keeps the upper blue half at $+f_c$ in its original orientation; the LSB's picture is the mirror image: its red half has moved to $+f$.

Practice

An amplitude modulator has output (in volts) $s(t)=A\cos(400t)+B\cos(360t)+B\cos(440t)$. The carrier power normalised to $1\,\Omega$ is 50 W. The ratio of the total sideband power to the total power is $1/9$. The value of $B$ (volts, two decimals) is ______.

$A=10$ (since $A^2/2=50$). Sidebands: $2\times B^2/2=B^2$. $\frac{B^2}{50+B^2}=\frac19\Rightarrow B^2=6.25$, $B=\mathbf{2.50}$.

A carrier is amplitude modulated by a single-tone message with modulation index 50%. If the carrier and one of the sidebands are suppressed, the percentage of power saved (one decimal) is ______.

Full AM: $P_c(1+\mu^2/2)=1.125P_c$. One sideband: $\mu^2P_c/4=0.0625P_c$. Saved $=1-\frac{0.0625}{1.125}=\mathbf{94.4\%}$.

A real band-limited process $X(t)$ has two-sided PSD $S_X(f)=10^{-6}(3000-|f|)$ W/Hz for $|f|\le3$ kHz and 0 otherwise. It modulates the carrier $\cos(16000\pi t)$ (i.e. $s(t)=X(t)\cos(2\pi\cdot8000\,t)$) and the result passes through an ideal band-pass filter of unity gain, centre frequency 8 kHz and bandwidth 2 kHz. The output power in watts is ______.

$S_s(f)=\frac14[S_X(f-f_c)+S_X(f+f_c)]$ (modulation theorem on PSDs: factor $\frac14$). The filter passes $7$–$9$ kHz, which is $|u|\le1$ kHz of $S_X$ shifted. Power in the positive band: $\frac14\int_{-1000}^{1000}10^{-6}(3000-|u|)du=\frac14\cdot10^{-6}\cdot2\big(3\times10^6-0.5\times10^6\big)=1.25$ W; the negative band gives another 1.25. Total 2.5 W (key 2.4–2.6).

$x(t)=m(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct)$ with $m(t)=4\cos(1000\pi t)$, $f_c=1$ MHz. The signal is multiplied by $\cos(2\pi(f_c+40)t)$ and passed through an ideal low-pass filter of cut-off 510 Hz. The output is
  1. $\cos(460\pi t)$
  2. $\cos(920\pi t)$
  3. $\cos(1000\pi t)$
  4. $\cos(540\pi t)$

Product low-pass part: $4\cos(1000\pi t)\cdot\frac12\cos(80\pi t)=2\cos(1000\pi t)\cos(80\pi t)=\cos(1080\pi t)+\cos(920\pi t)$, i.e. components at 540 Hz and 460 Hz. The 510 Hz LPF keeps only $460$ Hz: $\cos(920\pi t)$. (Original option letters differ; the physics is the point.)

$m(t)$ is band-limited to $[-W,W]$. DSB-SC: $f_{DSB}=A_cm(t)\cos2\pi f_ct$. AM: $f_{AM}=A_c(1+\mu m(t))\cos2\pi f_ct$. Which statements are correct?
  1. An envelope detector can be used for DSB-SC if $m(t)>0$ for all $t$.
  2. An envelope detector can be used for DSB-SC even if $m(t)$ changes sign.
  3. An envelope detector can be used for AM only if $m(t)>0$ for all $t$.
  4. An envelope detector can be used for AM even when $m(t)$ takes negative values, provided $1+\mu m(t)>0$.

The envelope detector outputs $|\text{envelope}|$. DSB-SC with $m>0$ looks exactly like AM with envelope $A_cm(t)$ ✓ (A). With sign changes, the envelope would be $|m|$ and the phase reversals are lost ✗ (B). For AM the requirement is $1+\mu m>0$, not $m>0$: (C) is false, (D) is true. The paper's statements were phrased (i) "DSB-SC if $m>0$ is TRUE" and (ii) "AM only if $m>0$ is FALSE", key A;D.

An AM transmitter has an unmodulated carrier power of 100 W. It is modulated to $\mu=0.8$ by a single tone. The total transmitted power (watts) is ______.

$P_t=100(1+0.64/2)=132$ W. (Sidebands: 32 W, efficiency $0.64/2.64=24\%$.)

The antenna current of an AM transmitter is 10 A unmodulated and rises to $11.18$ A when modulated by a single tone. The modulation index is ______.

$I_t/I_c=\sqrt{1+\mu^2/2}=1.118\Rightarrow1+\mu^2/2=1.25\Rightarrow\mu^2=0.5$, $\mu=0.707$.

An AM wave is modulated simultaneously by two tones with $\mu_1=0.3$ and $\mu_2=0.4$. The effective modulation index is ______.

$\mu=\sqrt{0.09+0.16}=0.5$.

A DSB-SC receiver's local oscillator has a constant phase error of $60^\circ$. The recovered message power relative to the ideal-phase case is ______.

Amplitude $\propto\cos60^\circ=0.5$; power $\propto\cos^2=0.25$.

A 1 MHz AM signal is modulated by speech up to 5 kHz. For an envelope detector to work without ripple or diagonal clipping, a suitable $RC$ time constant is
  1. 10 ns
  2. 0.1 μs
  3. 20 μs
  4. 5 ms

Need $1/f_c=1\,\mu$s $\ll RC\ll1/W=200\,\mu$s. Only 20 μs fits. (10 ns / 0.1 μs follow the carrier; 5 ms cannot track the envelope.)

Telephone speech occupying 300–3400 Hz is sent as upper-sideband SSB on a 10 MHz carrier. The occupied band is
  1. 9.9966 – 10.0003 MHz
  2. 10.0003 – 10.0034 MHz
  3. 9.9997 – 10.0003 MHz
  4. 9.9966 – 10.0034 MHz

USB: $f_c+300\ \text{Hz}$ to $f_c+3400$ Hz. Bandwidth $3.1$ kHz $\approx W$.

A sinusoidal carrier is amplitude-modulated by a single sinusoid giving $S(t)=5\cos1600\pi t+20\cos1800\pi t+5\cos2000\pi t$. The modulation index is ______.

Carrier amplitude 20 (at 900 Hz), sidebands 5 each (at 800 and 1000 Hz): each sideband is $\mu A_c/2$, so $\mu=\frac{2\cdot5}{20}=\mathbf{0.5}$.

Consider the AM signal $S(t)=\cos2000\pi t+4\cos2400\pi t+\cos2800\pi t$. The ratio (three decimals) of the power in the sidebands (message-carrying part) to the carrier power is ______.

Carrier: $4\cos2400\pi t$, $P_c=\frac{16}2=8$. Sidebands: two terms of amplitude 1, total $2\cdot\frac12=1$. Ratio $=\frac18=\mathbf{0.125}$ (equal to $\mu^2/2$ with $\mu=\frac12$).

An AM transmitter has an unmodulated carrier power of 5 kW and can be modulated by a sinusoid up to a maximum of 50%. If the maximum modulation is reduced to 40% without overloading the transmitter (same maximum total power), the maximum unmodulated carrier power (kW) that can be used is ______.

Total power limit $=5(1+0.5^2/2)=5.625$ kW. With $\mu=0.4$: $P_c(1+0.08)=5.625\Rightarrow P_c=\mathbf{5.21}$ kW.

  3. 1
