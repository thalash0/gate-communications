History · the hiss that was the Big Bang

In 1964, two Bell Labs engineers, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, were trying to use a giant horn antenna in Holmdel, New Jersey, to listen for faint radio signals from space. They kept hearing a steady hiss, about $3.5\,\mathrm{K}$ worth of noise, in every direction, day and night. They checked the electronics. They evicted a family of pigeons from the horn and scrubbed out what they politely called "white dielectric material". The hiss stayed.

It turned out that the hiss was the afterglow of the Big Bang. They got a Nobel Prize for it in 1978. Lesson for a communication engineer: noise is not an inconvenience that sits on top of physics. It is physics, and every receiver ever built has to live with it. To live with it you need to talk about it, and to talk about noise you need probability.

Here is the whole plan of this book in one sentence: a transmitter sends one of a few possible waveforms; the channel adds random junk; the receiver has to guess which waveform was sent, and we want to know how often it will guess wrong. Everything in digital communication, from BER curves to error-correcting codes, is some version of that sentence. In analog communication the question is the same, only the guess is "what was the message waveform?" and the score is signal-to-noise ratio instead of error rate.

So before we touch a single antenna, we are going to rebuild exactly the probability you need. Not the whole subject, only the ten ideas GATE keeps coming back to. You studied all of this once. We are going to rebuild it so that it sticks.

Probability is area

Here is the picture I want you to carry for the whole chapter. Take a square of area 1. Every outcome of your experiment lives somewhere inside it, and the probability of an event is simply how much of the square it covers. Conditioning is "zoom in on one region of the square and renormalise so that region has area 1". Bayes' rule is nothing but a ratio of two areas.

Let's use it for the most basic communication problem. A transmitter sends a bit: $1$ with probability $\pi$, $0$ with probability $1-\pi$. The channel flips the bit with probability $\varepsilon$. You see a 1. What is the probability a 1 was actually sent?

Cut the square vertically by the prior $\pi$ (left = "1 was sent"). Cut each column by the channel: the bottom part of each column is where the receiver saw a 1. The posterior is the green area divided by (green + orange). Try $\pi=0.3,\ \varepsilon=0.1$: even a quite reliable channel gives only 79% confidence when the prior is lopsided.
Definition · conditional probability and Bayes $$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{\sum_i P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)}$$

For the bit example: $P(\text{sent }1\mid\text{got }1)=\dfrac{\pi(1-\varepsilon)}{\pi(1-\varepsilon)+(1-\pi)\varepsilon}$. This is exactly the "posterior" that a MAP receiver (Chapter 10) uses to decide.

Pause and ponder

The channel above is a "binary symmetric channel" with $\varepsilon=0.1$ and a fair prior $\pi=0.5$. You receive a 1. What is the posterior probability that a 1 was sent? Now what if $\pi=0.01$? Which bit does the receiver bet on, and does that depend on what it received?

Reveal

With $\pi=0.5$: $0.9$. With $\pi=0.01$: $\frac{0.01\cdot0.9}{0.01\cdot0.9+0.99\cdot0.1}=0.083$. Even after seeing a 1, a sender who almost never sends 1 is probably still sending 0. A good receiver bets on the prior as much as on the evidence, which is the whole philosophy of MAP detection.

Random variables: turning outcomes into numbers

A random variable $X$ is just a rule that attaches a number to every point of that probability square: the voltage at the output, the number of bit errors in a block, the noise sample at time $t_0$. We describe it with three equivalent objects.

  • CDF $F_X(x)=P(X\le x)$: a non-decreasing function from 0 to 1. It always exists.
  • pmf $p_X(x)=P(X=x)$ for discrete $X$ (a list of lumps of probability).
  • pdf $f_X(x)=\dfrac{dF_X}{dx}$ for continuous $X$. A pdf is a density, not a probability. Probability is area under it: $P(a

Two facts we use constantly: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X=1$ and $f_X(x)\ge0$. A pdf can exceed 1 (a very narrow bump), a probability never can.

Expectation, variance, and why mean-square is power

The expectation (mean) is the probability-weighted average, $$\E[X]=\int x f_X(x)\,dx\quad\text{or}\quad \sum_x x\,p_X(x),\qquad \E[g(X)]=\int g(x)f_X(x)\,dx .$$ Think of the pdf as a thin wire with mass density $f_X$: $\E[X]$ is the balance point. The variance is the spread, the moment of inertia about that balance point: $$\sigma_X^2=\Var(X)=\E[(X-\mu)^2]=\E[X^2]-\mu^2 .$$

In electronics $X$ is a voltage across $1\,\Omega$. Then $X^2$ is instantaneous power, $\E[X^2]$ is the average power (mean-square value), $\mu^2$ is the DC power, and $\sigma^2$ is the AC (fluctuating) power. This is why in the noise chapters "variance" and "power" will be used almost interchangeably for zero-mean signals.

Rules you will use 100 times
  • Linearity (always, no independence needed): $\E[aX+bY+c]=a\E[X]+b\E[Y]+c$.
  • $\Var(aX+b)=a^2\Var(X)$. Shifting does nothing, scaling squares.
  • If $X,Y$ are independent: $\Var(X+Y)=\Var(X)+\Var(Y)$ and $\E[XY]=\E[X]\E[Y]$.
  • In general $\Var(X+Y)=\Var X+\Var Y+2\Cov(X,Y)$.

The Gaussian: why nature loves it

History · de Moivre, Gauss, Laplace

In 1733 Abraham de Moivre noticed that coin-toss counts pile up in a bell shape. In 1801 the astronomer Piazzi lost the newly discovered dwarf planet Ceres behind the Sun; the 24-year-old Gauss recovered it using a method of fitting noisy observations (least squares) and, in 1809, argued that measurement errors follow the bell curve. In 1810 Laplace proved the general reason: add up lots of small independent effects and you get a bell, whatever the effects looked like individually. That is the central limit theorem.

Thermal noise in a resistor is produced by $\sim10^{10}$ electrons jostling independently. Each contributes a tiny random push; their sum is the noise voltage. By the CLT that sum is Gaussian, which is why AWGN is the default model for almost every communication channel. Watch the CLT happen: add $n$ random numbers drawn from a decidedly non-Gaussian source and standardise the sum.

Histogram of 6000 standardised sums of $n$ draws (bars) against the Gaussian bell (line). Even the lopsided exponential becomes bell-shaped by $n\approx 20$. At $n=1$ you see the raw shape of one draw.
The Gaussian, and the only table you need $$X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2):\quad f_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}.$$

Key properties: (i) a linear function of a Gaussian is Gaussian: $aX+b\sim\mathcal N(a\mu+b,a^2\sigma^2)$. (ii) A sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian with means and variances added. (iii) For jointly Gaussian variables, uncorrelated ⇒ independent (only for Gaussians!). (iv) Standardise with $Z=(X-\mu)/\sigma\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$.

The Q-function: the shape of every error probability

There is no closed form for $\int e^{-t^2/2}dt$, so we give the tail area a name. For the standard normal, $$Q(x)=P(Z>x)=\int_x^\infty\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^2/2}\,dt=\tfrac12\erfc\!\Big(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt2}\Big),\qquad Q(-x)=1-Q(x),\qquad Q(0)=\tfrac12 .$$ For a general $X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$: $\;P(X>a)=Q\!\big(\tfrac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\big)$. Every bit-error probability in this book will have the form $Q(\text{half the distance between the two signals}/\text{noise }\sigma)$. You are looking at the most important function of the syllabus.

Functions of a random variable

A signal goes through a rectifier, a clipper, a quantiser, a squarer. What does the pdf of the output look like? There are two clean ways to think.

Method 1 (CDF method, always works): write $F_Y(y)=P(g(X)\le y)$, express it as a region of $x$, integrate $f_X$ over it, differentiate.

Method 2 (slope formula): if $y=g(x)$ has roots $x_1,x_2,\dots$ for a given $y$, $$f_Y(y)=\sum_i\frac{f_X(x_i)}{|g'(x_i)|}.$$ Picture: a thin slice $dy$ of output collects the probability from each thin slice $dx_i=dy/|g'(x_i)|$ of input that maps into it. Where $g$ is steep, a lot of input is squeezed into a little output (high density in $x$-terms, but the slice is wide in $y$ so the density thins out).

And there is a third, GATE-favourite phenomenon: where $g$ is flat over an interval (a clipper, a quantiser, a comparator), a whole interval of inputs collapses to one output value, which then carries a finite chunk of probability. The pdf of $Y$ then contains impulses $\delta(y-y_0)$ whose weight is that chunk. Play with it:

Input $X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ (top). Choose a transformation $Y=g(X)$ and compare the histogram of $Y$ (bars) with theory (line). Red arrows are point masses: their height is the probability carried by that one value.
Worked example · hard limiter

A clipper outputs $Y=-1$ for $X<-1$, $Y=X$ for $|X|\le1$, $Y=+1$ for $X>1$, with $X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. Then $P(Y=1)=P(X>1)=Q(1)=0.1587$, likewise for $Y=-1$, and in between $f_Y(y)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-y^2/2}$. Check: $0.1587\times2+\int_{-1}^1\!\varphi=0.317+0.683=1$ ✓.
So $f_Y(y)=0.1587\,\delta(y+1)+0.1587\,\delta(y-1)+\varphi(y)\,[|y|<1]$.

Two random variables: correlation and independence

The joint behaviour of $X$ and $Y$ lives in a joint pdf $f_{XY}(x,y)$, a bump over the plane. The two numbers you need: $$\Cov(X,Y)=\E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]=\E[XY]-\mu_X\mu_Y,\qquad \rho=\frac{\Cov(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}\in[-1,1].$$ $\rho=\pm1$ means $Y$ is an exact linear function of $X$; $\rho=0$ means "no linear relationship".

Independent means $f_{XY}=f_Xf_Y$ (the bump factorises). Independent ⇒ uncorrelated. The converse is false: let $X$ be symmetric about zero and $Y=X^2$. $Y$ is fully determined by $X$, yet $\E[XY]=\E[X^3]=0$ so $\rho=0$. Look at both situations:

Each dot is one realisation of $(X,Y)$. The correlation coefficient measures only the straight-line tendency. The parabola and circle datasets are perfectly dependent and yet have $\rho\approx0$.

The bivariate Gaussian is the pleasant exception, and its picture is worth seeing in 3D. The hill is round when $\rho=0$, and stretches into a ridge along the diagonal as $|\rho|\to1$. For Gaussians, and only Gaussians, $\rho=0$ makes the hill round (factorised) and therefore independent.

Joint pdf of two unit-variance Gaussians. Drag to rotate. As $\rho\to1$ all the probability piles onto the line $y=x$.
Worked example · why GATE 2026 Q.38 is easy

$X\sim U(-1,1)$ and $N\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ independent, $Y=X+N$, $Z=X^2+N$. Find the correlation (i.e. $\E[XY]$ and $\E[XZ]$ since means are zero). $\E[XY]=\E[X^2]+\E[XN]=\tfrac13+0$ since $\Var X=\frac{(2)^2}{12}=\frac13$. $\E[XZ]=\E[X^3]+\E[XN]=0+0$ because $X^3$ is an odd function of a symmetric variable. Answer $\tfrac13$ and $0$: $Z$ is strongly dependent on $X$ yet uncorrelated with it. Same lesson as the parabola above.

Sums of independent variables

The received sample is "signal + noise" and the noise is a sum of lots of things. Facts: means add, variances add (for independent terms), and the pdf of the sum is the convolution of the individual pdfs, $f_{X+Y}=f_X*f_Y$. (This will resurface in Fourier land as "convolution becomes multiplication": the characteristic function of a sum is a product.) If both are Gaussian, the result is Gaussian: $\mathcal N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)+\mathcal N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\to\mathcal N(\mu_1+\mu_2,\ \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$.

Note the plumbing consequence for receivers: doubling the number of independent noisy samples doubles the noise variance (power) but only $\sqrt2\times$ the noise amplitude, while the signal parts add coherently and double. That $\sqrt2$ is the root of "coherent combining / matched filtering buys SNR".

Let $X$ be the number of tosses of a fair coin needed until two consecutive heads appear for the first time. $\E[X]$ is ______.

Let $E_0$ be the expected number of further tosses when the last toss was not a head (or at the start) and $E_1$ when the last toss was a head. $E_0=1+\tfrac12E_1+\tfrac12E_0$ and $E_1=1+\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12E_0$. A binary symmetric channel has crossover probability $1/7$. The input is 1 with probability 0.8 and 0 with probability 0.2. Given that the received bit is $Y=0$, the probability that a 1 was transmitted is ______.

$P(Y=0)=0.2\cdot\frac67+0.8\cdot\frac17=\frac{2.0}{7}$. $P(X=1\mid Y=0)=\dfrac{0.8/7}{2.0/7}=\mathbf{0.4}$. A prior of 0.8 for a 1 becomes only 0.4 after seeing a 0: Bayes in action (cf. the probability square at the start of the chapter).

  • pdf is density, probability is area; $\E[X^2]$ is average power, $\sigma^2$ is AC power.
