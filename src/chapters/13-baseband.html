History · a cable, a mirror and the 1928 paper

In 1858 the first transatlantic telegraph cable arrived at its destination as a slow, smeared blob (Chapter 1): its resistance and capacitance made it a giant low-pass filter, so each dot spread into the next. The electrician Whitehouse fried the cable with 2000 V; William Thomson’s answer was less voltage, a sensitive detector (his mirror galvanometer) and slower signalling. In effect he had discovered inter-symbol interference.

The mathematics of how fast one can signal over a band-limited channel without the symbols overlapping came from Harry Nyquist in 1928 (Certain topics in telegraph transmission theory). His two answers (a limit of $2B$ pulses per second, and a family of pulse shapes that attains it with zero interference at the sampling instants) are the backbone of every modem, every Ethernet link, every DSL line. Nyquist worked from telegraph experience; the generalisation to Gaussian noise (the matched filter of the next chapter) came 15 years later.

Real world · which line code is in your wire?

10BASE-T Ethernet: Manchester (self-clocking, no DC, needs 20 MHz for 10 Mbit/s). 100BASE-TX: 4B5B + MLT-3. USB: NRZI with bit stuffing. T1 lines: AMI with B8ZS; E1 lines (India’s telephone trunks): HDB3. Magnetic tape/disk: run-length-limited codes. Gigabit Ethernet over copper: 5-level PAM with heavy equalisation and error coding. PCIe 3.0: 128b/130b with scrambling; USB 3.0: 8b/10b. Every one of these is a trade-off among bandwidth, DC content, timing information and cost.

From bits to pulses

A digital baseband signal is a train of pulses whose amplitudes carry the data: $$s(t)=\sum_{k}a_k\,p(t-kT),$$ where $a_k$ is the $k$-th symbol amplitude, $p(t)$ the pulse shape, $T$ the symbol interval. The symbol rate is $R_s=1/T$ (baud, after Baudot, Chapter 9). With $M$ amplitude levels, each symbol carries $\log_2M$ bits: $R_b=R_s\log_2M$. In the PSD formula derived in Chapter 5, with symbols of mean $\mu_a$ and variance $\sigma_a^2$ (uncorrelated), $$S_s(f)=\frac{\sigma_a^2}{T}|P(f)|^2+\frac{\mu_a^2}{T^2}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\Big|P\!\Big(\frac kT\Big)\Big|^2\delta\!\Big(f-\frac kT\Big).$$ The continuous part comes from the randomness, the impulses from any non-zero mean (DC and clock lines). For correlated symbols (AMI) replace $\sigma_a^2$ by the transform $S_b(f)=\sum_mR_b[m]e^{-j2\pi fmT}$ of the symbol autocorrelation.

Line codes: choosing the shape of the bit

What properties do we want from a line code? (1) No DC (transformers and AC-coupled cables block it, and DC wander ruins thresholds). (2) Self-clocking: enough transitions that the receiver can recover timing (Chapter 16). (3) Narrow bandwidth. (4) Some built-in error detection. (5) Transparency (works for any bit pattern). No code wins on all.

Derivation · the AMI and Manchester spectra from $S_b(f)$ and $|G(f)|^2$

AMI. Equiprobable independent bits: a “1” (probability $\frac12$) is $\pm A$ with alternating sign. Then $R_b[0]=\frac12A^2$. For adjacent symbols: $a_ka_{k+1}$ is nonzero only if both are 1s (probability $\frac14$), and then their signs are opposite, so $R_b[\pm1]=-\frac14A^2$; for $|m|\ge2$ the alternation makes the correlation average to zero. Hence $S_b(f)=\frac{A^2}2-\frac{A^2}{4}\cdot2\cos2\pi fT=\frac{A^2}2(1-\cos2\pi fT)=A^2\sin^2\pi fT$. With a rectangular pulse of width $T$, $|G|^2=T^2\sinc^2(fT)$: $S=\frac{|G|^2}TS_b=A^2T\sinc^2(fT)\sin^2(\pi fT)$ ✓. The factor $\sin^2\pi fT$ vanishes at $f=0$: alternate inversion kills the DC (the running digital sum stays bounded). Manchester. Each bit is a pulse pair $\pm A$ (first half) and $\mp A$ (second half); the pulse $g(t)=A\,\mathrm{rect}\big(\frac{t+T/4}{T/2}\big)-A\,\mathrm{rect}\big(\frac{t-T/4}{T/2}\big)$ has $|G(f)|^2=A^2T^2\sinc^2\!\frac{fT}2\sin^2\!\frac{\pi fT}2$; symbols $\pm1$ are independent, so $S_b=1$ and $S=A^2T\sinc^2\frac{fT}2\sin^2\frac{\pi fT}2$ ✓. It vanishes at DC because every bit has zero net area.

Traps
  • “No DC component” (no impulse at $f=0$, i.e. zero mean) is not the same as “PSD is zero at DC”. Polar NRZ has zero mean but $S(0)=A^2T>0$. Only AMI and Manchester have $S(0)=0$.
  • Unipolar NRZ PSD has an impulse at 0 and at no other multiples of $1/T$ (since $\sinc(k)=0$): only the DC line survives. Unipolar RZ also has impulses at $\pm1/T,\pm3/T,\ldots$: a clock line, which is why RZ gives timing recovery easily.
  • Bandwidth is quoted as the first null of the PSD (main lobe) unless stated otherwise.

Keeping the long runs of zeros and ones in check

AMI solves DC but a long string of zeros gives no transitions: the receiver’s clock drifts. Two fixes:

  • Zero substitution (bipolar with $n$-zero substitution): T1’s B8ZS replaces eight consecutive zeros by $000VB0VB$, where $V$ is a deliberate bipolar violation (same polarity as the previous pulse) and $B$ a normal pulse of opposite polarity; E1’s HDB3 replaces four zeros by $000V$ or $B00V$, choosing so that successive violations alternate in polarity (keeping the DC null). The receiver recognises the violations and puts the zeros back: transparent coding with at most 3 (HDB3) or 7 (B8ZS) consecutive zeros on the line.
  • Block codes and scramblers. 4B5B (Fast Ethernet): each 4-bit nibble is mapped to a 5-bit code word chosen so that no word has more than three consecutive zeros; overhead 25%. 8B10B (Gigabit Ethernet, SATA, PCIe 1–2, Fibre Channel): 8 bits → 10 with running disparity control: each 10-bit word has 4–6 ones, and the encoder picks between two forms to keep the long-run DC balance; run length ≤ 5; overhead 25%. 64B/66B and 128B/130B (10G Ethernet, PCIe 3+) add only 3% overhead and rely on a scrambler: the data is XORed with a long pseudo-random sequence from a linear-feedback shift register (e.g. $x^{58}+x^{39}+1$), which statistically gives enough transitions and a flat spectrum; the receiver’s self-synchronising descrambler is the exact inverse.
Engineering practice · differential encoding: NRZI and polarity ambiguity

NRZI (used by USB) sends a transition for a “0” and no transition for a “1” (or the reverse), i.e. the data is carried by changes: $w_k=w_{k-1}\oplus b_k$. Then the receiver does not need to know the absolute polarity (twisted pair wired backwards still works). The same differential trick in the modulator is DPSK (Chapter 15). The price is that a single bit error causes two data errors.

Inter-symbol interference

Send the pulse train through a channel with impulse response $h(t)$ and receive filter; the overall pulse becomes $g(t)=p*h*h_r$. At the receiver we sample at $t=mT$ (plus a delay): $$y(mT)=a_m\,g(0)\;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{k\ne m}a_k\,g\big((m-k)T\big)}_{\textstyle\text{inter-symbol interference}}\;+\;n(mT).$$ The first term is what we want; the second is leftover tails of neighbouring symbols. A band-limited channel necessarily has a spread-out $g(t)$, so ISI is the rule, not the exception. Peak distortion is the worst-case ISI normalised to the signal: $D=\dfrac{\sum_{k\ne0}|g(kT)|}{g(0)}$, so with antipodal $\pm A$ symbols the worst-case eye opening at the sampler is $2A\,g(0)(1-D)$, and the noise margin shrinks by the factor $1-D$ (a 3 dB loss when $D=0.3$). The question: can we shape $g(t)$ so that the tails vanish exactly at the other sampling instants?

Nyquist’s first criterion for zero ISI

Derivation · zero ISI in time and in frequency

In time: $g(nT)=\begin{cases}1&n=0\\0&n\ne0\end{cases}$: the pulse passes through zero at every other sampling instant. In frequency: multiply $g(t)$ by the impulse train $\sum_n\delta(t-nT)$ (sampling): the result is $\sum_ng(nT)\delta(t-nT)=\delta(t)$ iff the condition above holds. Transform both sides, using the sampling result of Chapter 3 ($\sum_n g(nT)\delta(t-nT)\leftrightarrow\frac1T\sum_mG(f-\frac mT)$) and $\delta(t)\leftrightarrow1$: $$\boxed{\ \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}G\!\Big(f-\frac mT\Big)=T\quad\text{for all }f\ }$$ the shifted copies of $G(f)$ add up to a flat line.

Consequence 1 (minimum bandwidth): the flat sum can be achieved with $G(f)=T\,\rect(fT)$, a brick of width $1/T$, whose pulse is $g(t)=\sinc(t/T)$ (zero at all nonzero multiples of $T$). If $G(f)$ were band-limited to less than $\frac1{2T}$ the copies could not overlap to fill the gaps: so the minimum bandwidth is $B_{\min}=\dfrac1{2T}=\dfrac{R_s}2$, i.e. at most $2B$ symbols/s through a channel of bandwidth $B$ (the Nyquist rate: GATE 2026 Q.22). The ideal pulse is unrealisable (non-causal brick wall) and its $1/t$ tails make it hypersensitive to timing error: a slight sampling jitter $\delta$ adds the alternating, slowly-converging sum $\sum_k(-1)^k a_k\delta/(kT)$, which diverges logarithmically in the worst case.

The raised-cosine compromise

Use a smoother edge. The raised-cosine spectrum with roll-off $\alpha\in[0,1]$: $$G(f)=\begin{cases}T&|f|\le\frac{1-\alpha}{2T}\\[1mm]\frac T2\Big[1+\cos\frac{\pi T}{\alpha}\big(|f|-\frac{1-\alpha}{2T}\big)\Big]&\frac{1-\alpha}{2T}<|f|\le\frac{1+\alpha}{2T}\\[1mm]0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$ $$g(t)=\sinc\!\Big(\frac tT\Big)\frac{\cos(\pi\alpha t/T)}{1-4\alpha^2t^2/T^2}.$$ The edge is a half-cosine that is odd-symmetric about the Nyquist frequency $\frac1{2T}$: if $f_N=\frac1{2T}$ then $G(f_N+x)+G(f_N-x)=T$, because $\cos$ of the two arguments are negatives of each other ($\cos\theta+\cos(\pi-\theta)=0$), which is exactly what the flat-sum condition requires. Bandwidth (baseband): $B=\dfrac{1+\alpha}{2T}=\dfrac{(1+\alpha)R_s}2$; for a bandpass (modulated) signal the double-sided bandwidth is $(1+\alpha)R_s$. The tails fall like $1/t^3$ instead of $1/t$: far less jitter sensitivity. $\alpha=0$ is the brick; $\alpha=1$ doubles the bandwidth ($B=1/T$). The excess bandwidth is $100\alpha\%$.

Bottom: $G(f)$ and its two neighbouring copies (faint); their sum (thick yellow) is perfectly flat. Larger $\alpha$ means a wider spectrum and faster-decaying tails.
Spectral efficiency of Nyquist signalling

Baseband $M$-level PAM at the zero-ISI symbol rate: $R_s=\dfrac{2B}{1+\alpha}$, $R_b=R_s\log_2M$, so $\eta=\dfrac{R_b}B=\dfrac{2\log_2M}{1+\alpha}$ bit/s/Hz. Passband (PSK/QAM with bandwidth $B=(1+\alpha)R_s$): $\eta=\dfrac{\log_2M}{1+\alpha}$. Examples: 64-QAM with $\alpha=0.35$: $\frac6{1.35}=4.44$ bit/s/Hz; QPSK with $\alpha=0.35$ (DVB-S): $1.48$ bit/s/Hz; the 3G WCDMA chip pulse uses $\alpha=0.22$, LTE and Wi-Fi use windowed OFDM instead (Chapter 20).

Worked examples

Rate and bandwidth. A channel of bandwidth 4 kHz is used with raised-cosine pulses, $\alpha=0.5$: $R_s=\frac{2B}{1+\alpha}=5333$ symbols/s. With 16-level PAM ($4$ bits/symbol): $R_b=21.3$ kbit/s.

GATE 2014 Set 4 Q.51. M-ary PSK carries 200 kbit/s through a band-pass channel of 100 kHz with a raised-cosine spectrum of 100% excess bandwidth ($\alpha=1$). Passband bandwidth $=(1+\alpha)R_s=2R_s=100$ kHz ⇒ $R_s=50$ ksym/s ⇒ $\log_2M=\frac{200}{50}=4$ ⇒ $M=\mathbf{16}$.

GATE 2026 Q.22. A source emits $10^4$ symbols/s using four levels $(-1,0,1,2)$. The Nyquist (minimum) bandwidth is $R_s/2=\mathbf5$ kHz: the number of levels is irrelevant for the symbol rate. GATE 2026 Q.32. 16-QAM at 4 Mbit/s: $R_s=\frac4{\log_216}=1$ Msym/s; a QAM signal’s minimum (Nyquist) double-sided bandwidth is $R_s=\mathbf1$ MHz.

Root-raised cosine: splitting the filter between transmitter and receiver

The end-to-end response $G(f)=P(f)H_c(f)H_r(f)$ must be Nyquist. In AWGN, the receiver filter should also be matched to the transmitted pulse (Chapter 14), $H_r=P^*$. For an ideal flat channel, then, $G=|P|^2$, so $$|P(f)|=\sqrt{G_{RC}(f)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{each end uses a root-raised-cosine (RRC) filter.}$$ The transmitted pulse is then the RRC, which has a slightly larger peak-to-average ratio than RC (its tails overshoot) but a transmit spectrum that decays quickly, and the matched-filter output is exactly raised-cosine: ISI-free at the sampling instants and with the noise samples uncorrelated (because the RRC response is orthogonal to its $T$-shifts). That is the arrangement of every modern modem: RRC at the transmitter, RRC at the receiver, symbol-rate sampling after it.

Engineering practice · implementing a pulse-shaping filter

A digital transmitter upsamples the symbol stream by $L=4$–$8$ (inserting zeros) and filters it with a finite-impulse-response (FIR) approximation of the RRC, typically spanning $\pm4$ to $\pm8$ symbols (e.g. 33–65 taps at 4 samples/symbol), then a DAC and analog reconstruction filter. The truncation of the infinite tails sets the out-of-band emission floor (typically $-40$ to $-60$ dBc), controlled by the window and the length: spectral masks of the regulator (e.g. the DVB-S or Wi-Fi mask) directly determine the number of taps. Increasing $\alpha$ makes the filter shorter and the spectrum wider; a typical compromise is $\alpha=0.2$–$0.35$. In optical, microwave and satellite links where the power amplifier is non-linear, spectral regrowth restores the sidelobes that the filter suppressed: this is why constant-envelope schemes (MSK, GMSK, Chapter 15) exist.

Partial-response signalling: duobinary

Nyquist’s criterion demands zero ISI. Partial response (correlative coding, Lender 1963; Kretzmer 1966) turns the idea around: allow a controlled, known amount of ISI that the receiver can remove, and in return achieve the minimum bandwidth $R_s/2$ with a realisable, fast-decaying pulse.

Derivation · duobinary

Use $g(t)=\sinc\!\big(\frac tT\big)+\sinc\!\big(\frac{t-T}T\big)$: nonzero only at $t=0$ and $t=T$ (value 1 each) at the sampling instants. Its spectrum is $G(f)=T\rect(fT)\,[1+e^{-j2\pi fT}]=2T\cos(\pi fT)e^{-j\pi fT}\rect(fT)$: a smooth cosine roll-off to zero at $f=\frac1{2T}$, bandwidth exactly $R_s/2$, and far less sensitive to timing than the sinc (tails $\propto1/t^2$). With binary $a_k=\pm1$ the samples are $y_k=a_k+a_{k-1}\in\{-2,0,+2\}$: three levels. To avoid error propagation (decoding $a_k=y_k-a_{k-1}$ would carry any mistake forward), precode: $w_k=b_k\oplus w_{k-1}$, $a_k=2w_k-1$. Then $y_k=0$ exactly when $w_k\ne w_{k-1}$, i.e. when $b_k=1$, and $|y_k|=2$ when $b_k=0$, so the decision rule is memoryless: $$\hat b_k=\begin{cases}1&y_k=0\\0&|y_k|=2.\end{cases}$$ Example: $b=1,0,1,1,0$ with $w_{-1}=0$ ($a_{-1}=-1$): $w=1,1,0,1,1$; $a=+,+,-,+,+$; $y=0,+2,0,0,+2$; decoded $1,0,1,1,0$ ✓. The modified duobinary $y_k=a_k-a_{k-2}$ (class IV, response $1-D^2$) also has a null at DC: it is the basis of the PRML read channels of every hard disk and of 1000BASE-T.

The price

Three levels instead of two: the distance between adjacent levels is $A$ rather than $2A$ (with the same peak amplitude), so noise immunity is 3 dB worse in amplitude terms (and the error rate is higher), but this is more than recovered by the savings in bandwidth when the channel is the limiting factor.

Seeing ISI: the eye diagram

Put a random bit stream through the system, then overlay every symbol-length-two segment on an oscilloscope, triggering on the symbol clock. The traces form an “eye”. The vertical opening at the sampling instant is the margin against noise; the horizontal opening is the tolerance to timing error; the thickness of the traces at the crossing is the jitter. ISI closes the eye; noise blurs it. For an $M$-level system there are $M-1$ stacked eyes.

Equalisation: undoing the channel

If the channel is not ideal, a transversal (FIR) equaliser $c_0,\dots,c_{N-1}$ can approximately undo it. The equalised response is $y[n]=\sum_kc_kp[n-k]$ where $p[n]$ are the samples of the channel pulse.

Derivation · zero-forcing equaliser and noise enhancement

Zero forcing. Choose $C(z)=1/P(z)$ so that $Y(z)=1$: all ISI cancelled (Nyquist’s condition imposed by brute force). For $P(z)=1+az^{-1}$ ($|a|<1$): $C(z)=\sum_k(-a)^kz^{-k}$, an infinite FIR with taps $c_k=(-a)^k$. Cost. The noise passes through the equaliser too, and its power is multiplied by $\sum_kc_k^2=\dfrac1{1-a^2}$ (noise enhancement): for $a=0.5$: $1.33$ ($1.25$ dB); $a=0.9$: $5.26$ ($7.2$ dB); as $a\to1$ (a spectral null at $f=\frac1{2T}$ where $|P|\to0$) it diverges. The equaliser is inverting the channel exactly where the channel has the least signal, boosting noise there. Truncating to $N$ taps leaves one residual tail of size $|a|^N$ and the noise gain $\frac{1-a^{2N}}{1-a^2}$.

MMSE (Wiener) equaliser. Minimise $\E|y_k-a_k|^2$ (ISI plus noise together). In the frequency domain, for a channel $P(f)$, white noise $N_0/2$ and unit-power symbols, the optimum is $$C_{\rm MMSE}(f)=\frac{P^*(f)}{|P(f)|^2+1/\mathrm{SNR}},$$ a regularised inverse: where $|P|^2\gg1/\mathrm{SNR}$ it is the zero-forcing inverse $1/P$, where $|P|^2\ll1/\mathrm{SNR}$ it rolls off to $\mathrm{SNR}\cdot P^*$ (it gives up on frequencies where the channel is buried in noise). This is the Wiener filter of Chapter 5 with $S_s=1$ and $S_n=1/\mathrm{SNR}$. At high SNR it equals ZF.

Zero-forcing equaliser for the two-path channel $P(z)=1+az^{-1}$ with $N$ taps. Adaptation: the LMS algorithm

The channel is unknown and varies. Let the equaliser input vector be $\mathbf x_k$ (the last $N$ received samples), output $y_k=\mathbf c^T\mathbf x_k$, and error $e_k=d_k-y_k$ where $d_k$ is a known training symbol (or the receiver’s own decision, “decision-directed”). The stochastic-gradient descent on $\E e_k^2$ is the least-mean-square (LMS) algorithm $$\mathbf c_{k+1}=\mathbf c_k+\mu\,e_k\,\mathbf x_k,$$ using the instantaneous estimate $-2e_k\mathbf x_k$ of the gradient. It converges in the mean if $0<\mu<\dfrac2{\lambda_{\max}}$ ($\lambda_{\max}$ the largest eigenvalue of the input autocorrelation matrix; since $\lambda_{\max}\le\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf R=N\sigma_x^2$, $\mu<\frac2{N\sigma_x^2}$ is a safe bound). Small $\mu$ gives slow, accurate adaptation, large $\mu$ fast and noisy: the same bandwidth–variance trade-off as in a PLL (Chapter 7). A decision-feedback equaliser (DFE) adds a feedback filter that subtracts the post-cursor ISI of already-decided symbols: it removes the tail without amplifying noise (the feedback uses clean decisions), at the risk of error propagation when a decision is wrong. The optimum, maximum-likelihood sequence estimation by the Viterbi algorithm (Chapter 18), searches the sequences of symbols directly.

Engineering practice · where equalisers live

The dial-up modem handshake’s hissing is the equaliser training (a known sequence lets $\mathbf c$ converge in a second or two). GSM burst: a 26-bit training sequence in the middle of every 148-bit burst lets the receiver estimate a channel with echoes of up to about 16 µs (some 4–5 symbol periods) and run a Viterbi equaliser. Ethernet over copper (1000BASE-T and 10GBASE-T) use five-level PAM, echo cancellers and DFEs per pair. Disk drives use PRML. High-speed serial links (PCIe 5/6, 56–112 Gb/s SerDes) combine a transmit FIR (pre-emphasis), a receiver continuous-time linear equaliser (CTLE) and a DFE, all adapted at power-up (“link training”). Wi-Fi and LTE avoid the problem by using OFDM (Chapter 20): the equaliser becomes one complex multiplication per sub-carrier.

Practice

Set A · Concept checks

Which line codes have zero power spectral density at $f=0$ (for equiprobable independent bits)?
  1. Polar NRZ
  2. Manchester
  3. Unipolar NRZ
  4. Bipolar AMI

Manchester: $\sin^2(\pi fT/2)$ factor; AMI: $\sin^2(\pi fT)$ factor. Polar NRZ has $S(0)=A^2T$; unipolar NRZ has an impulse at DC.

For zero inter-symbol interference with symbol interval $T$, the overall pulse spectrum $G(f)$ must satisfy
  1. $\sum_{m}G\!\left(f-\tfrac mT\right)=T$ (constant)
  2. $G(f)=T\rect(fT)$ only
  3. $\sum_m G(f-mT)=1/T$
  4. $G(f)$ must be zero for $|f|>\frac1{2T}$

(A): the periodised spectrum must be flat. (B) is just one solution (the minimum-bandwidth one). (D) is not required (raised-cosine violates it).

The main advantage of duobinary signalling over ideal Nyquist (sinc) signalling at the same symbol rate is
  1. a larger noise margin
  2. a smaller bandwidth than $R_s/2$
  3. a realisable pulse with a smooth roll-off and tolerance to timing error, at the minimum bandwidth $R_s/2$
  4. no need for a precoder

Duobinary needs bandwidth $R_s/2$ (not less), has 3 levels (worse noise margin) and needs a precoder to avoid error propagation. Its pulse decays as $1/t^2$ and its spectrum is a smooth cosine. (C).

$D=(0.1+0.2)/1=0.3$; opening $=1-D=\mathbf{0.7}$ (a $3.1$ dB loss of noise margin).

A channel has sampled pulse response $p(0)=1$, $p(1)=0.5$ (zero elsewhere). A two-tap zero-forcing equaliser is designed so that $y(0)=1$, $y(1)=0$. The residual ISI $y(2)$ is ______.

$c_0=1$, $c_1=-0.5$, $y(2)=c_1p(1)=-0.25$. (Zero-forcing tap-by-tap: $c_k=(-a)^k$ for the channel $1+az^{-1}$, $a=0.5$.)

The channel $P(z)=1+0.9z^{-1}$ is equalised by an (infinitely long) zero-forcing filter. The noise enhancement in dB is ______.

$\sum c_k^2=\frac1{1-0.81}=5.26\to\mathbf{7.2}$ dB.

A binary $\pm1$ sequence is sent through duobinary signalling ($y_k=a_k+a_{k-1}$). The number of distinct noiseless levels of $y_k$ is ______.

$y_k\in\{-2,0,+2\}$: 3 levels.

An LMS equaliser with $N=10$ taps has input power $\sigma_x^2=0.5$. The upper bound on the step size $\mu$ (using $\lambda_{\max}\le N\sigma_x^2$) for convergence in the mean is ______.

$\mu<\dfrac2{N\sigma_x^2}=\dfrac2{10\times0.5}=\mathbf{0.4}$.

Set C · Exam-style problems

A discrete memoryless source emits $10^4$ symbols per second from a four-symbol alphabet, represented by a multi-level signal taking values $(-1,0,+1,+2)$. The equivalent Nyquist bandwidth of the signal is
  1. 10 kHz
  2. 64 kHz
  3. 5 kHz
  4. 20 kHz

$R_s/2=5$ kHz.

A wireless link uses 16-QAM with an ML receiver over AWGN. The information rate is $4\times10^6$ bit/s. The minimum transmission (Nyquist) bandwidth of the modulated signal in MHz is ______.

$R_s=4\text{ M}/4=1$ Msym/s. Minimum passband bandwidth for QAM $=R_s=1$ MHz (key 0.95–1.05).

An M-level PSK modulation scheme is used to transmit independent binary digits over a band-pass channel with bandwidth 100 kHz. The bit rate is 200 kbps and the system characteristic is a raised-cosine spectrum with 100% excess bandwidth. The minimum value of $M$ is ______.

$(1+\alpha)R_s=100$ kHz with $\alpha=1$: $R_s=50$ k; $R_b/R_s=4$ bits ⇒ $M=16$.

A speech signal is sampled at 8 kHz and encoded with 8 bits per sample. The PCM bit stream is sent over a baseband channel using 4-level PAM. The minimum bandwidth (kHz) required is ______.

$R_b=64$ kbit/s; 4-level PAM carries 2 bits/symbol: $R_s=32$ ksym/s; Nyquist minimum bandwidth $R_s/2=\mathbf{16}$ kHz.

Symbols are transmitted at 2000 symbols/s. The overall pulse spectrum $P(f)$ at the sampler input is one of the four shapes below (all of unit height at $f=0$). Which one gives zero inter-symbol interference?
  1. rectangle to 1.2 kHz
  2. trapezoid: flat to 0.8 kHz, linear to 0 at 1.2 kHz
  3. flat to 1 kHz, then linear to 0 at 1.2 kHz
  4. triangle to 1.2 kHz

Nyquist: $\sum_kP(f-kR_s)=$ const with $R_s=2$ kHz; the roll-off must be odd-symmetric about $R_s/2=1$ kHz. Only (B) (a trapezoid centred on the Nyquist frequency, i.e. a linear-roll-off Nyquist pulse) satisfies this; the shape (C) rolls off entirely above 1 kHz and leaves a dip where the shifted copies overlap.
