History · the hiss that was the Big Bang

In 1964, two Bell Labs engineers, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, were trying to use a giant horn antenna in Holmdel, New Jersey, to listen for faint radio signals from space. They kept hearing a steady hiss, about $3.5\,\mathrm{K}$ worth of noise, in every direction, day and night. They checked the electronics. They evicted a family of pigeons from the horn and scrubbed out what they politely called “white dielectric material”. The hiss stayed.

It turned out that the hiss was the afterglow of the Big Bang. They got a Nobel Prize for it in 1978. Lesson for a communication engineer: noise is not an inconvenience that sits on top of physics. It is physics, and every receiver ever built has to live with it. To live with it you need to talk about it, and to talk about noise you need probability.

Here is the whole plan of this book in one sentence: a transmitter sends one of a few possible waveforms; the channel adds random junk; the receiver has to guess which waveform was sent, and we want to know how often it will guess wrong. Everything in digital communication, from BER curves to error-correcting codes, is some version of that sentence. In analog communication the question is the same, only the guess is “what was the message waveform?” and the score is signal-to-noise ratio instead of error rate.

So before we touch a single antenna we rebuild exactly the probability you need: the ideas that the rest of the book keeps coming back to, each with its derivation so that nothing is a formula from nowhere.

Probability is area

Take a square of area 1. Every outcome of your experiment lives somewhere inside it, and the probability of an event is how much of the square it covers. Formally, a probability model has a sample space $\Omega$ (all outcomes), events (subsets), and a measure $P$ satisfying Kolmogorov’s axioms: $P(A)\ge0$; $P(\Omega)=1$; and $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)$ for disjoint $A,B$. Everything else follows. For instance $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$ (don’t count the overlap twice), $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$.

Conditioning is “zoom in on one region of the square and renormalise so that region has area 1”. Independence means knowing $B$ does not change the area-fraction of $A$: $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$. Let’s use these for the most basic communication problem. A transmitter sends a bit: $1$ with probability $\pi$, $0$ with probability $1-\pi$. The channel flips the bit with probability $\varepsilon$. You see a 1. What is the probability a 1 was actually sent?

Cut the square vertically by the prior $\pi$ (left = “1 was sent”). Cut each column by the channel: the bottom part of each column is where the receiver saw a 1. The posterior is the green area divided by (green + orange). Try $\pi=0.3,\ \varepsilon=0.1$: even a quite reliable channel gives only 79% confidence when the prior is lopsided.
Derivation · total probability and Bayes’ rule

Definition: $P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}$, so $P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)P(B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)$. Dividing the second equality by $P(B)$ gives Bayes. If $A_1,\dots,A_M$ partition $\Omega$ (exactly one occurs), the denominator expands by the law of total probability $P(B)=\sum_iP(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)$: $$P(A_j\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A_j)\,P(A_j)}{\sum_iP(B\mid A_i)\,P(A_i)}.$$ For the bit example: $P(\text{sent }1\mid\text{got }1)=\dfrac{\pi(1-\varepsilon)}{\pi(1-\varepsilon)+(1-\pi)\varepsilon}$. In the language of detection (Chapter 14): $P(A_j)$ is the prior, $P(B\mid A_j)$ the likelihood, $P(A_j\mid B)$ the posterior, and the receiver that picks the largest posterior is the MAP receiver, which minimises the probability of error.

Pause and ponder

The channel above is a “binary symmetric channel” with $\varepsilon=0.1$ and a fair prior $\pi=0.5$. You receive a 1. What is the posterior probability that a 1 was sent? Now what if $\pi=0.01$? Which bit does the receiver bet on, and does that depend on what it received?

Reveal

With $\pi=0.5$: $0.9$. With $\pi=0.01$: $\frac{0.01\cdot0.9}{0.01\cdot0.9+0.99\cdot0.1}=0.083$. Even after seeing a 1, a sender who almost never sends 1 is probably still sending 0. A good receiver bets on the prior as much as on the evidence, which is the whole philosophy of MAP detection. The same logic explains why a “99% accurate” test for a rare disease (or a rare intrusion, or a rare radar target) produces mostly false alarms.

Engineering practice · the base-rate trap in alarms and detectors

A radar or an intrusion detector with false-alarm probability $10^{-3}$ per decision sounds excellent. If it makes $10^{6}$ decisions per second and genuine targets appear in one decision in $10^{7}$, then per second it raises $10^3$ false alarms against $0.1$ true detections: the posterior probability that an alarm is real is about $10^{-4}$. Detection systems are therefore designed on prior-aware thresholds, with false-alarm rates of $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-12}$, which is why the section on detection at the end of this chapter, and Chapter 14, spend so long on tails of the Gaussian.

Counting: where the binomial coefficient comes from

The number of ways to choose which $k$ of $n$ bits are in error is $\binom nk=\dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$ (order the $n$ positions in $n!$ ways; the $k!$ orderings of the chosen and the $(n-k)!$ of the unchosen do not matter). If each bit is independently in error with probability $p$, any particular pattern with $k$ errors has probability $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$, hence $$P(k\text{ errors in }n\text{ bits})=\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n.$$ This is the binomial law; you will use it for block-error probabilities of every code in Chapter 17.

Random variables: turning outcomes into numbers

A random variable $X$ is a rule that attaches a number to every point of the probability square: the voltage at the output, the number of bit errors in a block, the noise sample at time $t_0$. It is described by one of three equivalent objects.

  • CDF $F_X(x)=P(X\le x)$: non-decreasing, right-continuous, from 0 to 1. It always exists, for discrete, continuous and mixed variables alike.
  • pmf $p_X(x)=P(X=x)$ for discrete $X$ (a list of lumps of probability).
  • pdf $f_X(x)=\dfrac{dF_X}{dx}$ for continuous $X$. A pdf is a density, not a probability. Probability is area under it: $P(a

Two facts we use constantly: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X=1$ and $f_X(x)\ge0$. A pdf can exceed 1 (a very narrow bump of unit area), a probability never can. A mixed variable has both a continuous part and impulses in its pdf, $f_X(x)=f_c(x)+\sum_ip_i\delta(x-x_i)$; these arise whenever a device saturates or quantises (below).

Expectation, variance and characteristic functions

The expectation (mean) is the probability-weighted average, $$\E[X]=\int x f_X(x)\,dx\quad\text{or}\quad \sum_x x\,p_X(x),\qquad \E[g(X)]=\int g(x)f_X(x)\,dx .$$ Think of the pdf as a thin wire with mass density $f_X$: $\E[X]$ is the balance point. The variance is the moment of inertia about that point: $$\sigma_X^2=\Var(X)=\E[(X-\mu)^2]=\E[X^2]-\mu^2\qquad(\text{expand the square and use linearity}).$$ In electronics $X$ is a voltage across $1\,\Omega$. Then $X^2$ is instantaneous power, $\E[X^2]$ is the average power, $\mu^2$ is the DC power, and $\sigma^2$ is the AC (fluctuating) power. This is why in the noise chapters “variance” and “power” are used almost interchangeably for zero-mean signals.

Rules you will use 100 times
  • Linearity (always, no independence needed): $\E[aX+bY+c]=a\E[X]+b\E[Y]+c$.
  • $\Var(aX+b)=a^2\Var(X)$. Shifting does nothing, scaling squares.
  • In general $\Var(X+Y)=\Var X+\Var Y+2\Cov(X,Y)$; if $X,Y$ are independent, $\Cov=0$ so variances add, and $\E[XY]=\E[X]\E[Y]$.

How far from the mean? Markov and Chebyshev

Derivation · two distribution-free bounds

Markov. For $X\ge0$ and $a>0$: $\E[X]=\int_0^\infty xf\,dx\ge\int_a^\infty xf\,dx\ge a\int_a^\infty f\,dx$, so $P(X\ge a)\le\E[X]/a$. Chebyshev. Apply Markov to $(X-\mu)^2$ with threshold $k^2\sigma^2$: $$P\big(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma\big)\le\frac{\E[(X-\mu)^2]}{k^2\sigma^2}=\frac1{k^2}.$$ So at most $1/9=11\%$ of any distribution lies beyond $3\sigma$. For a Gaussian the truth is $0.27\%$: Chebyshev is loose, but it needs nothing except the variance. It proves the law of large numbers: the mean of $n$ independent samples has variance $\sigma^2/n$, so $P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2)\to0$.

The characteristic function: Fourier transform of the pdf

$\Phi_X(\omega)=\E[e^{j\omega X}]=\int f_X(x)e^{j\omega x}dx$. This is the Fourier transform of the pdf (with the opposite sign convention), so everything from Chapter 2 applies. Three payoffs: (i) moments: $\E[X^k]=j^{-k}\Phi^{(k)}(0)$; (ii) the pdf of a sum of independent variables is a convolution, so $\Phi_{X+Y}=\Phi_X\Phi_Y$ (convolution theorem!); (iii) uniqueness: if two variables have the same $\Phi$ they have the same pdf, which lets us identify distributions of sums by inspection. For example, $\Phi_{\rm Gauss}(\omega)=e^{j\mu\omega-\sigma^2\omega^2/2}$ (the Gaussian-is-its-own-transform pair of Chapter 2).

Where these come from

Binomial mean and variance

$K=\sum_{i=1}^nB_i$ with independent Bernoulli $B_i$ ($\E B_i=p$, $\Var B_i=p(1-p)$ since $\E B_i^2=p$). Means add; so do variances, by independence: $\E K=np$, $\Var K=np(1-p)$.

Poisson as the limit of the binomial: the law of rare events

Cut an interval into $n$ tiny sub-intervals, each containing an arrival with probability $p=\lambda/n$ independently. The count is Binomial$(n,\lambda/n)$ and $$\binom nk\Big(\frac\lambda n\Big)^k\Big(1-\frac\lambda n\Big)^{n-k}=\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k}}_{\to1}\cdot\underbrace{\Big(1-\frac\lambda n\Big)^{n}}_{\to e^{-\lambda}}\cdot\underbrace{\Big(1-\frac\lambda n\Big)^{-k}}_{\to1}\ \to\ \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}.$$ So independent, rarely-occurring events (telephone calls, photon arrivals, cosmic-ray hits, errors in a very long low-BER stream) give Poisson counts, and the waiting time to the first arrival is exponential: $P(T>t)=P(\text{no arrival in }t)=e^{-\lambda t}$.

Exponential: no memory

$P(X>s+t\mid X>s)=\dfrac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}}=e^{-\lambda t}=P(X>t)$. The time you have already waited tells you nothing about the time still to come. (It is the only continuous distribution with this property.)

Uniform variance

For $U(-\tfrac\Delta2,\tfrac\Delta2)$: $\sigma^2=\frac1\Delta\int_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2}x^2dx=\frac1\Delta\cdot\frac{2(\Delta/2)^3}3=\frac{\Delta^2}{12}$. This is the quantisation noise power of Chapter 12: a quantiser with step $\Delta$ makes an error uniformly distributed over one step.

GATE pattern

GATE 2014 (Set 4) asked: “If calls arrive at a telephone exchange such that the arrival of any call is independent of earlier or later calls, the distribution of the number of calls in a fixed interval will be: Poisson / Gaussian / Exponential / Gamma.” Answer: Poisson. Independent arrivals ⇒ Poisson count; the gaps between them are exponential. One-liners like this are free marks.

The Gaussian: why nature loves it

History · de Moivre, Gauss, Laplace

In 1733 Abraham de Moivre noticed that coin-toss counts pile up in a bell shape. In 1801 the astronomer Piazzi lost the newly discovered dwarf planet Ceres behind the Sun; the 24-year-old Gauss recovered it using a method of fitting noisy observations (least squares) and, in 1809, argued that measurement errors follow the bell curve. In 1810 Laplace proved the general reason: add up lots of small independent effects and you get a bell, whatever the effects looked like individually. That is the central limit theorem.

Derivation · the Gaussian integral and the density

Is $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2/2}dx$ finite, and what is it? Square it, and treat the product as a double integral over the plane, then switch to polar coordinates: $$I^2=\iint e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}dx\,dy=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\big[-e^{-r^2/2}\big]_0^\infty=2\pi\ \Longrightarrow\ I=\sqrt{2\pi}.$$ Hence $\varphi(x)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}$ integrates to 1, and by shifting and scaling $X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ has pdf $\dfrac1{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2}$. Its characteristic function is $e^{j\mu\omega-\sigma^2\omega^2/2}$.

The central limit theorem, derived

Derivation · why sums of many small things are Gaussian

Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be independent with mean $\mu$, variance $\sigma^2$ and a common characteristic function $\Phi$. Define the standardised sum $Z_n=\dfrac1{\sigma\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\mu)$. The characteristic function of a sum of independents is the product, and scaling by $1/\sigma\sqrt n$ rescales the argument. Let $\Psi(\omega)$ be the characteristic function of $X_i-\mu$, whose Taylor expansion is $\Psi(\omega)=1+j\omega\cdot0-\tfrac12\sigma^2\omega^2+o(\omega^2)$ (mean zero, second moment $\sigma^2$). Then $$\Phi_{Z_n}(\omega)=\Big[\Psi\!\Big(\frac{\omega}{\sigma\sqrt n}\Big)\Big]^n=\Big[1-\frac{\omega^2}{2n}+o\!\Big(\frac1n\Big)\Big]^n\ \xrightarrow{\ n\to\infty\ }\ e^{-\omega^2/2},$$ which is the characteristic function of $\mathcal N(0,1)$. By uniqueness, $Z_n\to\mathcal N(0,1)$ in distribution. Only the first two moments of each term survive; the shape of the individual distributions is forgotten. A lopsided exponential becomes bell-shaped by $n\approx20$:

Histogram of 6000 standardised sums of $n$ draws (bars) against the Gaussian bell (line). Even the lopsided exponential becomes bell-shaped by $n\approx 20$. At $n=1$ you see the raw shape of one draw.

Thermal noise in a resistor is produced by $\sim10^{10}$ electrons jostling independently. Each contributes a tiny random push; their sum is the noise voltage. By the CLT that sum is Gaussian, which is why AWGN is the default model for almost every communication channel. The same argument says that the interference from many independent users in a cellular network is also approximately Gaussian, and that multipath fading (a sum of many reflected rays) has Gaussian real and imaginary parts, hence Rayleigh magnitude (Chapter 21).

The Gaussian: properties to know $$X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2):\quad f_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}.$$

(i) A linear function of a Gaussian is Gaussian: $aX+b\sim\mathcal N(a\mu+b,a^2\sigma^2)$ (scaling and shifting the characteristic function). (ii) A sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian with means and variances added (characteristic functions multiply: exponents add). (iii) For jointly Gaussian variables, uncorrelated ⇒ independent (only for Gaussians!). (iv) Standardise with $Z=(X-\mu)/\sigma\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. (v) Among all distributions of given variance, the Gaussian has the largest entropy (Chapter 10), which is why it is the worst noise for a given power.

The Q-function: the shape of every error probability

There is no closed form for $\int e^{-t^2/2}dt$, so we give the tail area a name. For the standard normal, $$Q(x)=P(Z>x)=\int_x^\infty\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^2/2}\,dt=\tfrac12\erfc\!\Big(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt2}\Big),\qquad Q(-x)=1-Q(x),\qquad Q(0)=\tfrac12 .$$ For a general $X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$: $\;P(X>a)=Q\!\big(\tfrac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\big)$. Every bit-error probability in this book will have the form $Q(\text{half the distance between the two signals}/\text{noise }\sigma)$. You are looking at the most important function of the syllabus. (The error function: $\erfc(u)=\frac2{\sqrt\pi}\int_u^\infty e^{-t^2}dt$, so $\erfc(u)=2Q(\sqrt2u)$.)

Top: the shaded tail is $Q(x)$. Bottom: $\log_{10}Q(x)$. Notice how violently it falls: moving from $x=3$ to $x=4$ cuts the error rate by a factor of 40. The dashed curve is the handy bound $Q(x)\le\frac12e^{-x^2/2}$.
Derivation · bounds and the large-$x$ approximation

Upper bound. For $t\ge x>0$ we have $1\le t/x$, so $Q(x)=\int_x^\infty\varphi(t)dt\le\frac1x\int_x^\infty t\varphi(t)\,dt=\frac{\varphi(x)}x$ (because $t\varphi(t)=-\varphi'(t)$). Lower bound. Integrate by parts: $Q(x)=\int_x^\infty\frac1t\,t\varphi(t)\,dt=\frac{\varphi(x)}x-\int_x^\infty\frac{\varphi(t)}{t^2}dt\ge\frac{\varphi(x)}x-\frac{Q(x)}{x^2}$ (since $1/t^2\le1/x^2$ on the range), and rearranging gives $Q(x)\ge\dfrac{x}{1+x^2}\varphi(x)$. Together, $\dfrac{x}{1+x^2}\varphi(x)\le Q(x)\le\dfrac{\varphi(x)}x$. The two bounds merge as $x$ grows, so $$Q(x)\approx\frac1{x\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}\qquad(x\gtrsim2),$$ within 19% at $x=2$ and 6% at $x=4$ (always slightly too large). A second, cruder but cleaner bound, from Craig’s representation $Q(x)=\frac1\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\exp\!\big(-\frac{x^2}{2\sin^2\theta}\big)d\theta$ (each integrand is at most $e^{-x^2/2}$), is $$Q(x)\le\tfrac12e^{-x^2/2}\qquad(x\ge0).$$ These exponents explain the “waterfall” of BER curves: error probability falls as $e^{-\mathrm{SNR}/2\ldots}$, exponentially in the SNR, so a few dB buy orders of magnitude.

Engineering practice · generating random variables on a computer

Every BER simulation starts from a uniform random generator. To get any other distribution, use the inverse-CDF method: if $U\sim U(0,1)$ then $F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$, because $P(F^{-1}(U)\le x)=P(U\le F(x))=F(x)$. Exponential: $X=-\ln(1-U)/\lambda$. For the Gaussian, which has no closed-form inverse, the Box–Muller method uses the polar derivation above in reverse: $R=\sqrt{-2\ln U_1}$ (Rayleigh) and $\Theta=2\pi U_2$ (uniform) give two independent standard normals $R\cos\Theta,\ R\sin\Theta$.

Rayleigh and Rice: where the envelope of noise comes from

Derivation · the Rayleigh law

Let $X,Y$ be independent $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ (the in-phase and quadrature components of narrowband noise, Chapter 5). Their joint pdf is $\frac1{2\pi\sigma^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2\sigma^2}$. Convert to polar coordinates $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ with Jacobian $r$: $$f_{R\Theta}(r,\theta)=\frac{r}{2\pi\sigma^2}e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}\ =\ \underbrace{\frac{r}{\sigma^2}e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}}_{f_R:\ \text{Rayleigh}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac1{2\pi}}_{f_\Theta:\ \text{uniform}}.$$ It factorises: the envelope $R$ is Rayleigh, the phase $\Theta$ is uniform, and they are independent. The tail is $P(R>r)=e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}$, and $R^2/2\sigma^2$ is exponential with mean 1. If a constant (a carrier of amplitude $A$) is added to one component, the envelope is instead Rician: $f_R(r)=\frac r{\sigma^2}e^{-(r^2+A^2)/2\sigma^2}I_0\!\big(\frac{rA}{\sigma^2}\big)$ (the angle integral produces the modified Bessel function $I_0$). For $A\gg\sigma$ the Rician becomes Gaussian around $A$; for $A=0$ it is Rayleigh. These two laws govern non-coherent detection (Chapter 15) and fading (Chapter 21).

Two random variables: correlation and independence

The joint behaviour of $X$ and $Y$ lives in a joint pdf $f_{XY}(x,y)$, a bump over the plane. Marginals are obtained by integrating out the other variable, $f_X(x)=\int f_{XY}\,dy$; conditionals by slicing and renormalising, $f_{Y|X}(y|x)=f_{XY}(x,y)/f_X(x)$. The two numbers you need: $$\Cov(X,Y)=\E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]=\E[XY]-\mu_X\mu_Y,\qquad \rho=\frac{\Cov(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}\in[-1,1].$$ Why $|\rho|\le1$: the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $|\E[UV]|^2\le\E[U^2]\E[V^2]$ applied to $U=X-\mu_X$, $V=Y-\mu_Y$; equality holds only if $V$ is a multiple of $U$. So $\rho=\pm1$ means $Y$ is an exact linear function of $X$; $\rho=0$ means “no linear relationship”.

Independent means $f_{XY}=f_Xf_Y$ (the bump factorises). Independent ⇒ uncorrelated. The converse is false: let $X$ be symmetric about zero and $Y=X^2$. $Y$ is fully determined by $X$, yet $\E[XY]=\E[X^3]=0$ so $\rho=0$. Look at both situations:

Each dot is one realisation of $(X,Y)$. The correlation coefficient measures only the straight-line tendency. The parabola and circle datasets are perfectly dependent and yet have $\rho\approx0$.

The bivariate Gaussian is the pleasant exception, and its picture is worth seeing in 3D. The hill is round when $\rho=0$, and stretches into a ridge along the diagonal as $|\rho|\to1$. For Gaussians, and only Gaussians, $\rho=0$ makes the hill round (factorised) and therefore independent.

Derivation · the best estimate of $Y$ from $X$ (jointly Gaussian)

Take zero-mean jointly Gaussian $X,Y$ with variances $\sigma_X^2,\sigma_Y^2$ and correlation $\rho$. Try to write $Y=aX+W$ with $W$ independent of $X$. Making $W=Y-aX$ uncorrelated with $X$ requires $\E[(Y-aX)X]=0$, i.e. $a=\dfrac{\E[XY]}{\E[X^2]}=\rho\dfrac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}$. Since $(X,W)$ are jointly Gaussian (linear combinations of Gaussians) and uncorrelated, they are independent. Therefore $$\E[Y\mid X=x]=\rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}\,x,\qquad\Var(Y\mid X)=\Var W=\sigma_Y^2-a^2\sigma_X^2=\sigma_Y^2(1-\rho^2).$$ Given $X=x$, $Y$ is Gaussian with that mean and that variance. The conditional mean is the minimum mean-square-error (MMSE) estimate $\hat Y(x)$, a straight line through the cloud, and the residual error power is $\sigma_Y^2(1-\rho^2)$: a correlation of $0.8$ removes $64\%$ of the uncertainty. This is the heart of estimation: Wiener filtering, channel estimation and Kalman filtering (Chapters 5, 13, 16) are this formula with vectors.

Worked example · why GATE 2026 Q.38 is easy

$X\sim U(-1,1)$ and $N\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ independent, $Y=X+N$, $Z=X^2+N$. Find the correlation (i.e. $\E[XY]$ and $\E[XZ]$ since means are zero). $\E[XY]=\E[X^2]+\E[XN]=\tfrac13+0$ since $\Var X=\frac{(2)^2}{12}=\frac13$. $\E[XZ]=\E[X^3]+\E[XN]=0+0$ because $X^3$ is an odd function of a symmetric variable. Answer $\tfrac13$ and $0$: $Z$ is strongly dependent on $X$ yet uncorrelated with it. Same lesson as the parabola above.

Sums of independent variables and the value of averaging

The received sample is “signal + noise” and the noise is a sum of lots of things. Facts: means add, variances add (for independent terms), and the pdf of the sum is the convolution of the individual pdfs, $f_{X+Y}=f_X*f_Y$ (equivalently, characteristic functions multiply). If both are Gaussian, the result is Gaussian: $\mathcal N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)+\mathcal N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\to\mathcal N(\mu_1+\mu_2,\ \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$.

Derivation · averaging $N$ noisy samples gains $10\log N$ dB

Measure the same constant $s$ in $N$ independent looks, $r_i=s+n_i$, $\E n_i=0$, $\Var n_i=\sigma^2$. The average $\bar r=\frac1N\sum r_i$ has mean $s$ and variance $\frac1{N^2}\cdot N\sigma^2=\sigma^2/N$. SNR $=s^2/\mathrm{variance}$ therefore improves by exactly $N$: $$\mathrm{SNR}_{\rm avg}=N\,\frac{s^2}{\sigma^2}\quad(+10\log_{10}N\ \mathrm{dB}).$$ Doubling the number of samples doubles the noise power of the sum but only $\sqrt2\times$ its amplitude, while the signal parts add coherently and double: signal power ×4, noise power ×2, SNR ×2 (+3 dB). That factor is the root of the matched filter (Chapter 14), of integrating over a symbol, of receive-diversity combining (Chapter 21) and of the processing gain of spread spectrum (Chapter 19). For correlated noise the gain is smaller: if $\rho$ is the correlation between samples, $\Var\bar n=\frac{\sigma^2}N[1+(N-1)\rho]\to\rho\sigma^2$, so averaging stops helping.

A first look at detection: the receiver operating characteristic

Everything in Chapters 14–15 is a refinement of this one-page problem. Decide between $H_0$ (noise only: $r=n$) and $H_1$ (signal present: $r=A+n$) from one sample, with $n\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$. Declare $H_1$ if $r>\tau$. Two error probabilities: $$P_{FA}=P(r>\tau\mid H_0)=Q\!\Big(\frac\tau\sigma\Big),\qquad P_D=P(r>\tau\mid H_1)=Q\!\Big(\frac{\tau-A}{\sigma}\Big).$$ Eliminating the threshold: with $d=A/\sigma$, $$\boxed{\,P_D=Q\big(Q^{-1}(P_{FA})-d\big)\,}$$ Raise $\tau$ and both fall (fewer false alarms, more misses); the curve of $P_D$ against $P_{FA}$ is the receiver operating characteristic. The Neyman–Pearson lemma says the likelihood-ratio test (here, a threshold on $r$) is the best possible for any fixed $P_{FA}$. With equal priors and equal costs the best threshold is the midpoint $A/2$ and $P_e=Q(d/2)$, the first appearance of the formula that governs Chapter 14.

Worked example · how much SNR must a radar have?

Required: $P_{FA}=10^{-6}$, $P_D=0.9$. Then $Q^{-1}(10^{-6})=4.753$ and $Q^{-1}(0.9)=-1.282$, so $d=4.753+1.282=6.035$ and the required per-sample SNR is $d^2=36.4$, i.e. 15.6 dB. Integrating $N=100$ pulses coherently (the averaging gain above) lets each pulse be 20 dB weaker: $-4.4$ dB per pulse. Because $P_{FA}$ sits on the Gaussian tail, relaxing it a hundredfold, from $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-4}$, saves only $1.6$ dB ($d=5.0$, $d^2=25$, $14.0$ dB instead of $15.6$ dB): the price of tail probabilities is logarithmic in the SNR.

Engineering practice · how many bits must a BER simulation run?

Estimating an error probability $P$ by counting errors in $N$ bits is a binomial experiment: the count has mean $NP$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{NP(1-P)}\approx\sqrt{NP}$, so the relative accuracy is $1/\sqrt{NP}$. For $\pm10\%$ you need $NP\ge100$ errors: $N\ge100/P$ bits. To verify $\mathrm{BER}=10^{-6}$ you must simulate (or transmit) $10^{8}$ bits; to verify $10^{-12}$, as in an optical link, $10^{14}$ bits, which at 10 Gbit/s is almost 3 hours. This is why standards quote BER-test durations, and why importance sampling and the analytical $Q$-function formulas are indispensable.

Set B · Numerical practice

A $(7,4)$ Hamming code decodes a 7-bit word correctly if the channel makes at most one bit error. Each bit is flipped independently with probability $\varepsilon=0.1$. The probability that a word is decoded correctly is ______ (two decimals).

Number of errors $\sim$ Binomial$(7,0.1)$. $P(\le1)=0.9^7+7(0.1)(0.9)^6=0.4783+0.3720=\mathbf{0.85}$. (The real GATE 2022 question asked exactly this; its official key is 0.84–0.86.)

Telephone calls arrive at an average rate of 3 per minute (Poisson). The probability that no call arrives in a given minute is ______.

$P(0)=e^{-\lambda}\lambda^0/0!=e^{-3}=\mathbf{0.0498}$.

A uniform quantiser has step size $\Delta=0.1$ V. The mean-square quantisation error, in units of $10^{-4}\,\mathrm V^2$, is ______.

$\Delta^2/12=0.01/12=8.33\times10^{-4}$ V².

Noise $n\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ is passed through a detector that declares an error whenever $|n|>2\sigma$. The probability of error is ______ (use $Q(2)=0.02275$).

$P(|n|>2\sigma)=2Q(2)=0.0455$. Two tails.

A received sample is $r=\mu+n$ with $\mu=2\ \text{V}$ and $n\sim\mathcal N(0,\ 4\ \text{V}^2)$. The probability that $r>5\ \text{V}$ is ______. ($Q(1.5)=0.0668$)

$\sigma=2$. $P(r>5)=Q\big(\frac{5-2}{2}\big)=Q(1.5)=0.0668$.

The envelope $R$ of narrowband Gaussian noise (each quadrature component $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$) is observed. $P(R>2\sigma)$ is ______.

Rayleigh tail $P(R>r)=e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}$, so $e^{-2}=\mathbf{0.1353}$.

$X,Y$ are zero-mean, unit-variance, jointly Gaussian with $\rho=0.8$. The MMSE estimate of $Y$ given $X=2$ is ______.

$\E[Y\mid X=x]=\rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}x=0.8\times2=\mathbf{1.6}$. (Residual variance $1-\rho^2=0.36$.)

A receiver averages $N=100$ independent noisy looks of a constant signal. The SNR improvement in dB is ______.

$10\log_{10}100=\mathbf{20}$ dB.

Chapter 4 in one breath

  • Probability = area of a unit square; conditioning = zoom and renormalise; Bayes = ratio of two areas; prior can overrule evidence.
  • pdf is density, probability is area; $\E[X^2]$ is average power, $\sigma^2$ AC power. Chebyshev: $P(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma)\le1/k^2$. Characteristic function = Fourier transform of the pdf; sums ⇒ products.
  • Poisson is the rare-event limit of the binomial; exponential has no memory; uniform has variance $\Delta^2/12$; two Gaussian quadratures give a Rayleigh envelope and uniform phase.
  • CLT: standardised sums of independent finite-variance terms → $\mathcal N(0,1)$ (characteristic functions: $(1-\omega^2/2n)^n\to e^{-\omega^2/2}$). Gaussians stay Gaussian under linear maps; uncorrelated ⇔ independent only for jointly Gaussian.
  • $Q(x)\approx\frac{1}{x\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}$, $\le\frac12e^{-x^2/2}$; know $Q(1),Q(2),Q(3),Q(4)$ and $Q^{-1}(10^{-6})=4.75$.
  • Function of a random variable: slope formula $\sum f_X(x_i)/|g'(x_i)|$; flat segments ⇒ impulses; inverse-CDF sampling.
  • Joint Gaussian: $\E[Y|X]=\rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}x$, residual variance $\sigma_Y^2(1-\rho^2)$. Averaging $N$ looks: SNR $\times N$.
  • Detection: $P_D=Q(Q^{-1}(P_{FA})-d)$; BER simulation needs $\gtrsim100/P$ bits.
