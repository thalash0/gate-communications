What you will be able to do after this chapter
  • Classify a signal (energy/power, even/odd, periodic, deterministic/random) and compute its energy or power from first principles.
  • Test any system for linearity, time-invariance, causality, memory and stability, and know which tests are quick and which are traps.
  • Derive the convolution integral, the frequency response $H(f)$ and the rule “sinusoid in, sinusoid out”, instead of memorising them.
  • Analyse the RC circuit and the RLC resonant circuit in time and frequency domains, including rise time $\approx 0.35/B$ and the Q-factor.
  • State and use the conditions for distortionless transmission; explain group delay and why phase matters for data but little for speech.
  • Choose a filter order from a specification, and do decibel bookkeeping ($\mathrm{dB}$, $\mathrm{dBm}$, $\mathrm{dBW}$) without hesitation.

Prerequisites: school calculus and complex numbers ($e^{j\theta}=\cos\theta+j\sin\theta$). Nothing else.

History · the cable that taught engineers about systems

In August 1858 a transatlantic telegraph cable carried Queen Victoria’s message to President Buchanan, 98 words that took 16 hours to get through. Within weeks the cable died: the operators had raised the sending voltage to 2000 V trying to speed it up, and the insulation failed. The real problem was understood by a young William Thomson (later Lord Kelvin): a long submarine cable behaves like a giant distributed resistor–capacitor circuit, so that a sharp pulse put in at one end arrives at the other as a slow, smeared lump, and pulses sent too quickly overlap. Thomson’s analysis, written in 1855 before the cable was laid, predicted that the signalling speed is inversely proportional to $RC$ (i.e. proportional to bandwidth). Wildman Whitehouse, the cable’s chief electrician, ignored it; Thomson was right.

That is the whole of this chapter in one story: a system acts on a signal; the system’s memory (here, capacitance) limits how fast the signal can change; and “how fast” translates into bandwidth. Eight years later, with Thomson’s theory applied, a new cable worked.

What is a signal?

A signal is a quantity that varies and carries information: a voltage $v(t)$ from a microphone, the pressure of a sound wave, the brightness along a scan line of a television picture, the sequence of bits $b[n]$ in a file. Mathematically it is a function of one or more independent variables; in this book almost always time.

Energy and power, from first principles

Why call $\int|x|^2dt$ “energy”? Because if $x(t)$ is a voltage across a $1\,\Omega$ resistor, the instantaneous power dissipated is $v^2/R=x^2(t)$ watts, and integrating power over time gives energy in joules. Communication engineers adopt the normalised $1\,\Omega$ convention so that “power” of a signal is just $\overline{x^2}$, whatever the actual impedance; real power is $\overline{x^2}/R$, and ratios (SNR, efficiency) do not depend on $R$ at all.

$$E=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt,\qquad P=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|x(t)|^2\,dt .$$

If $0energy signal (and $P=0$); if $0power signal (and $E=\infty$). A signal can be neither (a ramp $t\,u(t)$). For a periodic signal the limit reduces to one period: $P=\frac1{T_0}\int_{T_0}|x|^2dt$.

Derivation · power of a sinusoid, and of a sum of sinusoids

For $x(t)=A\cos(2\pi f_0t+\theta)$ the period is $T_0=1/f_0$. Use $\cos^2u=\tfrac12(1+\cos2u)$: $$P=\frac1{T_0}\int_0^{T_0}A^2\cos^2(2\pi f_0t+\theta)\,dt=\frac{A^2}{2T_0}\Big[\underbrace{\int_0^{T_0}dt}_{T_0}+\underbrace{\int_0^{T_0}\cos(4\pi f_0t+2\theta)dt}_{0}\Big]=\frac{A^2}{2}.$$ The oscillating part integrates to zero over any whole number of cycles. So a sinusoid’s power is half the square of its peak, or the square of its RMS value $A/\sqrt2$. This $\tfrac12$ is the single most-forgotten factor in the whole of communications.

Now take two sinusoids of different frequencies, $x=A\cos(2\pi f_1t)+B\cos(2\pi f_2t)$. Squaring gives $A^2\cos^2+B^2\cos^2+2AB\cos(2\pi f_1t)\cos(2\pi f_2t)$, and the cross term is $AB[\cos2\pi(f_1-f_2)t+\cos2\pi(f_1+f_2)t]$, a pair of oscillations of non-zero frequency whose long-time average vanishes. Hence $$P=\frac{A^2+B^2}2\qquad(\text{different frequencies: powers add}).$$ This is orthogonality of sinusoids at work: components at different frequencies do not interact in the average. If the frequencies are equal the amplitudes combine as phasors first, $A\cos(\omega t)+B\cos(\omega t+\theta)$ has amplitude $\sqrt{A^2+B^2+2AB\cos\theta}$ and power $\tfrac12(A^2+B^2+2AB\cos\theta)$, which is why two equal signals in phase give four times the power of one, not two (+6 dB, not +3 dB).

Worked example · energy of a one-sided exponential and a pulse

(a) $x(t)=e^{-at}u(t)$, $a>0$: $E=\int_0^\infty e^{-2at}dt=\dfrac1{2a}$. (b) $x(t)=A\,\rect(t/\tau)$ (height $A$, width $\tau$): $E=A^2\tau$. (c) A random binary signal that is $\pm1\,\mathrm V$ forever has $P=1\,\mathrm W$ and $E=\infty$: a power signal. (d) $x(t)=3+4\cos(2\pi t)$ has $P=3^2+\tfrac{4^2}2=17$ (the DC and the sinusoid are orthogonal: they add in power).

Even and odd parts

Every signal splits uniquely as $x=x_e+x_o$ with $x_e(t)=\tfrac12[x(t)+x(-t)]$ and $x_o(t)=\tfrac12[x(t)-x(-t)]$. Even and odd parts have zero cross-energy, $\int x_ex_o\,dt=0$ (an odd integrand over a symmetric range), so $E_x=E_{x_e}+E_{x_o}$. For the causal $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$, $x_e=\tfrac12e^{-|t|}$ and $x_o=\tfrac12\sgn(t)e^{-|t|}$, and each carries half the energy. The same split reappears in Fourier analysis: the even part gives the real part of the spectrum and the odd part the imaginary part.

The elementary signals

  • Unit step $u(t)$ (0 for $t<0$, 1 for $t>0$): switches things on. Rectangle $\rect(t/T)$: $u(t+T/2)-u(t-T/2)$. Ramp $r(t)=t\,u(t)=\int_{-\infty}^tu$.
  • Complex exponential $e^{j2\pi ft}=\cos2\pi ft+j\sin2\pi ft$: the building block. Real sinusoids are $\cos=\tfrac12(e^{j\cdot}+e^{-j\cdot})$.
  • Sinc $\sinc(x)=\dfrac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}$: value 1 at the origin, zeros at nonzero integers.
  • Impulse (delta) $\delta(t)$: the idealisation of a very narrow, very tall pulse of unit area.
The impulse, defined by what it does

No ordinary function is zero everywhere but the origin yet has unit area. Instead, define $\delta$ as the limit of narrower and narrower unit-area pulses, e.g. $\delta_\varepsilon(t)=\frac1\varepsilon\rect(t/\varepsilon)$, and ask what happens when we integrate it against a smooth function $x(t)$: $$\int x(t)\delta_\varepsilon(t)\,dt=\frac1\varepsilon\int_{-\varepsilon/2}^{\varepsilon/2}x(t)\,dt\ \xrightarrow{\ \varepsilon\to0\ }\ x(0).$$ Shifting, we get the sifting property $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\,\delta(t-t_0)\,dt=x(t_0)$: the impulse “sifts out” the value of $x$ at the instant it sits on. Two consequences you will use repeatedly:

  • Scaling: $\delta(at)=\dfrac1{|a|}\delta(t)$. (Substitute $u=at$ in $\int\delta(at)dt$: the area becomes $1/|a|$. Squeezing time by 3 makes a pulse of the same height three times narrower, so its area is a third.)
  • Product: $x(t)\delta(t-t_0)=x(t_0)\delta(t-t_0)$, and $\delta(t)=\dfrac{du}{dt}$ (the derivative of a step).

Systems and their properties

A system is anything that turns an input signal into an output signal, $y=\mathcal S\{x\}$: an amplifier, a filter, a cable, the radio channel between two antennas, an algorithm. The following properties decide what mathematics applies.

Linearity
$\mathcal S\{ax_1+bx_2\}=a\,\mathcal S\{x_1\}+b\,\mathcal S\{x_2\}$. Two parts: additivity and homogeneity (scaling). Consequence: zero in ⇒ zero out. A system with an added offset, $y=2x+3$, is not linear (it is “affine”).
Time invariance
Delay the input by $t_0$, and the output is merely delayed by $t_0$: $x(t-t_0)\mapsto y(t-t_0)$. The system’s behaviour does not drift with the clock.
Causality
The output at time $t$ depends only on inputs at times $\le t$. All physical real-time systems are causal; off-line processing need not be.
Memory
A memoryless system’s output at $t$ depends only on $x(t)$ (a resistor, an ideal amplifier, a diode). Capacitors, inductors, delays and integrators have memory.
Stability (BIBO)
Every bounded input gives a bounded output.
Invertibility
Distinct inputs give distinct outputs, so the input can be recovered (important for equalisation, Chapter 13).
A quick procedure for the two tests that GATE loves

Time-invariance test: (1) feed the delayed input $x_d(t)=x(t-t_0)$ into the system and write the result $y_1(t)$. (2) Take the original output $y(t)$ and delay it: $y_2(t)=y(t-t_0)$. (3) The system is TI iff $y_1\equiv y_2$ for all $x$. The trap: in step (2) replace $t$ by $t-t_0$ everywhere in the formula, including the explicit $t$’s.
Linearity test: feed $ax_1+bx_2$; compare with $ay_1+by_2$. Quick kills: a constant added, a power or absolute value of $x$, a product of $x$ with itself.

Two traps

(1) Linear ≠ “straight line on a graph”. $y=2x+3$ is a straight line but fails the linearity test. (2) A linear system cannot create new frequencies. If an input contains only $f_1$ and $f_2$, an LTI system output contains only $f_1$ and $f_2$. New frequencies need a non-linear or time-varying element: the square-law diode, the multiplier. The whole of modulation theory is about exploiting exactly this.

Linear time-invariant (LTI) systems: one function tells you everything

Deriving the convolution integral

From sifting property to convolution
  1. Any input can be written as a continuum of impulses: by the sifting property, $x(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)\,\delta(t-\tau)\,d\tau$ (a weighted sum of delayed impulses, weight $x(\tau)d\tau$).
  2. Define the impulse response $h(t)=\mathcal S\{\delta(t)\}$: what the system does when kicked once at $t=0$.
  3. By time-invariance the response to $\delta(t-\tau)$ is $h(t-\tau)$.
  4. By linearity the response to the weighted sum is the same weighted sum of responses (the integral is a limit of sums): $$\boxed{\,y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)\,h(t-\tau)\,d\tau=(x*h)(t)\,}$$

This is the formal statement of the intuition “output = total of all the delayed, scaled echoes of the input”. The substitution $\tau\to t-\tau$ shows $x*h=h*x$.

A few properties follow directly: convolution is commutative, associative and distributive, so two LTI systems in cascade have $h=h_1*h_2$ (and the order does not matter), and in parallel $h=h_1+h_2$. Convolving with $\delta(t-t_0)$ just delays: $x*\delta(\cdot-t_0)=x(t-t_0)$. The step response is $s(t)=\int_{-\infty}^th(\tau)d\tau$, and so $h=ds/dt$. The widget in Chapter 2 lets you watch the flip–slide–multiply–integrate picture.

Causality in LTI language: $h(t)=0$ for $t<0$ (the system cannot react before it is kicked). Memorylessness: $h(t)=K\delta(t)$.

BIBO stability ⟺ $\int|h|\,dt<\infty$

Sufficiency. If $|x(t)|\le M$ then $|y(t)|=\big|\int x(\tau)h(t-\tau)d\tau\big|\le M\int|h|$. Necessity. Choose the bounded input $x(t)=\sgn[h(-t)]$. Then $y(0)=\int x(\tau)h(-\tau)d\tau=\int|h|$, which is infinite if $h$ is not absolutely integrable. ∎ Example: $h=e^{-at}u(t)$ is stable for $a>0$ ($\int|h|=1/a$); $h=u(t)$ (the integrator) is not.

Why sinusoids are special: the frequency response

Complex exponentials are eigenfunctions of every LTI system

Feed $x(t)=e^{j2\pi ft}$ into the convolution integral: $$y(t)=\int h(\tau)\,e^{j2\pi f(t-\tau)}\,d\tau=e^{j2\pi ft}\underbrace{\int h(\tau)\,e^{-j2\pi f\tau}d\tau}_{H(f)}=H(f)\,e^{j2\pi ft}.$$ The output is the same exponential, multiplied by a (complex) constant $H(f)$ that depends only on $f$ and on the system. An eigenfunction of a matrix is a vector that the matrix merely rescales; $e^{j2\pi ft}$ is the same thing for every LTI system. $H(f)$ is the frequency response, the Fourier transform of $h$ (Chapter 2).

For a real system ($h$ real) $H(-f)=H^*(f)$, and a real cosine $A\cos(2\pi ft+\theta)=\tfrac A2[e^{j(\cdot)}+e^{-j(\cdot)}]$ passes as $$A\cos(2\pi ft+\theta)\ \longmapsto\ A\,|H(f)|\cos\big(2\pi ft+\theta+\angle H(f)\big).$$ A sinusoid in is a sinusoid out, at the same frequency, with amplitude scaled by $|H(f)|$ and phase shifted by $\angle H(f)$. Since any signal is a sum of sinusoids (Chapter 2), this tells us the output for any input: the foundation of all filter analysis, and the reason we study the world in the frequency domain.

The RC circuit, the “hydrogen atom” of signal processing

A resistor $R$ in series with a capacitor $C$, output taken across the capacitor. The current is $i=C\,dy/dt$ and Kirchhoff’s voltage law gives $x=Ri+y$, i.e. $$RC\frac{dy}{dt}+y=x .$$ Frequency response. Put $x=e^{j2\pi ft}$, $y=H(f)e^{j2\pi ft}$, so $dy/dt=j2\pi f\,y$: $$H(f)=\frac1{1+j2\pi fRC},\qquad|H|=\frac1{\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}},\quad\angle H=-\arctan\frac f{f_c},\quad f_c=\frac1{2\pi RC}.$$ At $f=f_c$: $|H|=1/\sqrt2$ (power halved: $-3.01\,\mathrm{dB}$) and phase $-45^\circ$. Below $f_c$ the circuit passes; above, it attenuates at $-20\,\mathrm{dB/decade}$ ($-6\,\mathrm{dB/octave}$), because $|H|\approx f_c/f$.

Impulse and step response. The inverse transform (or direct solution) gives $$h(t)=\frac1{RC}e^{-t/RC}u(t),\qquad s(t)=\big(1-e^{-t/RC}\big)u(t).$$ The step response is the capacitor charging: it reaches 63.2% after one time constant $\tau=RC$ and 99.3% after $5\tau$. $\int h=1$ (unity DC gain).

Rise time ≈ 0.35 / bandwidth: a rule that works for every low-pass system

Define the 10–90% rise time $t_r$: from $0.1$ to $0.9$ of the final value. For the RC step response, $1-e^{-t_{10}/\tau}=0.1\Rightarrow t_{10}=\tau\ln\tfrac{1}{0.9}$ and $t_{90}=\tau\ln\tfrac1{0.1}$, so $t_r=\tau\ln9=2.197\,\tau$. The 3 dB bandwidth is $B=f_c=1/(2\pi\tau)$. Eliminating $\tau$: $$t_r=\frac{2.197}{2\pi B}=\frac{0.35}{B}.$$ Surprisingly, this holds approximately for every well-behaved low-pass system (a 2nd-order Butterworth gives $0.34/B$; a Gaussian $0.34/B$). It is the quantitative form of “fast edges need wide bandwidth”. A 100 MHz oscilloscope cannot show edges faster than $3.5$ ns; a digital link of bit rate $R_b$ whose receiver filter has bandwidth $B$ has a rise time $0.35/B$, which must be a fraction of the bit time $1/R_b$, giving the practical rule of thumb $B\gtrsim0.5\,R_b$ (Chapter 13 proves the exact lower bound, $B=R_b/2$).

Four filters, each in three views. Top: magnitude in dB on a logarithmic frequency axis (frequency in units of $f_0$). Middle: phase. Bottom: time response (step for low-/high-pass, impulse for the band-pass). Switch to the resonant types and increase the quality factor $Q$: the response peaks, the bandwidth shrinks as $f_0/Q$, and the time response rings for about $Q/\pi f_0$ seconds. Narrow in frequency ⇒ long in time: the uncertainty principle again.
Engineering practice · the RC filter at work

Anti-alias and smoothing filters. Every ADC input has an RC (or LC) low-pass in front of it; every PWM audio or DAC output has one behind it. Coupling capacitors are the RC high-pass ($f_c=1/2\pi RC$), blocking DC while passing audio: choose $f_c\ll$ lowest signal frequency, e.g. $f_c=20\,\mathrm{Hz}$ for audio ($R=10\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$, $C=0.8\,\mu$F). The detector in an AM radio is a diode and an RC whose time constant must be long compared to the carrier period but short compared to the modulating signal: that design trade (Chapter 6) is nothing but the step-response of this circuit. Cable and PCB traces are distributed RC lines; Thomson’s transatlantic problem reappears every time you try to drive a long on-chip wire or a long Ethernet run.

Laplace transform, poles and zeros (just enough)

The Fourier transform handles steady-state sinusoids. To handle transients, initial conditions and stability, generalise $j2\pi f$ to a complex variable $s=\sigma+j\omega$. The (unilateral) Laplace transform of a causal signal is $X(s)=\int_0^\infty x(t)e^{-st}dt$, defined where the integral converges (the region of convergence, ROC).

Convolution becomes multiplication, $Y(s)=H(s)X(s)$, where $H(s)$ is the transfer function; the frequency response is $H(f)=H(s)\big|_{s=j2\pi f}$. A rational $H(s)=K\dfrac{\prod(s-z_i)}{\prod(s-p_i)}$ is described by its zeros $z_i$ and poles $p_i$. Three facts to carry away:

  • A causal LTI system is BIBO-stable iff all poles lie in the open left half-plane (each pole $p$ contributes a term $e^{pt}$ to $h$, which decays iff $\mathrm{Re}\,p<0$). The RC circuit has $H(s)=\dfrac{1/RC}{s+1/RC}$, one pole at $s=-1/RC$.
  • Magnitude response from the pole–zero plot: $|H(f)|=K\,\dfrac{\prod\text{distances from }j2\pi f\text{ to zeros}}{\prod\text{distances from }j2\pi f\text{ to poles}}$. A pole near the imaginary axis creates a peak in the response at that frequency (resonance); a zero on the axis creates a null.
  • Initial/final value theorems: $x(0^+)=\lim_{s\to\infty}sX(s)$, $x(\infty)=\lim_{s\to0}sX(s)$ (if the latter exists).

The second-order resonant circuit: the tuned circuit of every radio

Series RLC driven by $x$; output across $R$. KVL: $x=Ri+L\,di/dt+\frac1C\int i\,dt$, so with $s$: $$H(s)=\frac{R}{R+sL+\frac1{sC}}=\frac{(R/L)\,s}{s^2+(R/L)s+\frac1{LC}}.$$ Match with the standard form $\dfrac{(\omega_0/Q)s}{s^2+(\omega_0/Q)s+\omega_0^2}$: $$\omega_0=\frac1{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad Q=\frac{\omega_0L}R=\frac1{\omega_0RC}=\frac1R\sqrt{\frac LC},\qquad\text{bandwidth }B=\frac{f_0}Q=\frac R{2\pi L}.$$ Magnitude. On the imaginary axis, $$|H(f)|^2=\frac1{1+Q^2\big(\frac f{f_0}-\frac{f_0}f\big)^2}\ \approx\ \frac1{1+\big(\frac{2Q\,\Delta f}{f_0}\big)^2}\quad(\Delta f=f-f_0\ll f_0).$$ Half-power ($|H|^2=\tfrac12$) occurs when $Q\big(\frac f{f_0}-\frac{f_0}f\big)=\pm1$, whose two positive roots $f_{1,2}$ satisfy $f_2-f_1=f_0/Q$ and $f_1f_2=f_0^2$ (the response is symmetric on a log frequency axis, and nearly symmetric on a linear axis if $Q\gg1$).

Worked example · selectivity of an AM-radio tuned circuit

An LC tuner at $f_0=1\,\mathrm{MHz}$ with $Q=100$ has $B=10\,\mathrm{kHz}$. A station $9\,\mathrm{kHz}$ away (the adjacent AM channel) is attenuated by $|H|^2=1/(1+Q^2(1.009-0.991)^2)$: here $f/f_0-f_0/f=1.009-0.99107=0.01793$, $Q\times=1.793$, $|H|^2=1/4.21$, i.e. only $-6.2\,\mathrm{dB}$. A single tuned circuit cannot reject an adjacent channel! This is exactly why the superheterodyne receiver (Chapter 8) moves the signal to a fixed intermediate frequency where several coupled, high-$Q$ resonators (ceramic or crystal filters, $Q$ in thousands) provide 40–60 dB of rejection.

Engineering practice · where Q comes from

Coil resistance, dielectric losses and radiation limit $Q$: an air-core RF coil reaches $Q\approx100$–$300$, a quartz crystal $10^4$–$10^6$ (hence the astonishing frequency stability of crystal oscillators and the sharp filters in communication receivers), a SAW filter in a phone a few hundred, and an optical cavity $10^8$ or more. In a radio-frequency amplifier the tuned load sets both gain and bandwidth, and the gain–bandwidth trade-off is simply $B=f_0/Q$. For a superhet IF of $455\,\mathrm{kHz}$ with a $9\,\mathrm{kHz}$ channel, $Q_{\rm loaded}\approx50$ per stage.

Distortionless transmission

When is a system a perfect conduit? A channel that merely amplifies and delays, $y(t)=Kx(t-t_d)$, changes nothing about the shape of the message. Taking the Fourier transform, $$H(f)=Ke^{-j2\pi ft_d}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\boxed{|H(f)|=K\ \text{(flat)}\quad\text{and}\quad\angle H(f)=-2\pi ft_d\ \text{(linear in }f)}$$ over the band that the signal occupies. Two kinds of distortion are possible, and they have different consequences:

  • Amplitude (frequency) distortion: $|H|$ not flat: some frequencies are boosted or cut (bass-heavy telephones, a coax cable that loses high frequencies). Fixed with an equaliser that has the inverse magnitude.
  • Phase (delay) distortion: $\angle H$ not linear: different frequencies are delayed by different times, so the waveform shape changes even if all amplitudes are right. The ear is nearly insensitive to this (speech remains intelligible), but a pulse train is wrecked: pulses spread and overlap, which is inter-symbol interference.
Group delay and phase delay: what a modulated wave actually experiences

Consider a bandpass signal $x(t)=a(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct)$ whose message $a(t)$ is slow (bandwidth $\ll f_c$). Let the channel have phase $\phi(f)=\angle H(f)$ and near $f_c$ the linear approximation $\phi(f)\approx\phi(f_c)+(f-f_c)\phi'(f_c)$. Write $\phi(f_c)=-2\pi f_ct_p$ and $\phi'(f_c)=-2\pi t_g$. In frequency terms, $X(f)$ has components near $\pm f_c$; the positive-frequency part, multiplied by $H=|H|e^{j\phi}$, is $$Y_+(f)\approx|H(f_c)|\,\tfrac12A(f-f_c)\,e^{j[\phi(f_c)+(f-f_c)\phi'(f_c)]}=|H(f_c)|\,\tfrac12A(f-f_c)\,e^{-j2\pi f_ct_p}\,e^{-j2\pi(f-f_c)t_g}.$$ The factor $e^{-j2\pi(f-f_c)t_g}$ delays the baseband message $a(t)$ by $t_g$; the constant $e^{-j2\pi f_ct_p}$ delays (phase-shifts) the carrier by $t_p$. Therefore $$y(t)\approx|H(f_c)|\;a(t-t_g)\cos\!\big(2\pi f_c(t-t_p)\big),$$ $$\boxed{\,\text{group delay }t_g=-\frac1{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{d f}=-\frac{d\phi}{d\omega},\qquad\text{phase delay }t_p=-\frac{\phi(f_c)}{2\pi f_c}\,}$$ The envelope (the information) travels at the group delay; the carrier’s crests travel at the phase delay. They are equal when the phase is exactly linear through the origin (the distortionless case), and different otherwise. In a waveguide or plasma the phase velocity can even exceed $c$, while the group velocity (the speed of information) never does.

Worked example · RC circuit group delay

$\phi(\omega)=-\arctan(\omega RC)$, so $t_g=\dfrac{RC}{1+(\omega RC)^2}$: the delay equals $RC$ at low frequencies and falls to zero above $f_c$. For a voice signal in $0.3$–$3.4$ kHz through an RC with $f_c=20\,\mathrm{kHz}$, $\omega RC\le0.17$ and $t_g$ varies by only $3\%$ over the band: distortion negligible. For a 1 Mbit/s data stream through the same filter, edges would be smeared noticeably. Same system; whether it distorts depends on what you send through it.

Ideal filters are impossible; real filters are approximations

The ideal low-pass filter $H(f)=\rect(f/2B)e^{-j2\pi ft_d}$ has impulse response $h(t)=2B\,\sinc\!\big(2B(t-t_d)\big)$, which starts at $t=-\infty$: non-causal. More generally, the Paley–Wiener criterion says a magnitude response $|H|$ is realisable by a causal filter only if $\int\frac{|\ln|H(f)||}{1+f^2}df<\infty$; in particular $|H|$ can be zero only at isolated frequencies, never over a band. So “brick-wall” filters must be approximated, and real design is a trade between sharpness, ripple, delay and complexity.

Worked example · why we oversample (anti-alias filter order)

Telephone speech is limited to $3.4$ kHz and sampled at $8$ kHz. Suppose the anti-alias filter must be at most $3$ dB down at $3.4$ kHz and at least $40$ dB down at $4.6$ kHz, so that aliases (which fold about $4$ kHz) are suppressed. For a Butterworth filter, $10\log\big[1+(4.6/3.4)^{2n}\big]\ge40$ requires $2n\ge\dfrac{\ln(9999)}{\ln(1.353)}=30.5$, so $n=16$: an unrealistically complex analog circuit. Now sample at $48$ kHz instead: the same 40 dB must be reached by $44.6$ kHz (the alias of 3.4 kHz), and $10\log[1+(44.6/3.4)^{2n}]\ge40$ is met by $n=2$ (44.7 dB). The sharp filtering is moved into cheap, perfectly reproducible digital arithmetic (decimation from 48 kHz to 8 kHz). This is how every modern voice codec and audio CD player works (“oversampling”).

Decibels, dBm and level bookkeeping

Communication links span absurd ranges: a transmitter of $10^{3}$ W, a received signal of $10^{-13}$ W, noise at $10^{-14}$ W. Multiplicative gains and losses along a chain become additions on a logarithmic scale. The decibel is $10\log_{10}$ of a power ratio: $$G_{\rm dB}=10\log_{10}\frac{P_{\rm out}}{P_{\rm in}},\qquad\text{and for voltages (same impedance):}\ \ G_{\rm dB}=20\log_{10}\frac{V_{\rm out}}{V_{\rm in}}.$$ The factor 20 is just $10\log(V^2)$. Memorise: $3\,\mathrm{dB}\approx\times2$ (exactly $10\log2=3.0103$), $6\,\mathrm{dB}\approx\times4$, $10\,\mathrm{dB}=\times10$, $20\,\mathrm{dB}=\times100$; $-3\,\mathrm{dB}=\times\tfrac12$. Everything else: $7\,\mathrm{dB}\approx\times5$, $13\,\mathrm{dB}=10+3\approx\times20$, $1\,\mathrm{dB}\approx\times1.26$.

Worked example · a level diagram

A transmitter outputs $+30\,\mathrm{dBm}$ (1 W). A cable loses $3\,\mathrm{dB/100\,m}$ over $200\,\mathrm{m}$ ($-6$ dB), then a $+12$ dB amplifier, then a $-2$ dB connector loss. Output level: $30-6+12-2=34\,\mathrm{dBm}=2.5\,\mathrm{W}$. Overall gain: $+4\,\mathrm{dB}$ (×2.5). In linear terms you would have multiplied $1\times0.25\times15.8\times0.63=2.5$ W: same answer, three times the work. Never add dB values with linear values, and never add two dBm numbers (adding two $-90\,\mathrm{dBm}$ signals gives $-87\,\mathrm{dBm}$, not $-180$).

Discrete-time systems in one page

Digital signal processing works on samples $x[n]$. Everything above has a discrete counterpart: the impulse is the Kronecker delta $\delta[n]$; the convolution sum is $y[n]=\sum_kx[k]h[n-k]$ (the same derivation: write $x[n]$ as a sum of weighted shifted deltas); the eigenfunctions are $e^{j\omega n}$ and the frequency response is $H(e^{j\omega})=\sum_nh[n]e^{-j\omega n}$, periodic in $\omega$ with period $2\pi$ (because sampling replicates spectra, Chapter 3). The z-transform $H(z)=\sum h[n]z^{-n}$ plays the role of Laplace; a causal system is stable iff all poles are inside the unit circle.

Two families matter in communications. FIR (finite impulse response) filters, $y[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}b_kx[n-k]$, have no feedback, are always stable and can have exactly linear phase if $b_k$ is symmetric (hence zero phase distortion, group delay $(N-1)/2$ samples). The simplest example, the $N$-point moving average, has $$H(e^{j\omega})=\frac1N\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{-j\omega k}=\frac1N\frac{\sin(N\omega/2)}{\sin(\omega/2)}e^{-j\omega(N-1)/2}$$ (a geometric series; the amplitude is the discrete cousin of the sinc, with nulls at $\omega=2\pi k/N$). IIR filters use feedback, e.g. $y[n]=ay[n-1]+(1-a)x[n]$, the digital RC; they are efficient but can be unstable and have non-linear phase. Equalisers, matched filters, pulse-shaping filters and the interpolators in every modem are FIR filters.

Engineering practice · what runs inside a modem

A modern receiver samples the incoming IF or baseband signal, then everything else is FIR/IIR filtering and multiply-accumulate arithmetic: channel-select filter (FIR, ~100 taps), matched filter, equaliser (LMS-adapted FIR, 5–50 taps), timing-recovery interpolator, carrier-recovery loop filter (digital PLL, an IIR of order 1–2). One multiply-accumulate per tap per sample: a $100$-tap filter at 20 Msample/s needs $2\times10^9$ MAC/s, trivial for a modern DSP or FPGA.

Set A · Concept checksQuick, no calculator. If any of these is shaky, re-read the matching section before going on.

Which system is linear but not time-invariant?
  1. $y(t)=x^2(t)$
  2. $y(t)=x(t-3)$
  3. $y(t)=t\,x(t)$
  4. $y(t)=2x(t)+1$

(A) is non-linear. (B) is LTI. (D) is affine, non-linear (zero in gives 1 out). (C): $t\,(ax_1+bx_2)=a\,tx_1+b\,tx_2$ is linear; delaying the input gives $t\,x(t-t_0)$ but delaying the output gives $(t-t_0)x(t-t_0)$: different, so not TI. (C).

Select all true statements about an LTI system with impulse response $h(t)$.
  1. It is causal iff $h(t)=0$ for $t<0$.
  2. It is BIBO stable iff $h(t)\to0$ as $t\to\infty$.
  3. Its output for $e^{j2\pi ft}$ is $H(f)e^{j2\pi ft}$.
  4. Cascading two such systems gives $h_1*h_2$, independent of order.

(B) is false: $h(t)=\dfrac{1}{1+t}u(t)\to0$ but $\int|h|=\infty$ (logarithmic divergence), so it is not stable. Stability needs absolute integrability. The others are exactly the results derived above.

A low-pass system’s 3 dB bandwidth is quadrupled. Its 10–90% rise time becomes
  1. 4 times as long
  2. one quarter as long
  3. unchanged
  4. half as long

$t_r\approx0.35/B$: inversely proportional to bandwidth. (B).

A channel has $|H(f)|=1$ and $\angle H(f)=-\alpha f^2$ over the signal band. It exhibits
  1. no distortion
  2. amplitude distortion only
  3. constant group delay
  4. delay distortion (dispersion)

Group delay $-\frac1{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{df}=\frac{\alpha f}{\pi}$ grows with $f$: different frequencies arrive at different times. Magnitude is flat, so no amplitude distortion. (D). (Optical fibre dispersion is the same effect.)

Set B · Numerical practiceEach takes 1–3 minutes. Every step in the solution uses a result derived above.

$x(t)=3\cos(2\pi\cdot100t)+4\sin(2\pi\cdot250t)+\sqrt6$ (volts across $1\,\Omega$). The average power in watts is ______.

Sinusoids at different frequencies add in power: $\frac{3^2}2+\frac{4^2}2=12.5$. The DC term contributes $(\sqrt6)^2=6$ (it is orthogonal to both sinusoids). Total $=\mathbf{18.5}$ W. Forgetting the DC term gives 12.5; using peak instead of RMS gives 31.

An RC low-pass filter has $R=2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ and $C=0.1\,\mu$F. Its 3-dB cut-off frequency in Hz is ______.

$f_c=\dfrac1{2\pi RC}=\dfrac1{2\pi\cdot2000\cdot10^{-7}}=\dfrac1{1.2566\times10^{-3}}=\mathbf{795.8}$ Hz.

A channel of 3 dB bandwidth 1 MHz is modelled as a first-order low-pass. Its 10–90% rise time for a step input, in microseconds, is ______.

$t_r=0.35/B=0.35/10^6\ \mathrm s=\mathbf{0.35}\ \mu$s.

A series RLC circuit (output across $R$) has $L=100\,\mu$H, $C=253\,$pF and $R=10\,\Omega$. Its 3-dB bandwidth in kHz is ______.

$f_0=\dfrac1{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}=\dfrac1{2\pi\sqrt{2.53\times10^{-14}}}=1.0006$ MHz. $Q=\dfrac{\omega_0L}R=\dfrac{2\pi(1.0006\times10^6)(10^{-4})}{10}=62.9$. $B=f_0/Q=15.9$ kHz, which equals $R/(2\pi L)=10/(2\pi\cdot10^{-4})=\mathbf{15.9}$ kHz (independent of $C$!).

A signal of $-10\,\mathrm{dBm}$ passes through an amplifier of gain $+30\,\mathrm{dB}$, then a cable of loss $6\,\mathrm{dB}$, then an amplifier of gain $20\,\mathrm{dB}$. The output level in dBm is ______.

$-10+30-6+20=\mathbf{34}$ dBm ($\approx2.5$ W).

A third-order Butterworth low-pass filter ($|H|^2=1/[1+(f/f_c)^{6}]$) is used. The attenuation at $f=2f_c$, in dB, is ______.

$|H|^2=1/(1+2^6)=1/65$, attenuation $=10\log_{10}65=\mathbf{18.1}$ dB. (Rule of thumb: $6n$ dB per octave in the stopband: $6\times3=18$ dB ✓.)

The impulse response of an LTI system is $h(t)=3e^{-2t}u(t)$. Its DC gain $H(0)$ is ______.

$H(0)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty h(t)dt=\int_0^\infty3e^{-2t}dt=\mathbf{1.5}$. (Equivalently $H(f)=\frac3{2+j2\pi f}$.)

$x(t)=\cos(2\pi\cdot5t)$ is applied to a system with $h(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$. The amplitude of the output is closest to
  1. 0.32
  2. 0.032
  3. 0.5
  4. 1

$H(f)=\dfrac1{1+j2\pi f}$ and $2\pi f=10\pi=31.4$ rad/s, so $|H|=1/\sqrt{1+31.4^2}=0.0318$. (B). (The trap is using $f=5$ in place of $2\pi f$.)

Which of these systems are time-invariant?
  1. $y(t)=x(t)\cos(10t)$
  2. $y(t)=x(t)+x(t-1)$
  3. $y(t)=\displaystyle\int_{t-1}^{t}x(\tau)d\tau$
  4. $y(t)=x(t^2)$

(A) multiplies by an explicit function of $t$: time-varying. (B) and (C) are LTI (a two-tap comb and a moving integrator). (D): delaying the input gives $x(t^2-t_0)$, delaying the output gives $x((t-t_0)^2)$: different, so time-varying. B, C.

A channel has $|H(f)|=1$ and phase $\phi(f)=-2\pi t_0f-\pi\kappa f^2$ with $t_0=2\,\mu$s and $\kappa=4\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{s^2}$. The group delay at $f=500$ kHz, in microseconds, is ______.

$t_g=-\dfrac1{2\pi}\dfrac{d\phi}{df}=\dfrac1{2\pi}(2\pi t_0+2\pi\kappa f)=t_0+\kappa f=2\,\mu\mathrm s+(4\times10^{-12})(5\times10^5)=2+2=\mathbf4\ \mu$s. The $f^2$ term makes higher frequencies arrive later: dispersion.

A system has $y(t)=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}x(\tau)\,d\tau$. A bounded input $x(t)=u(t)$ is applied. The system is
  1. stable, since $x$ is bounded
  2. stable, since $h(t)=\delta(t)$
  3. unstable, since the output is a ramp $t\,u(t)$
  4. non-causal

The integrator has $h(t)=u(t)$, so $\int|h|=\infty$. The bounded step produces $y=t\,u(t)$, which grows without limit. (C).

Chapter 1 in one breath

  • Energy $E=\int|x|^2$, power $P=\overline{|x|^2}$; sinusoid power $A^2/2$; powers of different-frequency components add.
  • Linear + time-invariant ⇒ $y=x*h$ (derived from sifting); $h=0$ for $t<0$ ⇔ causal; $\int|h|<\infty$ ⇔ BIBO stable.
  • $e^{j2\pi ft}$ is an eigenfunction: $y=H(f)e^{j2\pi ft}$, $H=\mathcal F\{h\}$. Sinusoid in ⇒ sinusoid out, scaled by $|H|$, shifted by $\angle H$. LTI systems cannot create new frequencies.
  • RC: $f_c=1/2\pi RC$, $t_r=0.35/B$. RLC: $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$, $Q=\omega_0L/R$, $B=f_0/Q$.
  • Distortionless: flat $|H|$ and linear phase. Envelope delay = group delay $-d\phi/d\omega$; carrier delay = phase delay $-\phi/\omega_c$.
  • Real filters approximate ideal ones; Butterworth order $n$ falls $6n$ dB/octave. Oversample to relax analog filters.
  • dB is $10\log$ of power ratio; $3\,\mathrm{dB}\approx2$, $\mathrm{dBm}=\mathrm{dBW}+30$; add dB for cascades.
