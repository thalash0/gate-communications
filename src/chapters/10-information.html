History · Shannon’s miracle year

Before 1948 everybody “knew” that noise forces a trade: to make a message more reliable, send it more slowly, or repeat it over and over. Send the word three times and take a majority vote. Send it a thousand times and the error shrinks, but so does your data rate, towards zero. Reliability costs rate. That was the common sense of the telegraph and telephone engineers.

Claude Shannon, a 32-year-old mathematician at Bell Labs (who also built a juggling robot, a mechanical mouse that solved mazes, and rode a unicycle through the corridors), published A Mathematical Theory of Communication in the Bell System Technical Journal in two instalments in July and October 1948. It did three astonishing things. (1) It defined information as a number, in bits (John Tukey’s coinage), independent of the meaning of the message. (2) It proved that every source has an irreducible compressed size, its entropy (Shannon later said that John von Neumann advised him to use that word, “because nobody knows what entropy really is, so in a debate you will always have the advantage”). (3) It proved that every noisy channel has a capacity $C$, and that for any rate $Ras small as you please, with no need for the rate to fall. Noise limits speed but not accuracy. It took engineers 45 years to build codes (turbo codes, 1993) that really got close.

The first half of the theory (this chapter) has a famous sequel. In 1951 at MIT, Robert Fano gave his students the problem of finding the best code for a set of messages; a student named David Huffman, who had been about to give up, found it. We meet him below.

Real world · where this shows up

ZIP, PNG, JPEG, MP3 and video codecs are source coding (the entropy half of the theory): a 3 MB raw photo becomes 300 kB because the pixels are redundant; a WAV file becomes a 10× smaller MP3 because the ear ignores what it cannot hear (that is lossy coding, with rate–distortion theory in charge). The Voyager probes, 20 billion kilometres away, whisper at about 160 bits per second with a few watts of power, and the signal is recovered on Earth with error-correcting codes within a fraction of a dB of Shannon’s limit (Chapter 11). Every JPEG you view has been Huffman- or arithmetic-coded; every web page you download was probably compressed with gzip/Brotli (LZ77 + Huffman).

Surprise, measured in bits

Suppose I tell you something. How much information did I give? Shannon’s answer starts from three common-sense requirements. (i) An event you were certain of carries no information. (ii) The rarer the event, the more it tells you. (iii) If two independent things happen, the information adds.

Derivation · why information is $-\log p$

Let $f(p)$ be the information in an event of probability $p$, continuous and decreasing, with $f(1)=0$. Independence means probabilities multiply, and (iii) demands $f(pq)=f(p)+f(q)$. Put $p=2^{-u}$, $g(u)=f(2^{-u})$: then $g(u+v)=g(u)+g(v)$, and a continuous solution of this Cauchy equation must be linear, $g(u)=ku$. Hence $f(p)=-k\log_2p$, and choosing $k=1$ fixes the unit as the bit (the information of a fair coin flip, $p=\frac12$). Taking $\ln$ instead gives the nat ($1\ \mathrm{nat}=1.443$ bits), $\log_{10}$ the Hartley. $$I(x)=\log_2\frac1{p(x)}\ \text{ bits}.$$

Your friend says “the coin landed heads”: $p=\frac12$, $I=1$ bit. “I rolled a six”: $\log_26=2.58$ bits. “I won the lottery ($p=10^{-7}$)”: 23 bits. This is the number of yes/no questions you’d need, on average, to pin the event down: the game of “twenty questions” is information theory. Entropy is the average surprise of a source: $$H(X)=\sum_xp(x)\log_2\frac1{p(x)}=-\sum_xp(x)\log_2p(x)\ \text{ bits/symbol}.$$

Top: the surprise $-\log_2p$ of an event with probability $p$: it explodes as $p\to0$. Bottom: the entropy of a coin that lands heads with probability $p$ (the binary entropy function $H_b(p)$): zero for a certain outcome, maximal (1 bit) when it is a fair coin: the most uncertainty a binary source can have.

The inequalities, proved

Derivation · Gibbs’ inequality and the bounds on entropy

Key lemma ($\ln x\le x-1$, equality only at $x=1$). For two probability distributions $p,q$ on the same alphabet, $$\sum_xp(x)\log_2\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\le\frac1{\ln2}\sum_xp(x)\Big(\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}-1\Big)=\frac1{\ln2}\Big(\sum_xq(x)-\sum_xp(x)\Big)=0.$$ Define the relative entropy (Kullback–Leibler divergence) $D(p\|q)=\sum_xp(x)\log_2\dfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}$. The lemma says $D(p\|q)\ge0$, with equality iff $p=q$. Consequences:

  • $0\le H(X)\le\log_2K$. $H\ge0$ because each term $p\log\frac1p\ge0$. Take $q$ uniform, $q=1/K$: $D(p\|q)=\log_2K-H(X)\ge0$, with equality iff $p$ is uniform. (GATE 2024: alphabet of 16 ⇒ max entropy 4 bits.)
  • Chain rule. $\log\frac1{p(x,y)}=\log\frac1{p(x)}+\log\frac1{p(y|x)}$; averaging over $p(x,y)$ gives $H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y\mid X)$.
  • Conditioning reduces entropy. $I(X;Y)\overset{\rm def}=H(X)-H(X\mid Y)=D\big(p(x,y)\,\|\,p(x)p(y)\big)\ge0$, so $H(X\mid Y)\le H(X)$, with equality iff $X,Y$ are independent. (On average, never in a particular case: seeing a particular $y$ can raise your uncertainty.)
  • Subadditivity. $H(X,Y)\le H(X)+H(Y)$, with equality for independent variables.
Properties of entropy (GATE tests them)
  • $0\le H(X)\le\log_2K$ for $K$ outcomes. Equality on the right iff all outcomes are equally likely.
  • Relabelling does not change entropy. $H(X)=H(2X)=H(2^X)=H(g(X))$ for any one-to-one $g$. A many-to-one $g$ (like $X^2$) merges outcomes and can only reduce entropy: $H(g(X))\le H(X)$. (GATE 2022 Q.29.)
  • Independent variables: $H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y)$. In general $H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y\mid X)$ (chain rule), and $H(Y\mid X)\le H(Y)$.
  • A geometric source (“toss until first head”, $p=\frac12$): $H=\sum_{k\ge1}k2^{-k}=2$ bits (GATE 2014).

Worked example · information gained (GATE 2021 Q.51)

A school has equal boys and girls; 75% study Science, 25% Commerce; a Commerce student is twice as likely to be a boy as a Science student. How many bits do you gain on learning that a randomly selected girl studies Commerce? Let $P(\text{boy}\mid\text{Sci})=b$, so $P(\text{boy}\mid\text{Com})=2b$. Boys are half the school: $0.75b+0.25(2b)=0.5\Rightarrow b=0.4$. Then $P(\text{girl}\mid\text{Com})=0.2$, $P(\text{girl}\mid\text{Sci})=0.6$ and $P(\text{Com}\mid\text{girl})=\dfrac{0.25(0.2)}{0.5}=0.1$. Information $=\log_2\frac1{0.1}=\mathbf{3.32}$ bits. (Bayes from Chapter 4, plus the definition of self-information.)

Sources with memory: the entropy rate

English text is not a sequence of independent letters: $q$ is almost always followed by $u$. For a stationary source the right measure is the entropy rate $\mathcal H=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1nH(X_1,\dots,X_n)=\lim H(X_n\mid X_{n-1},\dots,X_1)$ (the surprise of the next symbol given the whole past). For a first-order Markov chain with transition matrix $P$ and stationary distribution $\pi$, $$\mathcal H=\sum_i\pi_i\,H(\text{row }i\text{ of }P).$$

Worked example · a bursty binary source

States $A,B$ with $P(A\to A)=0.9$, $P(B\to A)=0.5$. Stationary: $\pi_A=\frac{0.5}{0.5+0.1}=\frac56$, $\pi_B=\frac16$. $\mathcal H=\frac56H_b(0.1)+\frac16H_b(0.5)=\frac56(0.469)+\frac16(1)=\mathbf{0.557}$ bit/symbol. An i.i.d. source with the same marginals would have $H_b(1/6)=0.650$: the memory saves $14\%$. Shannon’s own experiments with human guessers put the entropy rate of English at about $1$–$1.3$ bits per letter, compared with $\log_227=4.75$ for random letters: English is $\approx75\%$ redundant, which is why a good compressor gets text down to a quarter of its size, and why you can read “Th qck brwn fx”.

Why entropy is the answer: the AEP

Why should the average surprise be the minimum number of bits per symbol? Because of the law of large numbers.

Derivation · the asymptotic equipartition property (AEP) and typical sequences

Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be i.i.d. with pmf $p$. The probability of a sequence is $p(x^n)=\prod p(x_i)$, so $-\frac1n\log_2p(X^n)=\frac1n\sum_i\big[-\log_2p(X_i)\big]$ is the sample mean of the i.i.d. variables $-\log p(X_i)$, whose mean is $H(X)$. By the weak law of large numbers (Chebyshev, Chapter 4), for any $\epsilon>0$ $$P\Big(\Big|-\tfrac1n\log_2p(X^n)-H\Big|>\epsilon\Big)\to0.$$ Define the typical set $A_\epsilon^{(n)}=\{x^n:\ 2^{-n(H+\epsilon)}\le p(x^n)\le2^{-n(H-\epsilon)}\}$. Then: (a) $P(A_\epsilon^{(n)})\to1$; (b) since each typical sequence has probability at least $2^{-n(H+\epsilon)}$ and the total is at most 1, $|A_\epsilon^{(n)}|\le2^{n(H+\epsilon)}$; (c) similarly $|A_\epsilon^{(n)}|\ge(1-\epsilon)2^{n(H-\epsilon)}$ for large $n$.

Reading it: out of the $K^n$ sequences, almost all the probability sits on only $\approx2^{nH}$ of them, all of which are roughly equally likely, each with probability $\approx2^{-nH}$. Number the typical sequences with $nH$ bits, send an escape flag for the (rare) atypical ones, and you have a code with $H$ bits per symbol. Conversely no code can do better, since fewer than $2^{n(H-\epsilon)}$ codewords cover vanishingly little probability. That is Shannon’s source-coding theorem. For a fair coin all $2^n$ sequences are typical and nothing can be compressed; for $p=0.1$ (H=0.469) about $2^{0.469n}$ of the $2^n$ sequences matter: a $53\%$ saving.

Source coding: squeezing out the redundancy

We want to encode each symbol as a string of bits so that the average length is as short as possible, and so that the receiver can decode a continuous stream without punctuation. A code is prefix-free (instantaneous) if no codeword is the beginning of another; then the codewords are the leaves of a binary tree, and decoding is just walking down the tree.

Derivation · the Kraft inequality

Necessity. Let $\ell_{\max}$ be the longest codeword. A codeword of length $\ell_i$ in a prefix code “blocks” all its $2^{\ell_{\max}-\ell_i}$ descendants at depth $\ell_{\max}$; the blocked sets are disjoint (prefix property) and there are only $2^{\ell_{\max}}$ nodes at that depth, so $\sum_i2^{\ell_{\max}-\ell_i}\le2^{\ell_{\max}}$, i.e. $$\sum_{i=1}^{K}2^{-\ell_i}\le1.$$ Sufficiency. If the lengths satisfy it, sort them increasingly and assign each codeword the first unused node at its depth; the inequality guarantees one is available. (The inequality also holds for every uniquely decodable code, by McMillan.) Each codeword of length $\ell$ uses up $2^{-\ell}$ of the tree’s total capacity of 1.

Derivation · $H\le\bar L

Lower bound. For any prefix code, with $q_i=2^{-\ell_i}/c$, $c=\sum2^{-\ell_i}\le1$: $\bar L-H=\sum p_i\ell_i+\sum p_i\log p_i=\sum p_i\log\frac{p_i}{2^{-\ell_i}}=D(p\|q)-\log c\ge0$. Upper bound. Choose the “Shannon lengths” $\ell_i=\lceil\log_2\frac1{p_i}\rceil$. They satisfy Kraft ($\sum2^{-\ell_i}\le\sum p_i=1$), so a prefix code exists, and $\bar L=\sum p_i\ell_i<\sum p_i\big(\log\frac1{p_i}+1\big)=H+1$. Combining, $$\boxed{\,H(X)\ \le\ \bar L\ <\ H(X)+1\,}\qquad\bar L=\sum_ip_i\ell_i,$$ with $\bar L=H$ exactly when all $p_i$ are powers of $\frac12$ (a “dyadic” source: ideal length $\ell_i=\log_2\frac1{p_i}$). Huffman’s algorithm

In 1951, MIT student David Huffman was offered a choice by his professor, Robert Fano: take the final exam, or solve an open problem: find the optimal prefix code. After months he was about to give up and throw out his notes when the answer came to him: build the tree from the bottom. Repeatedly merge the two least probable symbols into one, adding their probabilities, until one node remains. Label the branches 0 and 1. Rare symbols end up deep (long codes), common ones near the root (short codes). Huffman’s code is provably optimal among symbol-by-symbol prefix codes.

Why Huffman is optimal (proof sketch)

(1) In an optimal code, a more probable symbol never has a longer codeword than a less probable one (swapping would reduce $\bar L$). (2) The two longest codewords have the same length and can be assumed to be siblings that differ only in the last bit (otherwise a leaf could be pruned). So the two least probable symbols may be merged into one parent node; (3) the cost of the reduced code differs from the original by the constant $p_a+p_b$, which does not depend on the rest of the tree: so an optimal code for the reduced alphabet extends to an optimal code for the original one. Induction on the alphabet size completes the proof. $\square$

Step through Huffman’s algorithm. Drag “merges done” from 0 to $K-1$. The dendrogram shows which merges have happened; the codeword of each symbol is read off from the root. Compare average length $\bar L$ with the entropy $H$. For the dyadic source they are exactly equal.
Worked example · a non-dyadic source

Probabilities $0.4,\ 0.3,\ 0.1,\ 0.1,\ 0.06,\ 0.04$. Merge $0.06+0.04=0.10$; then $0.1+0.1=0.2$; then $0.1+0.2=0.3$; then $0.3+0.3=0.6$; finally $0.4+0.6=1$. Codeword lengths $(1,2,4,4,4,4)$: $\bar L=0.4(1)+0.3(2)+0.1(4)\cdot2+0.06(4)+0.04(4)=\mathbf{2.20}$ bits, against $H=2.143$ bits: efficiency $H/\bar L=97.4\%$. A fixed-length code would need $\lceil\log_26\rceil=3$ bits.

Worked example · GATE 2023 Q.65

Eight symbols with probabilities $\frac12,\frac14,\frac18,\frac1{16},\frac1{32},\frac1{64},\frac1{128},\frac1{128}$. “The average number of yes/no questions asked in the most efficient sequence” is the Huffman average length $=H$ (dyadic): $\tfrac12(1)+\tfrac14(2)+\tfrac18(3)+\tfrac1{16}(4)+\tfrac1{32}(5)+\tfrac1{64}(6)+2\cdot\tfrac1{128}(7)=\mathbf{1.984}$ (key 1.97–1.99). The optimal question order is “is it a? is it b? …” Probabilities are dyadic, so no cleverness is needed.

Beyond Huffman: arithmetic coding and Lempel–Ziv

Huffman wastes up to 1 bit per symbol (it must use an integer number of bits per symbol) and requires the probabilities in advance. Two ideas remove those limitations.

Arithmetic coding encodes the whole message as one number. Start with the interval $[0,1)$; for each symbol, subdivide the current interval in proportion to the symbol probabilities and keep the sub-interval of the symbol that occurred. The final interval has width $\prod p(x_i)=p(x^n)$, so naming a point inside it needs $\lceil\log_2\frac1{p(x^n)}\rceil\le nH+1$ bits for the whole message: the one-bit loss is shared by all $n$ symbols.

Arithmetic coding of a short message over $\{A,B,C\}$. Each row shows the current interval (bar) refined by the next symbol. The final interval width is $P(\text{message})$; the readout gives the code length $\lceil\log_2(1/P)\rceil$ and compares it with $n\,H$. With $P(A)=0.7$ the message “AB” has $P=0.7\times0.2=0.14$ and needs 3 bits.

Lempel–Ziv (1977, 1978; ZIP, gzip, GIF, PNG): universal codes that learn the source as they go, with no probabilities at all. LZ78 parses the input into the shortest phrase not seen before, and each phrase is sent as (index of its longest earlier prefix, new symbol). Example, “ABBABBABBBAABABAA”: phrases A | B | BA | BB | AB | BBA | ABA | BAA, sent as $(0,A),(0,B),(2,A),(2,B),(1,B),(4,A),(5,A),(3,A)$. The dictionary grows, long repetitions get replaced by short pointers, and for any stationary ergodic source the rate converges to the entropy rate $\mathcal H$. (DEFLATE, the heart of gzip and PNG, is LZ77 followed by Huffman coding.) Run-length coding (fax machines: a page is long runs of white) and Golomb codes (for geometric sources, e.g. prediction residuals in FLAC and in video) are special-purpose cousins.

What if the model is wrong? The cost is $D(p\|q)$

If the true distribution is $p$ but the code is built (Shannon lengths) for a model $q$, the average length is $\sum p_i\lceil\log\frac1{q_i}\rceil\approx\sum p_i\log\frac1{q_i}=H(p)+D(p\|q)$. The relative entropy is the penalty, in bits per symbol, for using the wrong model. Example: a fair-coin source ($p=(\frac12,\frac12)$) coded for $q=(\frac14,\frac34)$ with lengths $(2,0.415)$ costs $\frac12(2)+\frac12(0.415)=1.2075$ bits instead of 1: a penalty of $D=0.2075$ bits (20%). This is why adaptive coders keep tracking the statistics; and why a Huffman table built for English works poorly on Hindi text.

Engineering practice · what real compressors do

Lossless. JPEG’s final stage, MPEG’s final stage and every ZIP file use Huffman or arithmetic coding of transformed data: the transform (DCT, prediction, LZ dictionary) makes the symbols much more skewed, and entropy coding then exploits that. Modern video uses context-adaptive binary arithmetic coding (CABAC). A fundamental limit: no compressor can shrink every file. By counting, there are $2^n$ files of $n$ bits and only $2^n-1$ shorter ones, so any scheme that shortens some files must lengthen others: random (incompressible) data stays the same size, and compressing already-compressed or already-encrypted data does nothing. Lossy. Where an approximation is allowed, we trade rate against distortion (below).

Lossy coding: the rate–distortion function

For analog sources (voice, video) exact reproduction needs an infinite number of bits, so we accept distortion $D$ (e.g. mean-square error) and ask for the minimum rate $R(D)$. Shannon defined it as the minimum mutual information between source $X$ and reproduction $\hat X$ over all test channels with $\E[d(X,\hat X)]\le D$: $$R(D)=\min_{p(\hat x|x):\,\E d\le D}I(X;\hat X).$$

Derivation · rate–distortion of a Gaussian source: $R(D)=\tfrac12\log_2(\sigma^2/D)$

Let $X\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ and squared-error distortion. Choose the “backward” test channel $X=\hat X+Z$ with $\hat X\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2-D)$ and independent $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,D)$: then $\E(X-\hat X)^2=D$ and $$I(X;\hat X)=h(X)-h(X\mid\hat X)=\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\sigma^2)-\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi eD)=\tfrac12\log_2\frac{\sigma^2}D,$$ using the differential entropy $h=\frac12\log2\pi e\sigma^2$ of a Gaussian (Chapter 11); Shannon showed that no other test channel does better. Hence $$\boxed{R(D)=\max\Big(0,\ \tfrac12\log_2\frac{\sigma^2}D\Big)\ \text{bits/sample},\qquad D(R)=\sigma^22^{-2R}.}$$ Each extra bit per sample divides the distortion by 4: $10\log_{10}4=\mathbf{6.02\ dB}$ per bit, the same “6 dB per bit” of PCM (Chapter 12). Scalar uniform quantisers followed by entropy coding come within 0.25 bit ($1.53$ dB) of this bound at high rate; vector quantisation and trellis coding close the rest. For a Bernoulli($p$) source with Hamming distortion, $R(D)=H_b(p)-H_b(D)$ for $D\le\min(p,1-p)$.

Engineering practice · lossy compression in the real world

MP3/AAC discard components below the masking threshold of the ear, so the allowed distortion is shaped in frequency; JPEG quantises DCT coefficients with coarse steps at high spatial frequencies; video codecs (H.264/HEVC) predict each frame from earlier ones and transmit only the quantised residual, reaching 100–300:1 compression at acceptable quality. Voice codecs in phones (AMR, EVS) run at 5–24 kbit/s against the 64 kbit/s of plain PCM, by modelling speech production (linear prediction). All of these are engineering attempts to reach $R(D)$ for sources whose statistics are only approximately known.
