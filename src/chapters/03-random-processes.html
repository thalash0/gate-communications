History · three men, one hiss

Einstein (1905) explained why pollen grains jiggle in water: they are kicked by invisible molecules, so a particle's path is a random process with a precise statistical law. John B. Johnson at Bell Labs found in 1926 that a plain resistor, with nothing connected to it, produces a faint random voltage. His colleague Harry Nyquist saw at once that this was the same phenomenon: the electrons in the metal are doing Brownian motion. In 1928 he derived the open-circuit noise voltage $\overline{v_n^2}=4kTRB$ using nothing but thermodynamics, and the result does not depend on what the resistor is made of.

The final piece came from Norbert Wiener (1930) and, independently, Aleksandr Khinchin (1934): the power spectrum of a random signal is the Fourier transform of its autocorrelation. Wiener's wartime work on predicting aircraft positions from noisy radar became the first optimal filter, an ancestor of the matched filter of Chapter 10.

Real world · the noise floor you can calculate

At room temperature ($T_0=290\,$K) the thermal noise density available from any resistor is $kT_0=4\times10^{-21}\,$W/Hz, which is $-174$ dBm/Hz. Over a 1 MHz receiver bandwidth that is $-174+60=-114$ dBm. Every received signal in every Wi-Fi router, GPS receiver and phone has to climb above this number. Its closeness to the true limit is the reason receivers are cooled (radio telescopes use liquid helium) and why the first amplifier in any receiver is the most important component.

A random process is a bag of waveforms

Pick a random outcome $\omega$ (the "universe"). It determines an entire waveform $x(t;\omega)$. Run the experiment again, get another waveform. The collection of all possible waveforms with their probabilities is the ensemble. Two ways to see a random process:

  • Vertical slice: freeze time at $t_0$; the values $X(t_0)$ across the ensemble form an ordinary random variable.
  • Horizontal slice: freeze the outcome; you get one ordinary waveform ("sample function" or "realisation").
Fifty sample functions (thin) and the distribution of values across the ensemble at the chosen time $t_0$ (histogram, bottom). The three "well-behaved" processes look the same at every $t_0$. The random-amplitude cosine does not: at $t_0=0.25$ every realisation crosses zero, so its variance there is zero.

Mean, autocorrelation, and what "stationary" means

For a process $X(t)$ define $$\mu_X(t)=\E[X(t)],\qquad R_X(t_1,t_2)=\E[X(t_1)X(t_2)].$$ $R_X$ answers: how much does the value now tell me about the value $t_2-t_1$ seconds later? A process is wide-sense stationary (WSS) when (i) the mean is constant and (ii) the autocorrelation depends only on the time difference $\tau=t_2-t_1$: $$\mu_X(t)=\mu_X,\qquad R_X(t,t+\tau)=R_X(\tau).$$ (Strict-sense stationarity demands that all joint distributions be shift-invariant; for Gaussian processes the two notions coincide.) Almost everything we use in communications, receiver noise and random data streams, is modelled as WSS.

Worked example · random-phase sinusoid

$X(t)=A\cos(2\pi f_0t+\Theta)$ with $\Theta\sim U[0,2\pi)$. The mean is $\E[\cos(\cdot+\Theta)]=0$ for every $t$. Using $\cos a\cos b=\tfrac12[\cos(a-b)+\cos(a+b)]$: $$R_X(t,t+\tau)=\frac{A^2}2\cos(2\pi f_0\tau)+\frac{A^2}{2}\underbrace{\E[\cos(4\pi f_0t+2\pi f_0\tau+2\Theta)]}_{=0\ \text{(uniform phase averages out)}}=\frac{A^2}{2}\cos(2\pi f_0\tau).$$ It is WSS, with power $R_X(0)=A^2/2$. This is precisely GATE 2014's question for $X=\sqrt2\sin(2\pi t+\varphi)$: $R=\cos 2\pi(t_1-t_2)$.

Worked example · the random binary wave

Every $T$ seconds, independently, the signal is $+A$ or $-A$ with equal probability, with a random start offset $T_d\sim U[0,T)$ (so that it is stationary). Two samples $\tau$ apart fall in the same bit with probability $1-|\tau|/T$ (then product $=A^2$) and in different, independent bits otherwise (average product 0). Hence $$R_X(\tau)=A^2\Big(1-\frac{|\tau|}T\Big)\ \text{ for }|\tau|\le T,\qquad 0\text{ otherwise}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_X(\tau)=A^2\,\tri(\tau/T).$$ This triangle is the autocorrelation of every NRZ data stream. Chapter 9 reuses it.

Trap · "ergodic" and "stationary" are different words

Ensemble averages ($\E[\cdot]$ over realisations) equal time averages over a single long realisation only for ergodic processes. That is what lets us measure the power of a noise source from one long recording. Not every stationary process is ergodic: the process "$X(t)=C$ where $C$ is a random constant" is stationary but a single realisation tells you only about its own $C$.

What an autocorrelation function looks like (and what it cannot be)

Properties of $R_X(\tau)$ for a real WSS process
  1. Even: $R_X(-\tau)=R_X(\tau)$.
  2. Peak at the origin: $|R_X(\tau)|\le R_X(0)$, and $R_X(0)=\E[X^2]$ is the total average power.
  3. Tail = DC power: if $X$ has no periodic component, $R_X(\infty)=\mu_X^2$ (the DC power). Hence AC power (variance) $=R_X(0)-R_X(\infty)$.
  4. If $X(t)$ has a periodic component, $R_X$ has a periodic component of the same period.
  5. Non-negative definite: its Fourier transform must be $\ge0$ at every frequency. This is the sneaky one. $\rect(\tau)$ is even, peaks at zero, but its transform is $\sinc(f)$ which goes negative, so a rectangle can never be an autocorrelation; the triangle can (its transform is $\sinc^2\ge0$).

Example: $R_X(\tau)=9+16e^{-|\tau|}$. DC power $=R(\infty)=9$ so $\mu=\pm3$; total power $R(0)=25$; variance $=25-9=16$.

Power spectral density: where in frequency is the power?

The Wiener–Khinchin theorem says: the autocorrelation and the power spectral density are a Fourier-transform pair, $$S_X(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R_X(\tau)e^{-j2\pi f\tau}d\tau,\qquad R_X(\tau)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S_X(f)e^{j2\pi f\tau}df .$$ Evaluate at the origin and you get the most-used identity of the chapter: $$\boxed{\ \text{Average power}=R_X(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S_X(f)\,df=\E[X^2]\ }$$ $S_X(f)\ge0$ (W/Hz) and, for real processes, even in $f$. Its meaning: pass $X$ through a narrow ideal band-pass filter around $f_1$ of width $\Delta f$; the output power is $\approx 2\,S_X(f_1)\Delta f$ (the factor 2 collects $+f_1$ and $-f_1$).

Symbols with memory: the PSD of a correlated pulse train

Chapter 9 will need this. Suppose the pulse amplitudes $b_n$ are not independent but have autocorrelation $R_b[m]=\E[b_nb_{n-m}]$. A pulse train $X(t)=\sum_nb_n\,g(t-nT)$ (made stationary with a random delay) has $$S_X(f)=\frac{|G(f)|^2}{T}\;\underbrace{\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}R_b[m]\,e^{-j2\pi fmT}}_{S_b(f)}.$$ The first factor is the pulse's own spectrum, the second the "spectrum of the symbol sequence". For independent $\pm1$ symbols $R_b[m]=\delta_m$ and $S_b=1$, recovering $|G|^2/T$. Correlation sculpts the spectrum. Example: $b_n=\alpha_n+k\,\alpha_{n-3}$ with $\alpha_n=\pm1$ independent. Then $R_b[0]=1+k^2$, $R_b[\pm3]=k$, others 0, so $S_b(f)=1+k^2+2k\cos(2\pi f\cdot3T)$. At $f=\frac1{3T}$ the cosine is $1$ and $S_b=(1+k)^2$: a spectral null at $f=1/3T$ requires $k=-1$ (this is GATE 2016's question). Line codes like AMI and duobinary are exactly this idea: choose the correlation so that the nulls fall where you want (at DC for AMI).

White noise

If a process has a flat PSD $S(f)=N_0/2$ for all $f$, its autocorrelation is $\frac{N_0}2\delta(\tau)$: values at any two different instants are uncorrelated, however close. That is "white" (all frequencies, like white light). An ideal white process has infinite power $\int\frac{N_0}2df=\infty$, which no physical source can have, but thermal noise is white to well beyond $10^{12}$ Hz, far more than any receiver bandwidth, so the idealisation is excellent. The factor $\tfrac12$ is a convention: $N_0/2$ is the two-sided density (spread over $-\infty0$), and for thermal noise $N_0=kT$.

AWGN

Additive White Gaussian Noise: $r(t)=s(t)+n(t)$, where $n(t)$ is a zero-mean white Gaussian process with PSD $N_0/2$. After any filter of bandwidth $B$ its samples are Gaussian with variance $\sigma^2=\frac{N_0}2\times(\text{filter's noise bandwidth in two-sided Hz})$. For an ideal low-pass of cut-off $B$: $\sigma^2=\frac{N_0}2\cdot2B=N_0B$.

Through a linear time-invariant system

A WSS process into an LTI system with transfer function $H(f)$ yields a WSS output with $$\mu_Y=\mu_XH(0),\qquad S_Y(f)=|H(f)|^2S_X(f),\qquad R_Y=R_X*h*\tilde h\ \ (\tilde h(t)=h(-t)).$$ Note: only the magnitude of $H$ matters for the PSD, not its phase. (GATE 2022 Q.23 hid exactly this fact: with only $|H(f)|$ given, one cannot say the system is a pure delay, yet $S_Y=S_X$ in the pass-band.)

White noise (grey) filtered by an RC low-pass, an ideal low-pass or an ideal band-pass. The output looks smoother as the bandwidth falls: filtering is correlating neighbouring samples. Below, the output PSD is $|H|^2$ times the flat input PSD; its area is the output power, which the readout compares against the variance measured on the sample.
Worked example · the most-tested calculation in this book

White noise of two-sided PSD $N_0/2$ through the RC low-pass $H(f)=\dfrac1{1+j2\pi fRC}$. Output power: $$P_Y=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{N_0}{2}\frac{df}{1+(2\pi fRC)^2}=\frac{N_0}2\cdot\frac1{2RC}=\boxed{\frac{N_0}{4RC}}.$$ (using $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{df}{1+(af)^2}=\frac\pi a$ with $a=2\pi RC$.) Equivalently use the time domain: $P_Y=\frac{N_0}2\int h^2(t)\,dt$ with $h=\frac1{RC}e^{-t/RC}u(t)$, and $\int h^2=\frac1{2RC}$. ✓. This is GATE 2024 Q.20, and GATE 2023 Q.59 is the same calculation with $h(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ and $N_0/2=\tfrac12$: $P=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=0.25$ W.

Fast rule: "white noise through anything" $$P_{\rm out}=\frac{N_0}2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|H(f)|^2df=\frac{N_0}2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h^2(t)\,dt=\frac{N_0}2\,E_h .$$ For a linear functional $Y=\int w(t)\phi(t)\,dt$ of white noise: $\Var Y=\frac{N_0}2\int\phi^2(t)\,dt$. (GATE 2020: $S_W=3$ W/Hz and $\phi$ a unit-width pulse gives variance $3\times$ the pulse energy.)

Noise-equivalent bandwidth. The ideal low-pass that passes the same noise power as a real filter of peak gain $|H(0)|=1$ has $B_N=\int_0^\infty|H|^2df$. For the RC filter, $B_N=\frac1{4RC}=\frac\pi2f_{3\mathrm{dB}}$: a real filter lets through about 57% more noise than the "3-dB bandwidth" suggests.

Gaussian processes and narrowband noise

A process is Gaussian if every set of samples $X(t_1),\dots,X(t_k)$ is jointly Gaussian. Two properties explain why we love it: (i) a linear operation on a Gaussian process (filtering, integrating, sampling) gives a Gaussian result; (ii) a WSS Gaussian process is fully specified by $\mu$ and $R_X$, and for white Gaussian noise uncorrelated samples are independent. The second fact is why in digital detection the noise samples of different symbol intervals can be treated independently.

Receiver noise after a band-pass filter around the carrier $f_c$ is narrowband noise, and has the same form as any bandpass signal (Chapter 2): $$n(t)=n_c(t)\cos2\pi f_ct-n_s(t)\sin2\pi f_ct=r(t)\cos(2\pi f_ct+\psi(t)).$$ For narrowband Gaussian noise of power $\sigma^2$: $n_c$ and $n_s$ are independent zero-mean Gaussians, each with variance $\sigma^2$ (the same as $n$ itself); the envelope $r=\sqrt{n_c^2+n_s^2}$ is Rayleigh distributed and the phase $\psi$ is uniform on $[0,2\pi)$. (Check: $\overline{n^2}=\frac12(\overline{n_c^2}+\overline{n_s^2})\cdot$... in the cos/sin form the cross terms vanish and $\overline{n^2}=\tfrac12\sigma^2+\tfrac12\sigma^2=\sigma^2$ ✓.)

Left: 2500 samples of $(n_c,n_s)$: a round Gaussian cloud. Right: the histogram of the distance from the origin (the envelope) against the Rayleigh pdf $\frac r{\sigma^2}e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}$. A cloud that is round in two variables becomes a pdf that starts at zero in the radius: a Rayleigh. This envelope law is the starting point for noise in envelope detectors (Chapter 6) and for fading channels.

Noise figure and cascades (why the first amplifier matters)

Real amplifiers add their own noise. The noise figure $F=\dfrac{\mathrm{SNR}_{\rm in}}{\mathrm{SNR}_{\rm out}}\ge1$ (as a ratio; in dB, $NF=10\log_{10}F$) says by how much the SNR degrades. An equivalent description is the noise temperature $T_e=(F-1)T_0$. For a chain of stages with gains $G_1,G_2,\dots$ and noise figures $F_1,F_2,\dots$ (all as ratios), the Friis formula is $$F_{\rm tot}=F_1+\frac{F_2-1}{G_1}+\frac{F_3-1}{G_1G_2}+\cdots,\qquad T_{e,\rm tot}=T_1+\frac{T_2}{G_1}+\frac{T_3}{G_1G_2}+\cdots$$ A passive attenuator of loss $L$ (gain $1/L$) at $T_0$ has $F=L$. The lesson of the formula: noise added late is divided by all the gain before it, so put a low-noise, high-gain stage first.

A three-stage receiver (LNA → mixer → IF amplifier). The bar shows how much each stage contributes to the total noise figure. Make the LNA gain small and watch the later stages take over; make it large and they almost vanish. (Satellite dishes put the LNA directly at the feed for exactly this reason.)

Let $X(t)$ be a WSS random process with PSD $S_X(f)$. If $Y(t)=X(2t-1)$, the PSD of $Y$ is
  1. $\tfrac12S_X(\tfrac f2)e^{-j\pi f}$
  2. $\tfrac12S_X(\tfrac f2)e^{-j\pi f/2}$
  3. $\tfrac12S_X(\tfrac f2)$
  4. $S_X(2f)$ (scaled)

The shift by 1 only adds a phase factor to the Fourier transform, but a PSD ignores phase (it is the transform of an autocorrelation, and $R_Y(\tau)=R_X(2\tau)$ is unaffected by a constant shift). Time compression by 2 stretches the spectrum: $R_Y(\tau)=R_X(2\tau)\Rightarrow S_Y(f)=\tfrac12S_X(f/2)$. (C). A great "shifts never matter" question.

Consider $f(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n\,p(t-nT)$, with $p(t)=1$ on $[0,0.5T]$ and 0 otherwise, and $a_n$ pairwise independent, zero-mean, unit-variance. (i) The mean of $f(t)$ is independent of $t$. (ii) $\E[f(t)f(t+\tau)]$ is independent of $t$ for all $\tau$. Which is correct?
  1. (i) TRUE, (ii) FALSE
  2. Both TRUE
  3. Both FALSE
  4. (i) FALSE, (ii) TRUE

Mean $=\sum\E[a_n]p(\cdot)=0$ for every $t$: (i) true. Autocorrelation: $\E[f(t)f(t+\tau)]=\sum_np(t-nT)p(t+\tau-nT)$ (cross terms vanish). At $\tau=0$ this equals $1$ while $t$ is inside a pulse and $0$ in the gap: it depends on $t$. The process is cyclostationary (statistics repeat every $T$), not WSS. (ii) false. (A). Chapter 3 in one breath

  • Process = ensemble of waveforms; WSS means constant mean and $R_X$ depending on $\tau$ only.
  • $R_X(0)$ = power; $R_X(\infty)$ = DC power; $R_X$ is even, peaks at 0, transform $\ge0$.
  • Wiener–Khinchin: $S_X=\mathcal F\{R_X\}$, $\int S_X\,df=R_X(0)$. Sine ⇒ two lines; NRZ bits ⇒ $A^2T\sinc^2(fT)$; white ⇒ flat.
  • Through LTI: $S_Y=|H|^2S_X$ (phase irrelevant). White noise through RC ⇒ $N_0/4RC$; generally $P=\frac{N_0}2\int h^2$.
  • Thermal noise: $N_0=kT$, $-174$ dBm/Hz; AWGN after bandwidth $B$: $\sigma^2=N_0B$.
  • Narrowband Gaussian noise: $n_c,n_s$ independent, variance $\sigma^2$ each; envelope Rayleigh, phase uniform.
  • Friis: $F=F_1+\frac{F_2-1}{G_1}+\cdots$, so the front end decides the noise performance.
