What you will be able to do after this chapter
  • Describe any digital passband modulation by its constellation; compute symbol energy, $E_b$, $d_{\min}$, bits per symbol and bandwidth.
  • Derive the coherent receiver and the error probabilities of BPSK, QPSK, $M$-PSK, $M$-QAM and coherent FSK from the detection theory of Chapter 14; derive the non-coherent results for DPSK ($\frac12e^{-E_b/N_0}$) and non-coherent FSK ($\frac12e^{-E_b/2N_0}$) from the Rayleigh/Rice laws.
  • Explain OQPSK, $\pi/4$-QPSK, MSK and GMSK, the envelope fluctuation problem and the PSD of each; derive the orthogonality conditions of FSK and MSK’s equivalence to offset QPSK.
  • Compare the schemes on the power–bandwidth plane and relate them to the Shannon limit; choose a scheme for a given channel.
  • Estimate the effect of carrier-phase error, nonlinear amplifiers and Gray labelling on BER.

Prerequisites: Chapters 3 (I/Q), 4 (Rayleigh, Rice), 13 (RRC), 14 (detection).

History · from telegraph clicks to 1024-QAM

The oldest digital modulation is ASK: Samuel Morse’s key switching a current on and off (1844), later a radio spark. Frequency-shift keying arrived with early radioteletype and the Bell 103 modem (1962; two tones for each direction, 300 bit/s). Phase-shift keying came with the space race: Mariner and Voyager use BPSK because it gets the lowest error rate for the little power they can spare. Then in the 1960s AT&T and Codex realised that phase and amplitude could be varied together: QAM. The V.29 fax modem (9600 bit/s, 1976) used 16-QAM; V.32 (1984) added the trellis-coded modulation of Gottfried Ungerboeck (1982), which married the error-correcting codes of Chapter 17 to the constellation, squeezing 33.6 kbit/s out of the ordinary phone line, within a few kbit/s of Shannon’s limit (Chapter 11). Today your Wi-Fi 6 router sends 1024 points per symbol (10 bits).

The bandpass signal in the IQ plane

From Chapter 3, any digital carrier signal can be written $$s(t)=\sum_k\Big[I_k\,p(t-kT)\cos2\pi f_ct-Q_k\,p(t-kT)\sin2\pi f_ct\Big],$$ where $(I_k,Q_k)$ is the $k$-th symbol, a point in the I–Q plane, and $p(t)$ the pulse shape (rectangular, or the raised-cosine of Chapter 13). The collection of allowed points is the constellation. Everything follows from the picture:

  • $M$ points ⇒ $\log_2M$ bits per symbol: $R_b=R_s\log_2M$.
  • The distance of a point from the origin is the amplitude; its angle is the phase. For a rectangular pulse of unit height the energy of symbol $k$ is $E_k=\frac T2(I_k^2+Q_k^2)$ (the $\tfrac12$ from the carrier, Chapter 3), so $E_s=\overline{E_k}$ and $E_b=E_s/\log_2M$.
  • Noise is a round Gaussian cloud around each point (Chapter 14). Error probability is dominated by the minimum distance $d_{\min}$ between points: for a given $E_s$ we want the points spread as far apart as possible.
  • The signal’s power spectrum is the baseband spectrum of $p(t)$ shifted to $\pm f_c$: bandwidth $2\times$ the baseband bandwidth. With a raised-cosine, $B=(1+\alpha)R_s$.
Derivation · the coherent quadrature receiver

The orthonormal basis for any $(I,Q)$ constellation with a unit-energy pulse is $\phi_1(t)=\sqrt{\frac2T}p(t)\cos2\pi f_ct$, $\phi_2(t)=-\sqrt{\frac2T}p(t)\sin2\pi f_ct$ (orthogonal because $\int\cos2\pi f_ct\sin2\pi f_ct=0$ and $\int\cos^2=\frac T2$, for $f_cT$ an integer or $f_c\gg1/T$). The receiver multiplies the incoming signal by $\sqrt{2/T}\cos2\pi f_ct$ and by $-\sqrt{2/T}\sin2\pi f_ct$, integrates over $T$ (the matched filter for a rectangular pulse) and samples: the outputs are the coordinates $(r_I,r_Q)=(I_k\sqrt{T/2}+n_I,\ Q_k\sqrt{T/2}+n_Q)$ with independent noises of variance $N_0/2$. A decision device then picks the nearest constellation point. Every scheme in this chapter is a different set of points fed to this one receiver (FSK needs a different basis: one correlator per tone).

The family, one by one

ASK / OOK

$s(t)=A\,m(t)\cos2\pi f_ct$, $m\in\{0,1\}$. Simple, but the two points are $0$ and $A$: the “1” must carry all the distance, so for the same average energy it behaves like orthogonal signalling: the points are $0$ and $\sqrt{2E_b}$ (average energy $E_b$), $d^2=2E_b$, $P_b=Q\big(\sqrt{E_b/N_0}\big)$ (coherent) with $E_b$ the average energy: 3 dB worse than BPSK. It can be detected without a carrier reference by an envelope detector (non-coherent) at a small further penalty. Bandwidth $=2R_b$ (null-to-null with rectangular pulses). In optical links, on–off keying is the norm because a laser is either on or off.

BPSK

Derivation · BPSK error probability

$s=\pm A\cos2\pi f_ct$ over $0\le t\sqrt{E_b})=Q\big(\sqrt{E_b}/\sqrt{N_0/2}\big)$: $$P_b=Q\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{2E_b}{N_0}}\Big)=\tfrac12\erfc\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{E_b}{N_0}}\Big).$$ The same result from the pairwise formula of Chapter 14: $d=2\sqrt{E_b}$, $Q(d/\sqrt{2N_0})=Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})$ ✓. For $P_b=10^{-5}$, $\sqrt{2E_b/N_0}=4.265$, $E_b/N_0=9.59$ dB.

Bandwidth (null-to-null, rectangular pulses) $=2R_b$; with raised-cosine $(1+\alpha)R_b$. It needs a coherent reference (a Costas loop, Chapter 16) and has a phase ambiguity: the recovered carrier could be inverted (resolved by differential encoding, which costs a factor $\approx2$ in BER: an error in either of two consecutive decisions flips the decoded bit, so $P_b\approx2Q(\cdot)$, a loss of only ≈0.2 dB at $10^{-5}$). A phase error $\varphi$ in the recovered carrier scales the useful signal by $\cos\varphi$: $P_b=Q\big(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0}\cos\varphi\big)$: a constant $20^\circ$ error costs $20\log\cos20^\circ=0.54$ dB; at $45^\circ$ it costs 3 dB. This is why carrier-recovery accuracy limits the SNR of coherent systems.

DPSK

Encode the bit in the change of phase from the previous symbol (a “1” means “flip”, “0” means “don’t”). The receiver multiplies each symbol by the previous one: no carrier recovery (the carrier phase need only be constant over two symbols). The price: the previous symbol is itself noisy, so noise appears twice.

Derivation · DPSK as non-coherent orthogonal signalling

Let the matched-filter outputs of two successive symbols be the complex numbers $r_k=\sqrt{E_b}e^{j\theta_k}+n_k$ and $r_{k-1}=\sqrt{E_b}e^{j\theta_{k-1}}+n_{k-1}$, where $n_k,n_{k-1}$ are independent complex noises with $\E|n|^2=N_0$ (variance $N_0/2$ per quadrature), and the unknown carrier phase is common to both. The decision statistic is $\mathrm{Re}\{r_kr_{k-1}^*\}=\frac14\big(|r_k+r_{k-1}|^2-|r_k-r_{k-1}|^2\big)$: decide “no change” if $|r_k+r_{k-1}|>|r_k-r_{k-1}|$. Form the normalised combinations $u=(r_k+r_{k-1})/\sqrt2$ and $v=(r_k-r_{k-1})/\sqrt2$: they carry independent noises of the same variance as a single sample (an orthogonal rotation of two independent noises). If the phase did not change, $u$ contains the signal $\sqrt{2E_b}\,e^{j\theta}$ and $v$ contains noise only. So the receiver is exactly a non-coherent comparison of two orthogonal branches, one of which carries a signal of energy $2E_b$, in noise $N_0$. Using the non-coherent result derived below ($P=\frac12e^{-E_{\rm on}/2N_0}$) with $E_{\rm on}=2E_b$: $$P_b^{\rm DPSK}=\tfrac12e^{-E_b/N_0}.$$ At $10^{-5}$: $E_b/N_0=\ln(5\times10^4)=10.82$, i.e. $10.34$ dB: 0.75 dB worse than coherent BPSK (9.59 dB), but with a receiver that needs no carrier recovery and is immune to slow phase drift. (The exponent $E_b/N_0$ instead of BPSK’s $\approx E_b/N_0$ inside a $Q$: the previous symbol is a noisy reference, which costs about 0.75 dB; for very low error rates DPSK is nearly as good as BPSK.)

QPSK

Four points $(\pm1,\pm1)\sqrt{E_s/2}$: two BPSK signals in quadrature (the quadrature-carrier multiplexing of Chapter 6). Each bit rides its own orthogonal carrier, so they don’t interfere, and each sees exactly the BPSK noise problem with the same $E_b$ (each carries one bit with energy $E_s/2=E_b$): $$P_b=Q\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{2E_b}{N_0}}\Big)\quad(\text{identical to BPSK}),\qquad P_s=1-\big(1-Q(\sqrt{E_s/N_0})\big)^2=2Q-Q^2\approx2Q\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{E_s}{N_0}}\Big).$$ (The two decisions are independent because the two noises $n_I,n_Q$ are independent.) With Gray labelling a symbol error to a neighbour flips one bit, so $P_b=P_s/2$ to first order, consistent with $P_b=Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})$. Because the symbol rate is half the bit rate, QPSK uses half the bandwidth of BPSK at the same BER: free efficiency.

Engineering practice · OQPSK, $\pi/4$-QPSK and the envelope problem

A QPSK signal switches phase by 0°, ±90° or 180° at each symbol edge. When it passes through a band-limited filter the 180° transitions drive the envelope through zero. Peaks of 2–3 dB above the average (PAPR) force a power amplifier to run with “back-off”, wasting power, or else a nonlinear amplifier clips them and regrows the sidelobes the filter had removed. OQPSK (offset QPSK) delays the Q stream by $T_s/2$ so that only one of I, Q changes at a time and phase jumps are at most 90°: the envelope never goes through zero, PAPR falls by ≈1 dB; used in satellite and CDMA (IS-95 reverse link, ZigBee). $\pi/4$-DQPSK alternates between two QPSK constellations rotated by 45°, so transitions are $\pm45^\circ$ or $\pm135^\circ$: never 180°. Used in North American TDMA (IS-136), PDC in Japan and TETRA, with differential demodulation (no carrier recovery). Extreme case: MSK, below, has a constant envelope.

$M$-PSK

Derivation · $M$-PSK

$M$ points on a circle of radius $\sqrt{E_s}$, at angles $2\pi i/M$. Adjacent points are $d_{\min}=2\sqrt{E_s}\sin(\pi/M)$ apart, and each has two nearest neighbours: $P_s\approx2Q\Big(\dfrac{d_{\min}}{\sqrt{2N_0}}\Big)=2Q\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{2E_s}{N_0}}\sin\tfrac\pi M\Big)$ and (Gray) $P_b\approx P_s/\log_2M$. For $M=2$ the formula reduces to $Q$ (one neighbour, not two), for $M=4$ to $2Q(\sqrt{E_s/N_0})$ ✓. Doubling $M$ reduces $\sin(\pi/M)$ by almost 2, i.e. costs about 6 dB in $E_s/N_0$ (less per bit: $E_b=E_s/\log_2M$ gives ≈ 4–5 dB for large $M$): the points crowd on a circle (8-PSK 13.0 dB, 16-PSK 17.4 dB at $10^{-5}$).

QAM

Put the points on a square grid: $\sqrt M\times\sqrt M$ levels of $I$ and $Q$. Because the grid fills the plane, it packs points much more tightly than a ring.

Derivation · energy and error probability of square $M$-QAM

Each axis is $\sqrt M$-PAM with spacing $d$: levels $\pm\frac d2,\pm\frac{3d}2,\dots$ with mean square $\frac{(\sqrt M)^2-1}{12}d^2=\frac{(M-1)d^2}{12}$ per axis, so $E_s=2\cdot\frac{(M-1)d^2}{12}\cdot\frac T2\!\!\to\;E_s=\frac{(M-1)d^2}{6}$ in the normalised units where a point’s squared distance from the origin equals its symbol energy (check: QPSK, $M=4$: $E_s=d^2/2$ ✓). The decisions on I and Q are independent PAM decisions: each is correct with probability $1-P_{\sqrt M}$, $P_{\sqrt M}=2\big(1-\frac1{\sqrt M}\big)Q\big(\frac d{\sqrt{2N_0}}\big)$ (Chapter 14), so $P_s=1-(1-P_{\sqrt M})^2\approx2P_{\sqrt M}$: $$P_s\approx4\Big(1-\frac1{\sqrt M}\Big)Q\Big(\sqrt{\frac{3E_s}{(M-1)N_0}}\Big),\qquad P_b\approx\frac4{\log_2M}\Big(1-\frac1{\sqrt M}\Big)Q\Big(\sqrt{\frac{3\log_2M}{M-1}\frac{E_b}{N_0}}\Big).$$

The price of density is power: each doubling of $M$ (one more bit/symbol) costs about 3 dB more $E_b/N_0$ at high $M$ (16-QAM 13.4 dB, 64-QAM 17.8 dB, 256-QAM 22.5 dB, 1024-QAM 27.5 dB at $10^{-5}$), and the receiver needs ever cleaner SNR, linear amplifiers (the signal has amplitude variations: high peak-to-average power ratio) and accurate phase tracking. This is why 256-QAM is used on short, clean links (cable, home Wi-Fi) while the deep-space link uses BPSK. For odd numbers of bits (32-QAM, 128-QAM) cross-shaped constellations are used. On a satellite channel, where the travelling-wave tube is driven near saturation, the amplitude variations of QAM would be distorted, so DVB-S2 uses APSK (amplitude-phase shift keying: 2–4 concentric rings, e.g. 16-APSK = 4+12) as a compromise between PSK’s constant envelope and QAM’s density.

FSK

Send one of $M$ tones. In signal space the $M$ tones are $M$ orthogonal axes: points at distance $\sqrt{2E_s}$ from each other. The question is how close the tones may be.

Derivation · tone orthogonality and error probability

Two tones of frequencies $f_1,f_2$ over a symbol of length $T$: $\int_0^T\cos2\pi f_1t\cos2\pi f_2t\,dt=\frac12\Big[\frac{\sin2\pi(f_1-f_2)T}{2\pi(f_1-f_2)}+\frac{\sin2\pi(f_1+f_2)T}{2\pi(f_1+f_2)}\Big]$. The second term is negligible for $f\gg1/T$; the first vanishes when $2(f_1-f_2)T$ is a non-zero integer: coherent detection (phases known) needs spacing $\Delta f=\dfrac{k}{2T}$, minimum $\dfrac1{2T}$. If the phase of each tone is unknown (non-coherent), orthogonality must hold for every phase: the quadrature component must vanish too, giving $\Delta f=\dfrac kT$, minimum $\dfrac1T$. With coherent orthogonal tones the error probability is the orthogonal-signalling result of Chapter 14: $P_b=Q\big(\sqrt{E_b/N_0}\big)$ (3 dB worse than BPSK), and for $M$-ary $P_s\le(M-1)Q(\sqrt{E_s/N_0})$. Bandwidth: $M$ tones spaced $\frac1{2T}$ apart (coherent) occupy $\approx\frac{M}{2T}$ plus edges.

Derivation · non-coherent binary orthogonal detection: $P_b=\frac12e^{-E_b/2N_0}$

Without carrier phase the receiver cannot correlate with $\cos$ alone; it correlates each tone with both $\cos$ and $\sin$ and forms the envelope $R_i=\sqrt{r_{Ic}^2+r_{Is}^2}$ of branch $i$ (an “energy detector”), choosing the larger. If tone 1 was sent, branch 1 contains the signal and noise: its envelope $R_1$ is Rician (Chapter 4) with $A^2=E_b$ and per-quadrature variance $\sigma^2=N_0/2$; branch 2 contains noise only: Rayleigh, $P(R_2>r)=e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}$. An error occurs when $R_2>R_1$: $$P_b=\int_0^\infty f_{R_1}(r)\,e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}dr=\frac1{\sigma^2}e^{-E_b/2\sigma^2}\int_0^\infty r\,e^{-r^2/\sigma^2}I_0\!\Big(\frac{r\sqrt{E_b}}{\sigma^2}\Big)dr .$$ Using $\int_0^\infty re^{-ar^2}I_0(br)dr=\frac1{2a}e^{b^2/4a}$ with $a=1/\sigma^2$, $b=\sqrt{E_b}/\sigma^2$, the integral is $\frac{\sigma^2}2e^{E_b/4\sigma^2}$, so $$\boxed{P_b=\tfrac12e^{-E_b/4\sigma^2}=\tfrac12\exp\!\Big(-\frac{E_b}{2N_0}\Big)}.$$ At $10^{-5}$: $E_b/N_0=2\ln(5\times10^4)=21.6=13.35$ dB. Compared with coherent BFSK (12.6 dB) the non-coherent penalty is only 0.75 dB.

Three-tone orthogonal FSK lives in 3-D signal space: the three tones are the three axes and the three signal points sit at distance $\sqrt{E_s}$ along each. The noise cloud (500 samples around the selected symbol) is a round ball. The decision boundaries are the planes equidistant from two axes (e.g. $x=y$). Increase the noise and watch points cross the planes. Neighbouring signal points are $\sqrt{2E_s}$ apart, versus $2\sqrt{E_s}$ for antipodal signals: the 3 dB penalty of Chapter 14.

But watch the trade for large $M$: more tones means more dimensions (more bandwidth!) and better power efficiency: with $M\to\infty$ the required $E_b/N_0\to-1.59$ dB, the Shannon limit (Chapter 14), at the cost of bandwidth $\to\infty$.

Continuous-phase FSK, MSK and GMSK

Switching between two oscillators causes phase jumps and hence wide sidelobes. Continuous-phase FSK (CPFSK) makes the phase continuous: $s(t)=A\cos\big[2\pi f_ct+\theta(t)\big]$ with $\dot\theta=\pm\pi h/T$ for bit $\pm1$, where $h=2\Delta f\,T$ is the modulation index. Over one bit the phase moves by $\pm\pi h$.

Derivation · MSK is OQPSK with half-sine pulses

MSK is CPFSK with $h=\frac12$ ($\Delta f=\frac1{4T}$, tones spaced $\frac1{2T}$: the minimum for orthogonality): the phase advances by exactly $\pm\frac\pi2$ in each bit, in a straight line. Write $s(t)=A\cos(2\pi f_ct+b_k\frac{\pi t}{2T}+\varphi_k)$ for $kT\le t<(k+1)T$ and expand: with $\cos(\omega_ct+\theta)=\cos\theta\cos\omega_ct-\sin\theta\sin\omega_ct$, one finds $$s(t)=a_I(t)\cos\Big(\frac{\pi t}{2T}\Big)\cos2\pi f_ct-a_Q(t)\sin\Big(\frac{\pi t}{2T}\Big)\sin2\pi f_ct,$$ where $a_I,a_Q=\pm1$ are the even- and odd-indexed bits (held for $2T$ and offset by $T$). That is offset QPSK with half-sine pulse shaping $\cos\frac{\pi t}{2T}$, $\sin\frac{\pi t}{2T}$: so MSK has the same BER as BPSK/QPSK, $Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})$, and a perfectly constant envelope ($\cos^2+\sin^2=1$). Its PSD, $$S(f)=\frac{16T}{\pi^2}\Big[\frac{\cos2\pi fT}{1-16f^2T^2}\Big]^2\quad(\text{baseband, unit power}),$$ has first null at $f=\frac{0.75}T$ (null-to-null bandwidth $1.5R_b$ in passband) and sidelobes that decay as $f^{-4}$, against $f^{-2}$ for QPSK, so adjacent-channel interference is smaller.

The MSK phase trajectory (a piecewise-linear path advancing $\pm\pi/2$ per bit) and the power spectra of BPSK, QPSK and MSK (rectangular pulses, equal bit rate, dB scale). QPSK’s main lobe is the narrowest but its sidelobes fall slowly; MSK’s sidelobes die away quickly: that is why constant-envelope, continuous-phase schemes are used where amplifier efficiency and adjacent-channel leakage matter.

GMSK passes the data through a Gaussian low-pass filter of 3 dB bandwidth $B$ ($BT=0.3$ in GSM, 0.5 in Bluetooth’s GFSK) before the FM modulator. The smoothing cuts the sidelobes further (down to $-60$ dB within 400 kHz of a GSM carrier) at the price of controlled ISI (the bit now overlaps its neighbours) that the receiver’s equaliser removes. GSM sends 270.833 kbit/s in a 200 kHz channel: $1.35$ bit/s/Hz, with a constant envelope that allows 55–60%-efficient class-C amplifiers in the handset.

Traps
  • BER vs symbol error: QPSK $P_s\approx2P_b$; $M$-ary Gray: $P_b\approx P_s/\log_2M$. Read which one the question asks.
  • $E_s/N_0$ vs $E_b/N_0$: $E_s=E_b\log_2M$. In dB, add $10\log_{10}\log_2M$; e.g. QPSK: $E_s/N_0=E_b/N_0+3$ dB; 16-QAM: $+6$ dB.
  • “Bandwidth” may mean null-to-null (rectangular pulses, $2R_s$ passband), Nyquist ($R_s$), or raised-cosine ($(1+\alpha)R_s$). The problem will usually say, or say “minimum”.
  • FSK has no $\sqrt2$ advantage like BPSK: coherent BFSK is 3 dB worse; non-coherent is a further ≈0.75 dB.
Engineering practice · choosing a modulation: adaptive modulation and coding

No single point on the map suits every link, so modern systems pick one per packet. LTE and 5G tabulate a modulation and coding scheme (MCS) ladder from QPSK with rate-1/12 coding up to 256-QAM (1024 in 5G) with rate ≈0.93: the base station selects the highest rung for which the user’s reported SNR (CQI) keeps the block error rate below 10%. Wi-Fi’s “MCS 0–11” does the same (BPSK 1/2 to 1024-QAM 5/6), explaining why your phone’s throughput falls in steps as you walk away from the router, and why the farthest rooms get the most robust BPSK/QPSK. The rule of thumb: at an SNR of $\gamma$ dB the achievable spectral efficiency is about $\log_2(1+\gamma_{\rm lin}/\Gamma)$ with the gap $\Gamma\approx3$–$5$ dB for a good code (Chapter 11).

(A) ✓ ($\sqrt\gamma$ vs $\sqrt{2\gamma}$). (B) ✗: 16-QAM (13.4 dB) beats 16-PSK (17.4 dB) by 4 dB because QAM points are farther apart. (C) ✓. (D) ✓.

Which modulation has a perfectly constant envelope and the same BER as BPSK?
  1. OQPSK with raised-cosine filtering
  2. 16-QAM
  3. MSK
  4. Non-coherent BFSK

MSK = OQPSK with half-sine pulses: constant envelope, $P_b=Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})$. Filtered OQPSK has some envelope variation; non-coherent BFSK is constant-envelope but worse in BER. (C).

Set B · Numerical practice

An 8-PSK system signals at 1.2 Mbaud. The bit rate in Mbit/s is ______.

$1.2\times\log_28=3.6$.

A square 16-QAM constellation has $I,Q\in\{\pm1,\pm3\}$ (units of volts, amplitude scaling so that $E_s=\overline{I^2+Q^2}$). The average energy per bit (same units) is ______.

$\overline{I^2+Q^2}=10$, $E_b=10/4=2.5$.

The minimum distance of 8-PSK of symbol energy $E_s$, expressed as a multiple of $\sqrt{E_s}$, is ______.

$2\sin(\pi/8)=2\times0.3827=\mathbf{0.765}$.

The minimum distance of square 16-QAM of average symbol energy $E_s$, as a multiple of $\sqrt{E_s}$, is ______.

$E_s=\frac{(M-1)d^2}6=2.5d^2\Rightarrow d=\sqrt{E_s/2.5}=\mathbf{0.632}\sqrt{E_s}$.

QPSK in AWGN has $E_s/N_0=10$ dB. The bit error probability, using $P_b=Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})$ with Gray coding, is about ______ ($Q(3.16)=7.8\times10^{-4}$).

$E_s=2E_b$ ⇒ $E_b/N_0=5$; $\sqrt{2\cdot5}=3.16$; $P_b=7.8\times10^{-4}$.

DPSK operates at $E_b/N_0=10$ dB. Using $P_b=\frac12e^{-E_b/N_0}$, the bit error probability in units of $10^{-5}$ is ______.

$\tfrac12e^{-10}=2.27\times10^{-5}$.

Non-coherent binary FSK has $P_b=\frac12e^{-E_b/2N_0}$. The $E_b/N_0$ (dB) needed for $P_b=10^{-5}$ is ______.

$e^{-\gamma/2}=2\times10^{-5}$ ⇒ $\gamma/2=10.82$ ⇒ $\gamma=21.6\to13.35$ dB.

A coherent BPSK receiver has a constant carrier phase error of $20^\circ$. The effective change in $E_b/N_0$ (dB, negative for a loss) is ______.

The amplitude scales by $\cos20^\circ$ so $E_b/N_0\to E_b/N_0\cos^2\varphi$: $10\log_{10}(0.883)=\mathbf{-0.54}$ dB.

A coherent binary FSK system at 10 kbit/s uses the minimum tone spacing for orthogonality. The spacing in kHz is ______. (For non-coherent detection it would be 10 kHz.)

Coherent: $\Delta f=\frac1{2T}=R_b/2=\mathbf5$ kHz.

The null-to-null bandwidth of MSK at 1 Mbit/s, in MHz, is ______.

$1.5R_b=\mathbf{1.5}$ MHz.

GSM sends 270.833 kbit/s in a 200 kHz carrier. The spectral efficiency (bit/s/Hz) is ______.

$270.833/200=\mathbf{1.35}$.

Set C · Exam-style problems

A symbol stream contains alternate QPSK and 16-QAM symbols. If symbols are transmitted at 1 mega-symbol per second, the raw (uncoded) data rate is ______ Mbit/s (one decimal).

Average bits per symbol $=(2+4)/2=3$ ⇒ 3.0 Mbit/s.

A modem carries 2 Mbit/s using 16-QAM with raised-cosine pulses in a passband channel of 1 MHz. The maximum roll-off factor $\alpha$ allowed is ______.

$R_s=2/4=0.5$ Msym/s; $B=(1+\alpha)R_s=1$ MHz ⇒ $\alpha=1$.

An ideal band-pass channel occupies 500 Hz–2000 Hz. A modem sends 4800 bit/s using 16-QAM with raised-cosine pulses that exactly fill the band. The roll-off factor is ______.

$B=1500$ Hz $=(1+\alpha)R_s$ (passband), $R_s=4800/4=1200$ sym/s ⇒ $1+\alpha=1.25$, $\alpha=\mathbf{0.25}$.

A GSM carrier occupies 200 kHz and is shared by 8 users in TDMA. At a given time 12 users are talking in a cell. The minimum total bandwidth (kHz) of the signal received by the base station is ______.

One 200 kHz carrier supports 8 time slots. 12 users need $\lceil12/8\rceil=2$ carriers: $2\times200=\mathbf{400}$ kHz. (GSM modulation itself is GMSK: constant-envelope MSK with a Gaussian filter.)

A satellite link uses QPSK with raised-cosine filtering of roll-off 0.35. Its spectral efficiency in bit/s/Hz is ______.

$\log_2M/(1+\alpha)=2/1.35=\mathbf{1.48}$.

Chapter 15 in one breath

  • Symbols are points in the IQ plane; $R_b=R_s\log_2M$; $E_b=E_s/\log_2M$; noise cloud $\sigma^2=N_0/2$; $d_{\min}$ rules. One quadrature correlator receiver serves every PSK/QAM scheme.
  • BPSK = QPSK BER $Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})$ (derived); QPSK half the bandwidth; phase error $\varphi$: $\cos\varphi$ loss. BFSK coherent $Q(\sqrt{E_b/N_0})$, tone spacing $1/2T$; non-coherent $\frac12e^{-E_b/2N_0}$ (Rice/Rayleigh), spacing $1/T$; DPSK $\frac12e^{-E_b/N_0}$ (0.75 dB worse than BPSK).
  • $M$-PSK: $2Q\big(\sqrt{2E_s/N_0}\sin\frac\pi M\big)$. Square $M$-QAM: $E_s=\frac{(M-1)d^2}6$, $P_s\approx4(1-\frac1{\sqrt M})Q\big(\sqrt{3E_s/((M-1)N_0)}\big)$; APSK for nonlinear amplifiers.
  • OQPSK/$\pi/4$-QPSK soften envelope jumps; MSK = CPFSK $h=\frac12$ = OQPSK with half-sine pulses: constant envelope, BER of BPSK, null-null $1.5R_b$; GMSK (GSM) smooths further.
  • Bandwidth: null-null $2R_s$, Nyquist $R_s$, raised-cosine $(1+\alpha)R_s$; efficiency $\frac{\log_2M}{1+\alpha}$. PSK/QAM = bandwidth-efficient, power-hungry; FSK = power-efficient, bandwidth-hungry. Adaptive modulation picks the rung per link.
