History · Shannon's miracle year

Before 1948 everybody "knew" that noise forces a trade: to make a message more reliable, send it more slowly, or repeat it over and over. Send the word three times and take a majority vote. Send it a thousand times and the error shrinks, but so does your data rate, towards zero. Reliability costs rate. That was the common sense of the telegraph and telephone engineers.

Claude Shannon, a 32-year-old mathematician at Bell Labs (who also built a juggling robot, a mechanical mouse that solved mazes, and rode a unicycle through the corridors), published A Mathematical Theory of Communication in the Bell System Technical Journal in two instalments in July and October 1948. It did three astonishing things. (1) It defined information as a number, in bits (John Tukey's coinage), independent of the meaning of the message. (2) It proved that every source has an irreducible compressed size, its entropy (Shannon later said that John von Neumann advised him to use that word, "because nobody knows what entropy really is, so in a debate you will always have the advantage"). (3) It proved that every noisy channel has a capacity $C$, and that for any rate $Ras small as you please, with no need for the rate to fall. Noise limits speed but not accuracy. It took engineers 45 years to build codes (turbo codes, 1993) that really got close.

Real world · where this shows up

ZIP, JPEG, MP3 and video codecs are source coding (the entropy half of the theory). Wi-Fi, LTE and 5G, DVB satellite TV, SSD flash memory, QR codes and CDs all use channel codes (the capacity half). The Voyager probes, 20 billion kilometres away, whisper at about 160 bits per second with a few watts of power, and the signal is recovered on Earth with error-correcting codes within a fraction of a dB of Shannon's limit.

Surprise, measured in bits

Suppose I tell you something. How much information did I give? Shannon's answer starts from three common-sense requirements. (i) An event you were certain of carries no information. (ii) The rarer the event, the more it tells you. (iii) If two independent things happen, the information adds. The only function satisfying all three is $-\log p$. Taking the logarithm to base 2 measures it in bits: $$I(x)=\log_2\frac1{p(x)}\ \text{ bits}.$$ Your friend says "the coin landed heads": $p=\frac12$, $I=1$ bit. "I rolled a six": $\log_26=2.58$ bits. "I won the lottery ($p=10^{-7}$)": 23 bits. This is the number of yes/no questions you'd need, on average, to pin the event down: the game of "twenty questions" is information theory.

Entropy is the average surprise of a source: $$H(X)=\sum_xp(x)\log_2\frac1{p(x)}=-\sum_xp(x)\log_2p(x)\ \text{ bits/symbol}.$$

Top: the surprise $-\log_2p$ of an event with probability $p$: it explodes as $p\to0$. Bottom: the entropy of a coin that lands heads with probability $p$ (the binary entropy function $H_b(p)$): zero for a certain outcome, maximal (1 bit) when it is a fair coin: the most uncertainty a binary source can have.
Properties of entropy (GATE tests them)
  • $0\le H(X)\le\log_2K$ for $K$ outcomes. Equality on the right iff all outcomes are equally likely (GATE 2024: alphabet of 16 ⇒ max entropy 4 bits).
  • Relabelling does not change entropy. $H(X)=H(2X)=H(2^X)=H(g(X))$ for any one-to-one $g$. A many-to-one $g$ (like $X^2$) merges outcomes and can only reduce entropy: $H(g(X))\le H(X)$. (GATE 2022 Q.29.)
  • Independent variables: $H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y)$. In general $H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y\mid X)$ (chain rule), and $H(Y\mid X)\le H(Y)$: knowing something never increases uncertainty on average.
  • A geometric source ("toss until first head", $p=\frac12$): $H=\sum_{k\ge1}k2^{-k}=2$ bits (GATE 2014).
Entropy of a three-symbol source as a function of its probabilities $(p_1,p_2,p_3)$. The floor is the probability triangle (corners = certainty, centre = uniform). The dome peaks at $\log_23=1.585$ bits at the centre and falls to 0 at the three corners. The edges are the binary entropy curves.

Worked example · information gained (GATE 2021 Q.51)

A school has equal boys and girls; 75% study Science, 25% Commerce; a Commerce student is twice as likely to be a boy as a Science student. How many bits do you gain on learning that a randomly selected girl studies Commerce? Let $P(\text{boy}\mid\text{Sci})=b$, so $P(\text{boy}\mid\text{Com})=2b$. Boys are half the school: $0.75b+0.25(2b)=0.5\Rightarrow b=0.4$. Then $P(\text{girl}\mid\text{Com})=0.2$, $P(\text{girl}\mid\text{Sci})=0.6$ and $P(\text{Com}\mid\text{girl})=\dfrac{0.25(0.2)}{0.5}=0.1$. Information $=\log_2\frac1{0.1}=\mathbf{3.32}$ bits. (Bayes from Chapter 1, plus the definition of self-information.)

Source coding: squeezing out the redundancy

We want to encode each symbol as a string of bits so that the average length is as short as possible, and so that the receiver can decode a continuous stream without punctuation. A code is prefix-free (instantaneous) if no codeword is the beginning of another; then the codewords are the leaves of a binary tree, and decoding is just walking down the tree. A prefix code with lengths $\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_K$ exists if and only if the Kraft inequality holds: $$\sum_{i=1}^{K}2^{-\ell_i}\le1.$$ (Each codeword of length $\ell$ "uses up" $2^{-\ell}$ of the tree's total capacity of 1.) And the average length obeys Shannon's source coding theorem: $$\boxed{\,H(X)\ \le\ \bar L\ <\ H(X)+1\,}\qquad\bar L=\sum_ip_i\ell_i,$$ with $\bar L=H$ exactly when all $p_i$ are powers of $\frac12$ (a "dyadic" source: ideal length $\ell_i=\log_2\frac1{p_i}$). Encoding blocks of $n$ symbols together squeezes the overhead to $\frac1n$ bits per symbol, so entropy is exactly the minimum rate of lossless compression.

Huffman's algorithm

In 1951, MIT student David Huffman was offered a choice by his professor, Robert Fano: take the final exam, or solve an open problem: find the optimal prefix code. After months he was about to give up and throw out his notes when the answer came to him: build the tree from the bottom. Repeatedly merge the two least probable symbols into one, adding their probabilities, until one node remains. Label the branches 0 and 1. Rare symbols end up deep (long codes), common ones near the root (short codes). Huffman's code is provably optimal among symbol-by-symbol prefix codes. Try it:

Step through Huffman's algorithm. Drag "merges done" from 0 to $K-1$. The dendrogram shows which merges have happened; the codeword of each symbol is read off from the root. Compare average length $\bar L$ with the entropy $H$. For the dyadic source they are exactly equal.
Worked example · GATE 2023 Q.65

Eight symbols with probabilities $\frac12,\frac14,\frac18,\frac1{16},\frac1{32},\frac1{64},\frac1{128},\frac1{128}$. "The average number of yes/no questions asked in the most efficient sequence" is the Huffman average length $=H$ (dyadic): $\tfrac12(1)+\tfrac14(2)+\tfrac18(3)+\tfrac1{16}(4)+\tfrac1{32}(5)+\tfrac1{64}(6)+2\cdot\tfrac1{128}(7)=\mathbf{1.984}$ (key 1.97–1.99). The optimal question order is "is it a? is it b? ..." Probabilities are dyadic, so no cleverness is needed.

Practical cousins: Shannon–Fano (split the sorted list into near-equal halves; not always optimal), arithmetic coding (encodes the whole message as one number, approaches $H$ without the $+1$), and Lempel–Ziv (ZIP, GIF, PNG: learns the source statistics on the fly; no probabilities needed).

Channels and mutual information

Now add noise. A discrete memoryless channel (DMC) is a table of conditional probabilities $p(y\mid x)$: given the input symbol $x$, the output is $y$ with that probability, independently each use. The simplest is the binary symmetric channel (BSC): each bit flips with probability $p$. Another: the binary erasure channel (BEC): each bit is lost (erased, and flagged as lost) with probability $\varepsilon$.

How much does seeing $Y$ tell us about $X$? Before seeing $Y$ our uncertainty about $X$ is $H(X)$; after seeing it, $H(X\mid Y)$ (the "equivocation"). The reduction is the mutual information: $$I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X\mid Y)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)=\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log_2\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}.$$ It is symmetric, non-negative, and zero exactly when $X$ and $Y$ are independent (GATE 2025 Q.60: a joint table $P(0,0)=0.06,\ P(0,1)=0.14,\ P(1,0)=0.24,\ P(1,1)=0.56$ has marginals $P(X=0)=0.2$, $P(Y=0)=0.3$ and $0.2\times0.3=0.06$ ✓ so every cell equals the product of marginals; $I=0$).

Binary symmetric channel with flip probability $p$ and input prior $\pi=P(X=1)$. The two bars decompose the entropies: $H(X)=H(X|Y)+I$ and $H(Y)=H(Y|X)+I$ with the same (green) $I(X;Y)$. The curve shows $I(X;Y)$ as a function of the prior: capacity is its peak.

Capacity: the best the channel can do

The mutual information depends on how we choose to use the channel (the input distribution). The channel capacity is the best we can do: $$C=\max_{p(x)}I(X;Y)\quad\text{bits per channel use}.$$ For the BSC the maximum is at the uniform prior (by symmetry) and $H(Y\mid X)=H_b(p)$ no matter what we send, so $$\boxed{C_{\rm BSC}=1-H_b(p)},\qquad\boxed{C_{\rm BEC}=1-\varepsilon}.$$ At $p=0$: 1 bit/use. At $p=\frac12$: $C=0$ (GATE 2014: the output is independent of the input; nothing gets through). At $p=1$: also $C=1$: the channel is a perfect inverter! The worst channel is the useless one, not the very noisy one. A cascade of two BSCs of crossover $p$ is a BSC with $p'=2p(1-p)$, a worse channel (Data-processing inequality: processing cannot create information).

$I(X;Y)$ for the BSC as a surface over the prior $\pi$ and the flip probability $p$. The ridge along $\pi=\frac12$ is the capacity curve $1-H_b(p)$; the valley along $p=\frac12$ is where the channel is useless; the corners at $p=0$ and $p=1$ are perfect channels.
Worked example · GATE 2022 Q.61 (a cyclic channel)

Three inputs $a,b,c$; each is received correctly with probability $1-\alpha$ and as the "next" letter ($a\to b,\ b\to c,\ c\to a$) with probability $\alpha$. By symmetry the uniform input is optimal and $H(Y\mid X)=H_b(\alpha)$ for every input: $C=\log_23-H_b(\alpha)$. On $[0.25,1]$, $H_b(\alpha)$ is smallest at $\alpha=1$ (where the channel deterministically permutes the letters: no noise at all, just relabelling), so capacity is maximised at $\alpha=\mathbf{1.00}$ ($C=\log_23$). Again: a perfectly predictable channel is a perfect channel.

Why a noisy channel can still carry bits perfectly: the noise-ball picture

Here is the idea behind Shannon's theorem. Send a long block of $n$ symbols. The channel adds noise that, with high probability, moves the received point to somewhere within a "noise ball" around the sent point; that ball contains about $2^{nH(Y|X)}$ typical outputs. Altogether the receiver can see about $2^{nH(Y)}$ typical outputs. If we choose codewords so that their noise balls don't overlap, the receiver identifies the right codeword every time. How many non-overlapping balls fit? About $$\frac{2^{nH(Y)}}{2^{nH(Y|X)}}=2^{n\,I(X;Y)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{rate }R=I(X;Y)\to C\text{ bits per use.}$$ This is a sphere-packing argument. Try packing balls yourself in a unit sphere:

Illustration only: random sequential packing of non-overlapping noise balls (radius $r$) inside the space of received signals (big wire sphere). Bigger noise ⇒ bigger balls ⇒ fewer codewords fit ⇒ lower rate. The count (bits ≈ $\log_2$ of the number of balls) is a toy analogue of capacity.
Shannon's noisy-channel coding theorem

For a DMC with capacity $C$: if $RC$, error probability is bounded away from 0 (and $\to1$ as $n\to\infty$). Existence, not construction: Shannon's proof is by choosing codewords at random! Constructing practical capacity-approaching codes took until turbo codes (1993), LDPC (Gallager 1960, rediscovered 1996) and polar codes (Arıkan 2008).

The Gaussian channel and the Shannon–Hartley formula

For the AWGN channel $Y=X+N$ with $N\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ and power constraint $E[X^2]\le P$, the optimal input is Gaussian and $$C=\tfrac12\log_2\Big(1+\frac P{\sigma^2}\Big)\ \text{bits per real sample}.$$ (Picture: the received sphere has radius$^2$ $n(P+\sigma^2)$, noise balls have radius$^2$ $n\sigma^2$; the ratio of volumes in $n$ dimensions is $\big(\frac{P+\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}\big)^{n/2}$.) A channel of bandwidth $B$ supports $2B$ independent samples per second (Nyquist), with noise power per sample $\sigma^2=N_0B$ (Chapter 3) and signal power $P$: $$\boxed{\,C=B\log_2\!\Big(1+\frac{S}{N}\Big)=B\log_2\!\Big(1+\frac{P}{N_0B}\Big)\ \text{bits/s}\,}$$ the Shannon–Hartley theorem (Ralph Hartley 1928 had the form $B\log(\ldots)$ without the noise; Shannon completed it). Remember, SNR here is a ratio: 30 dB $=1000$.

  • Telephone line: $B=4$ kHz, SNR 30 dB: $C=4000\log_2(1001)=39.9$ kbit/s: the famous barrier of analog modems (the 56k modem trick was to bypass the analog noise on the downstream path).
  • Power-limited regime ($S/N\ll1$): $C\approx1.44\,B\cdot S/N=1.44\,S/N_0$ independent of $B$.
  • Bandwidth-limited regime ($S/N\gg1$): every extra 3 dB of SNR buys $+1$ bit/s/Hz; doubling the bandwidth doubles $C$.
Capacity versus bandwidth with fixed signal power $P$ and noise density $N_0$. Capacity does not grow forever: it saturates at $C_\infty=\frac P{N_0}\log_2e=1.44\,P/N_0$, because more bandwidth also admits more noise. This is the plot GATE 2025 Q.15 asked about: increasing and concave, flattening to a horizontal asymptote.

The ultimate limit: $-1.59$ dB

Express signal power as $P=E_bR$ (energy per bit times bit rate). At the capacity rate $R=C$ with spectral efficiency $\eta=C/B$ bit/s/Hz, $$\eta=\log_2\!\Big(1+\eta\frac{E_b}{N_0}\Big)\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{E_b}{N_0}\Big|_{\min}=\frac{2^\eta-1}{\eta}.$$ Push $\eta\to0$ (infinite bandwidth) and this tends to $\ln2=0.693=\mathbf{-1.59\ dB}$: the Shannon limit. No code, no modulation, no technology can deliver reliable communication at an $E_b/N_0$ below $-1.59$ dB. A rate of $\eta=2$ bit/s/Hz needs at least 1.76 dB; $\eta=6$ needs 10.2 dB. Modern codes (turbo, LDPC) work within 0.5–1 dB of this wall.

The Shannon boundary: minimum $E_b/N_0$ against spectral efficiency. Reliable communication is possible only in the region to the right of the curve. The red markers are the uncoded schemes you will meet in Chapter 11 (at a bit-error rate of $10^{-5}$): they sit about 8–10 dB to the right of the wall; powerful codes close that gap.

The minimum is the entropy: $\frac12(1)+\frac14(2)+2\cdot\frac18(3)=1.75$ bits (dyadic, so a Huffman code with lengths 1, 2, 3, 3 attains it).
