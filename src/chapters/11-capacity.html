History · the modem race and the wall at 35 kbit/s

In the 1960s a telephone modem delivered 300 bit/s. Thirty years of engineering, from V.21 (300 bit/s) through V.32 (9.6 kbit/s, 1984) to V.34 (33.6 kbit/s, 1994), pushed the speed up a hundredfold, and then it stopped. The reason is a number you can compute on a napkin. A telephone line is about $3.1$ kHz wide with an SNR of 30–35 dB, and the Shannon–Hartley formula gives $C=3100\log_2(1+10^{3.3})\approx34$ kbit/s. V.34 was essentially at capacity, and the industry knew nothing more could be squeezed from the analog line. The 56k modems that followed (V.90) did not break Shannon’s law: they avoided the analog bottleneck on the downstream path by plugging the server’s digital line straight into the exchange, so only one noisy analog segment remained. The channel capacity is not a suggestion.

Real world · distance from the wall, and the Voyagers

Everything in modern communications is designed with the capacity formula open on the desk: the number of bits per second per hertz a Wi-Fi router can offer, the SNR a satellite TV set needs, the power of a deep-space transmitter. DVB-S2 satellite broadcasting operates within about 0.7 dB of the Shannon limit; LTE and 5G turbo/LDPC/polar codes within 1 dB; the Voyager probes, 20 billion kilometres away, whisper at about 160 bits per second with a few watts of power, and Earth’s 70 m dishes recover them with coding gains of several dB over the uncoded case.

The discrete memoryless channel

A discrete memoryless channel (DMC) is a table of conditional probabilities $p(y\mid x)$ (the transition matrix, rows indexed by input $x$, columns by output $y$, each row summing to 1): given the input symbol $x$, the output is $y$ with that probability, independently each use. The standard examples:

The simplest of these, the BSC, is what a hard-decision demodulator turns an AWGN channel into (Chapter 15): the BSC is therefore the model for every coded system in Chapter 17.

Mutual information and what it can and cannot do

How much does seeing $Y$ tell us about $X$? Before seeing $Y$ our uncertainty about $X$ is $H(X)$; after seeing it, $H(X\mid Y)$ (the “equivocation”). The reduction is the mutual information: $$I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X\mid Y)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)=\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log_2\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}.$$ It is symmetric, non-negative ($=D(p_{XY}\|p_Xp_Y)\ge0$, Chapter 10), and zero exactly when $X$ and $Y$ are independent (GATE 2025 Q.60: a joint table $P(0,0)=0.06,\ P(0,1)=0.14,\ P(1,0)=0.24,\ P(1,1)=0.56$ has marginals $P(X=0)=0.2$, $P(Y=0)=0.3$ and $0.2\times0.3=0.06$ ✓ so every cell equals the product of marginals; $I=0$). Two structural facts:

  • $I(X;Y)$ is concave in the input distribution (for a fixed channel) and convex in the channel (for a fixed input): so the maximum over inputs is a well-behaved convex problem, with any local maximum global.
  • Data-processing inequality. If $X\to Y\to Z$ form a Markov chain ($Z$ depends on $X$ only through $Y$), then $I(X;Z)\le I(X;Y)$. Proof. Expand $I(X;Y,Z)$ in two ways with the chain rule: $I(X;Y,Z)=I(X;Y)+I(X;Z\mid Y)=I(X;Z)+I(X;Y\mid Z)$. The Markov property makes $I(X;Z\mid Y)=0$, and $I(X;Y\mid Z)\ge0$, hence $I(X;Y)\ge I(X;Z)$. $\square$ No processing of the received data, however clever, can create information about the input. (It justifies the slogan that a hard-decision decoder cannot beat a soft-decision one: quantising $Y$ to a bit is processing.)
Binary symmetric channel with flip probability $p$ and input prior $\pi=P(X=1)$. The two bars decompose the entropies: $H(X)=H(X|Y)+I$ and $H(Y)=H(Y|X)+I$ with the same (green) $I(X;Y)$. The curve shows $I(X;Y)$ as a function of the prior: capacity is its peak.

Capacity: the best the channel can do

The mutual information depends on how we choose to use the channel (the input distribution). The channel capacity is the best we can do: $$C=\max_{p(x)}I(X;Y)\quad\text{bits per channel use}.$$

Derivation · capacities of the symmetric channels

Symmetric channels (every row is a permutation of every other row, and likewise columns). Then $H(Y\mid X=x)$ is the same number $h_{\rm row}$ for every input, so $H(Y\mid X)=h_{\rm row}$ regardless of $p(x)$, and $I(X;Y)=H(Y)-h_{\rm row}\le\log_2|\mathcal Y|-h_{\rm row}$. The bound is attained by the uniform input (which makes the output uniform by the column symmetry). Hence $$C=\log_2|\mathcal Y|-H(\text{any row}).$$ BSC: $|\mathcal Y|=2$, row $=(1-p,p)$: $\boxed{C_{\rm BSC}=1-H_b(p)}$. At $p=0$: 1 bit/use. At $p=\frac12$: $C=0$ (GATE 2014: the output is independent of the input; nothing gets through). At $p=1$: also $C=1$: the channel is a perfect inverter! The worst channel is the useless one, not the very noisy one.

BEC. Seeing an unerased output reveals $X$ exactly; seeing the erasure reveals nothing. So $H(X\mid Y)=\varepsilon H(X)$ and $I(X;Y)=(1-\varepsilon)H(X)\le1-\varepsilon$, attained by a uniform input: $$\boxed{C_{\rm BEC}=1-\varepsilon}.$$ A fraction $1-\varepsilon$ of the bits get through: capacity is exactly what common sense says, and an optimal code simply has to hide the erasures. Z-channel. With input probability $P(X=1)=\pi$ the output is 1 with probability $\pi(1-p)$; maximising $H_b(\pi(1-p))-\pi H_b(p)$ over $\pi$ gives $C=\log_2\!\big(1+(1-p)p^{p/(1-p)}\big)$, e.g. $0.322$ bit for $p=\frac12$, attained by a non-uniform input with $P(X=1)=0.4$ (the best input uses the reliable “0” more often).

$I(X;Y)$ for the BSC as a surface over the prior $\pi$ and the flip probability $p$. The ridge along $\pi=\frac12$ is the capacity curve $1-H_b(p)$; the valley along $p=\frac12$ is where the channel is useless; the corners at $p=0$ and $p=1$ are perfect channels.
Worked example · GATE 2022 Q.61 (a cyclic channel)

Three inputs $a,b,c$; each is received correctly with probability $1-\alpha$ and as the “next” letter ($a\to b,\ b\to c,\ c\to a$) with probability $\alpha$. By symmetry the uniform input is optimal and $H(Y\mid X)=H_b(\alpha)$ for every input: $C=\log_23-H_b(\alpha)$. On $[0.25,1]$, $H_b(\alpha)$ is smallest at $\alpha=1$ (where the channel deterministically permutes the letters: no noise at all, just relabelling), so capacity is maximised at $\alpha=\mathbf{1.00}$ ($C=\log_23$). Again: a perfectly predictable channel is a perfect channel.

Cascades and repeated use

Two BSCs in cascade with crossover $p_1,p_2$: the bit is flipped iff exactly one of them flips it, so $p=p_1(1-p_2)+p_2(1-p_1)$; for equal $p$, $p'=2p(1-p)$. For $N$ identical BSCs, writing $1-2p'=(1-2p)^N$ (each stage multiplies the “bias” $1-2p$) gives $p_N=\frac12\big[1-(1-2p)^N\big]\to\frac12$: a long chain of noisy links ends up carrying nothing, which is the reason long-haul links regenerate (decode and re-encode) at every repeater instead of just amplifying (Chapter 12). This is the data-processing inequality in action: $C$ can only fall in a cascade. E.g. $p=0.1$: $N=2$: $0.18$; $N=3$: $0.244$.

Shannon’s noisy-channel coding theorem

The theorem

For a DMC with capacity $C$: if $RC$, error probability is bounded away from 0 (and $\to1$ as $n\to\infty$). Existence, not construction: Shannon’s proof is by choosing codewords at random! Constructing practical capacity-approaching codes took until turbo codes (1993), LDPC (Gallager 1960, rediscovered 1996) and polar codes (Arıkan 2008).

Why it is true: the noise-ball picture

Send a long block of $n$ symbols. The channel adds noise that, with high probability, moves the received point to somewhere within a “noise ball” around the sent point; that ball contains about $2^{nH(Y|X)}$ typical outputs. Altogether the receiver can see about $2^{nH(Y)}$ typical outputs. If we choose codewords so that their noise balls don’t overlap, the receiver identifies the right codeword every time. How many non-overlapping balls fit? About $$\frac{2^{nH(Y)}}{2^{nH(Y|X)}}=2^{n\,I(X;Y)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{rate }R=I(X;Y)\to C\text{ bits per use.}$$ This is a sphere-packing argument. Try packing balls yourself in a unit sphere:

Illustration only: random sequential packing of non-overlapping noise balls (radius $r$) inside the space of received signals (big wire sphere). Bigger noise ⇒ bigger balls ⇒ fewer codewords fit ⇒ lower rate. The count (bits ≈ $\log_2$ of the number of balls) is a toy analogue of capacity.
Derivation · the random-coding proof in five steps
  1. Random code. Pick $2^{nR}$ codewords $x^n(1),\dots,x^n(2^{nR})$ independently, each symbol drawn from the capacity-achieving $p(x)$.
  2. Decoder: joint typicality. Decode to the message $w$ whose codeword is jointly typical with the received $y^n$ (i.e. $(x^n,y^n)$ has empirical statistics matching $p(x,y)$), if there is exactly one such $w$.
  3. Correct codeword is jointly typical: by the AEP the transmitted $x^n$ and the received $y^n$ are jointly typical with probability $\to1$.
  4. A wrong codeword is jointly typical only rarely: a codeword independent of $y^n$ is jointly typical with it with probability $\le2^{-n[I(X;Y)-3\epsilon]}$ (the fraction of $(x,y)$ pairs that are typical, from the sizes of the typical sets $2^{nH(X,Y)}$ vs $2^{nH(X)}2^{nH(Y)}$).
  5. Union bound. There are $2^{nR}-1$ wrong codewords, so $P(\text{error})\le\epsilon+2^{nR}2^{-n[I-3\epsilon]}=\epsilon+2^{-n[I-R-3\epsilon]}\to0$ whenever $R

Converse. For any code of rate $R$ and error probability $P_e$, Fano’s inequality ($H(W\mid\hat W)\le H_b(P_e)+P_e\log_2(M-1)$, where $M=2^{nR}$) combined with the data-processing inequality gives $nR=H(W)=I(W;\hat W)+H(W\mid\hat W)\le nC+1+P_eR\,n$, i.e. $P_e\ge1-\frac CR-\frac1{nR}$: for $R>C$ the error probability cannot go to zero. $\square$

What the theorem does not say

It promises error-free communication only as the block length $n\to\infty$, so it says nothing about delay or complexity; Shannon’s random codes are impossible to decode (exhaustive search). At finite length $n$ the best achievable rate is lower: $R\approx C-\sqrt{V/n}\,Q^{-1}(\epsilon)$ with $V$ the “channel dispersion”. For a BSC with $p=0.11$ ($C=0.5$) a block length of $n=1000$ gives $R\approx0.43$ at $P_e=10^{-3}$: real, practical codes (Chapter 17) trade a loss of this kind for latency.

Worked example · repetition coding vs the capacity promise

BSC with $p=0.1$ has $C=1-H_b(0.1)=0.531$. The 3-fold repetition code (rate $\frac13$) decodes by majority vote, with $P_e=3p^2(1-p)+p^3=0.028$: not small, and its rate is only $\frac13$. Increasing the repetition factor to reach $P_e=10^{-6}$ needs about 21 repetitions (rate $\approx0.05$). Shannon says that a good long code of rate $0.5$ (ten times more efficient than repetition) can have $P_e<10^{-6}$ with the same channel. The trick is coding across many bits so that the noise is “averaged out”, not repeating individual bits.

Continuous channels: the Gaussian channel

Real channels carry real numbers. The role of entropy is played by differential entropy $h(X)=-\int f(x)\log_2f(x)\,dx$, which (unlike $H$) can be negative and changes under scaling, $h(aX)=h(X)+\log_2|a|$, but whose differences (mutual information $I=h(Y)-h(Y|X)$) are meaningful. Two values: uniform on an interval of length $a$: $h=\log_2a$; Gaussian $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$: $$h=\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\sigma^2).$$

Derivation · the Gaussian has the largest entropy for a given power

Let $f$ be any density with variance $\sigma^2$ (mean zero), and $g=\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$. Then $0\le D(f\|g)=\int f\log\frac fg=-h(f)-\int f\log g$, and $-\int f\log_2g=\int f\big[\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi\sigma^2)+\frac{x^2}{2\sigma^2}\log_2e\big]=\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi\sigma^2)+\tfrac12\log_2e=h(g)$ (this uses only the second moment of $f$). Therefore $h(f)\le h(g)=\frac12\log_22\pi e\sigma^2$, with equality iff $f$ is Gaussian. Among all noises of a given power, Gaussian noise is the worst; and among all signals of a given power the Gaussian carries the most information.

Derivation · capacity of the AWGN channel

$Y=X+Z$, $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ independent of $X$, with the power constraint $\E[X^2]\le P$. Then $I(X;Y)=h(Y)-h(Y\mid X)=h(Y)-h(Z)$. The output variance is at most $P+\sigma^2$, so $h(Y)\le\frac12\log_22\pi e(P+\sigma^2)$ with equality when $Y$ (hence $X$) is Gaussian. Therefore $$C=\tfrac12\log_22\pi e(P+\sigma^2)-\tfrac12\log_22\pi e\sigma^2=\tfrac12\log_2\Big(1+\frac P{\sigma^2}\Big)\ \ \text{bits per real sample}.$$ Geometric view (sphere packing): in $n$ dimensions the received vectors lie in a sphere of radius$^2$ $n(P+\sigma^2)$ and each noise ball has radius$^2$ $n\sigma^2$; the number of balls that fit is the ratio of volumes $\big(\frac{P+\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}\big)^{n/2}$, i.e. $\frac12\log(1+P/\sigma^2)$ bits per dimension.

Derivation · the Shannon–Hartley theorem

A channel of bandwidth $B$ supports $2B$ independent real samples per second (Nyquist, Chapter 3). The noise per sample has variance $\sigma^2=\frac{N_0}2\cdot2B=N_0B$ (Chapter 5) and the signal has power $P$ per sample, so multiplying the per-sample capacity by $2B$ samples/s: $$\boxed{\,C=B\log_2\!\Big(1+\frac{S}{N}\Big)=B\log_2\!\Big(1+\frac{P}{N_0B}\Big)\ \text{bits/s}\,}$$ the Shannon–Hartley theorem (Ralph Hartley 1928 had the form $B\log(\ldots)$ without the noise; Shannon completed it). Remember, SNR here is a ratio: 30 dB $=1000$. For a complex baseband channel with complex noise of variance $N_0B$: $C=\log_2(1+\mathrm{SNR})$ per complex sample, $B$ complex samples/s: the same.

  • Telephone line: $B=4$ kHz, SNR 30 dB: $C=4000\log_2(1001)=39.9$ kbit/s (with the real 3.1 kHz band: 31 kbit/s): the famous barrier of analog modems.
  • Power-limited regime ($S/N\ll1$): $C\approx1.44\,B\cdot S/N=1.44\,S/N_0$ independent of $B$ (using $\ln(1+x)\approx x$).
  • Bandwidth-limited regime ($S/N\gg1$): every extra 3 dB of SNR buys $+1$ bit/s/Hz; doubling the bandwidth doubles $C$.
Capacity versus bandwidth with fixed signal power $P$ and noise density $N_0$. Capacity does not grow forever: it saturates at $C_\infty=\frac P{N_0}\log_2e=1.44\,P/N_0$, because more bandwidth also admits more noise. This is the plot GATE 2025 Q.15 asked about: increasing and concave, flattening to a horizontal asymptote.

The ultimate limit: $-1.59$ dB

Express signal power as $P=E_bR$ (energy per bit times bit rate). At the capacity rate $R=C$ with spectral efficiency $\eta=C/B$ bit/s/Hz, $$\eta=\log_2\!\Big(1+\eta\frac{E_b}{N_0}\Big)\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{E_b}{N_0}\Big|_{\min}=\frac{2^\eta-1}{\eta}.$$ Push $\eta\to0$ (infinite bandwidth) and this tends to $\ln2=0.693=\mathbf{-1.59\ dB}$: the Shannon limit. No code, no modulation, no technology can deliver reliable communication at an $E_b/N_0$ below $-1.59$ dB.

Derivation · the gap of uncoded QAM (and what coding must win back)

Square $M$-QAM with $M=2^\eta$ has symbol-error probability $P_e\approx4Q\big(\sqrt{3\,\mathrm{SNR}/(M-1)}\big)$ (Chapter 15). The SNR needed for a given $P_e$ is therefore $\mathrm{SNR}_{\rm req}=\frac{M-1}3\big[Q^{-1}(P_e/4)\big]^2$, whereas capacity at spectral efficiency $\eta=\log_2M$ needs $\mathrm{SNR}_{\rm cap}=2^\eta-1=M-1$. The ratio is independent of $M$: $$\Gamma=\frac{\mathrm{SNR}_{\rm req}}{\mathrm{SNR}_{\rm cap}}=\frac{[Q^{-1}(P_e/4)]^2}3.$$ For $P_e=10^{-5}$: $Q^{-1}(2.5\times10^{-6})=4.56$, $\Gamma=6.9=\mathbf{8.4}$ dB; for $P_e=10^{-6}$: $9.3$ dB. Uncoded QAM is about 9 dB from capacity at any rate. Of this, at most $1.53$ dB ($10\log\frac{\pi e}6$) can be won by shaping (using Gaussian-like instead of uniform constellation points); the rest, $\approx8$ dB, is the potential coding gain of Chapter 17, harvested by turbo/LDPC codes to within 1 dB. Hard decisions cost extra: slicing the received value into a bit before decoding turns the AWGN channel into a BSC and, at low SNR, loses about $10\log_{10}(\pi/2)=2$ dB of $E_b/N_0$ compared with passing unquantised (soft) values to the decoder. Practical soft decoders use 3–4 bit quantisation, within 0.2 dB of unquantised.

Parallel channels and water-filling

Real channels are rarely flat: a telephone line attenuates high frequencies, a multipath wireless channel has deep fades at some frequencies, and a MIMO link splits into several parallel sub-channels of unequal strength. If we have $N$ independent Gaussian sub-channels with noise levels $N_i$ (strength inverse to $N_i$) and a total power budget $P=\sum P_i$, how should we divide the power?

Derivation · water-filling

Maximise $\sum_i\frac12\log_2\big(1+\frac{P_i}{N_i}\big)$ subject to $\sum P_i=P$, $P_i\ge0$. The objective is concave, so the KKT conditions are necessary and sufficient. With a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$: $\frac{1}{2\ln2}\frac1{N_i+P_i}=\lambda$ whenever $P_i>0$, i.e. $N_i+P_i=\nu$ (a constant, “the water level”), and $P_i=0$ if $N_i>\nu$ (because even $P_i=0$ has marginal gain $\frac1{2N_i\ln2}<\lambda$). Hence $$\boxed{\,P_i=(\nu-N_i)^+\,,\qquad\nu\ \text{chosen so that}\ \sum_i(\nu-N_i)^+=P\,}$$ Picture the noise levels $N_i$ as the bumpy floor of a vessel, and pour in a volume $P$ of water: the water surface sets $\nu$, and the depth above each floor point is the power given to that channel. Strong channels (low noise) get the most power; channels whose floor is above the water get none. This is counter-intuitive: give the most to the best, not to the worst.

Water-filling over eight sub-channels whose noise levels (grey) vary like a frequency-selective channel. The blue columns are the power allotted to each. Raise the total power and more channels turn on; at very low power only the best one is used. The readout compares the water-filling capacity with equal power allocation.

Engineering practice · water-filling in DSL, OFDM and MIMO

An ADSL modem divides the copper pair into 256 sub-carriers of 4.3125 kHz (discrete multi-tone, the same idea as OFDM, Chapter 20). It measures the SNR of each at start-up and loads each tone with 0–15 bits (BPSK to 32768-QAM) according to the available SNR: high-frequency tones on a long line are “below the water” and carry nothing, while the tones at a few hundred kHz carry 10–12 bits each. A fixed total power constraint (about $20$ dBm) and a flat power spectral density ceiling make the allocation “approximate water-filling”. A MIMO link with channel matrix $\mathbf H$ is diagonalised by the SVD into $\min(N_t,N_r)$ parallel sub-channels with gains $\sigma_k^2$ and the capacity-achieving transmitter water-fills across them (Chapter 21). At high SNR water-filling gives nothing over equal allocation; at low SNR it concentrates all power on the best mode (beamforming).

A glance ahead: fading and MIMO capacity

If the channel gain $h$ is random (fading, Chapter 21) and known at the receiver, the capacity of a block-fading channel is the ergodic capacity $\E\big[\log_2(1+|h|^2\mathrm{SNR})\big]$, which is less than $\log_2(1+\E|h|^2\mathrm{SNR})$ by Jensen’s inequality (concavity of $\log$): fading costs capacity. When the channel is slow and the code is shorter than the channel’s coherence, the relevant measure is the outage probability $P(\log_2(1+|h|^2\mathrm{SNR})linearly with the number of antennas, the “multiplexing gain”, the single biggest advance in wireless since turbo codes.

Types: (1) BSC/BEC/DMC capacity, including “which $p$ maximises capacity”; (2) $I(X;Y)=0$ for independent variables; (3) Shannon–Hartley numerics, $C_\infty=1.44P/N_0$; (4) channel-type recognition (useless/lossless/…); (5) minimum $E_b$ or bandwidth for a given rate.
  • Shortcuts: independence ⇒ $I=0$; symmetric channel ⇒ uniform input optimal and $C=\log|Y|-H(\text{row})$; BEC $=1-\varepsilon$.
  • Do not confuse SNR in dB with the ratio inside the log.

Practice

Set A · Concept checks

Which channel has the larger capacity: a BEC with erasure probability 0.2, or a BSC with crossover probability 0.2?
  1. The BEC (0.8 vs 0.278 bit/use)
  2. The BSC
  3. They are equal
  4. Cannot be compared

$C_{\rm BEC}=1-0.2=0.8$; $C_{\rm BSC}=1-H_b(0.2)=1-0.722=0.278$. The BEC is much better because it tells the receiver which bits are unreliable. (A).

$X\to Y\to Z$ is a Markov chain. Which statements are true?
  1. $I(X;Z)\le I(X;Y)$
  2. $I(X;Z)\ge I(X;Y)$
  3. $I(X;Z)\le I(Y;Z)$
  4. $H(X\mid Z)\le H(X\mid Y)$

Data-processing: $I(X;Z)\le\min\{I(X;Y),I(Y;Z)\}$, so (A) and (C) are true and (B) false. (D) is false: $H(X|Z)=H(X)-I(X;Z)\ge H(X)-I(X;Y)=H(X|Y)$. Processing can only increase the equivocation.

A channel has three inputs and two outputs: input 1 always gives output 1, and inputs 2 and 3 always give output 2. The channel is
  1. lossless
  2. deterministic
  3. noiseless
  4. useless

Each row of the transition matrix has a single 1, so $H(Y|X)=0$: deterministic. It is not lossless (output 2 could come from input 2 or 3, so $H(X|Y)>0$), and its capacity is $\log_22=1$ bit.

Set B · Numerical practice

The capacity of a binary erasure channel with erasure probability 0.25 (bits per use) is ______.

$1-\varepsilon=\mathbf{0.75}$.

The capacity of a BSC with crossover probability 0.2 (bits per use) is ______.

$1-H_b(0.2)=1-0.7219=\mathbf{0.278}$.

Three identical BSCs with $p=0.1$ are cascaded. The crossover probability of the equivalent BSC is ______.

$p_3=\frac12[1-(1-2p)^3]=\frac12[1-0.8^3]=\frac12(0.488)=\mathbf{0.244}$.

A 3-fold repetition code (majority-vote decoding) is used over a BSC with $p=0.1$. The probability of decoding error is ______.

Error iff $\ge2$ of 3 bits flip: $3p^2(1-p)+p^3=3(0.01)(0.9)+0.001=\mathbf{0.028}$.

The capacity of a Z-channel in which a transmitted 1 is received as 0 with probability $p=\frac12$ (and a 0 is always received correctly) is, using $C=\log_2\big(1+(1-p)p^{p/(1-p)}\big)$, ______ bits/use.

$(1-p)p^{p/(1-p)}=0.5\times0.5^{1}=0.25$; $C=\log_2(1.25)=\mathbf{0.322}$.

The Shannon capacity (Mbit/s) of a 1 MHz channel at an SNR of 20 dB is ______.

$10^6\log_2(101)=\mathbf{6.66}$ Mbit/s.

The minimum SNR (dB) at which the capacity of an AWGN channel equals $2B$ ($2$ bit/s/Hz) is ______.

$2=\log_2(1+\mathrm{SNR})\Rightarrow\mathrm{SNR}=3\to10\log3=\mathbf{4.77}$ dB.

A modem must carry 33.6 kbit/s over a channel of bandwidth 3.1 kHz. The minimum SNR (dB) required by Shannon is ______.

$\eta=33.6/3.1=10.84$ bit/s/Hz; $\mathrm{SNR}=2^{10.84}-1=1830\to\mathbf{32.6}$ dB.

A channel has $P/N_0=2000$ (Hz). Its capacity as $B\to\infty$ (in kbit/s) is ______.

$C_\infty=\frac P{N_0}\log_2e=2000\times1.4427=\mathbf{2.885}$ kbit/s.

Three parallel Gaussian sub-channels have noise levels $(1,2,4)$ and a total power of 4. The water-filling power allotted to the best (first) sub-channel is ______.

Channel 3 is off ($\nu=3.67

Using $\Gamma=\frac13[Q^{-1}(P_e/4)]^2$ with $Q^{-1}(2.5\times10^{-7})=5.03$, the gap to capacity of uncoded square QAM at $P_e=10^{-6}$, in dB, is ______.

$\Gamma=\frac{5.03^2}3=8.43\to10\log_{10}8.43=\mathbf{9.3}$ dB.

Set C · Exam-style problems

The capacity of a Binary Symmetric Channel (BSC) with cross-over probability 0.5 is ______.

$C=1-H_b(0.5)=0$.

The capacity of a band-limited AWGN channel is $C=W\log_2\!\big(1+\frac P{\sigma^2W}\big)$ bps, with $\sigma^2$ the one-sided noise PSD. For a fixed $P/\sigma^2=1000$, the capacity (kbps) with infinite bandwidth is approximately
  1. 1.44
  2. 1.08
  3. 0.72
  4. 0.36

$C_\infty=\frac P{\sigma^2}\log_2e=1000\times1.4427=1.44$ kbps. (A).

A binary random variable $X$ takes the value 1 with probability $\frac13$. $X$ is the input to a cascade of two independent identical BSCs, each with crossover probability $\frac12$, with outputs $Y_1$ and $Y_2$. The value of $H(Y_1)+H(Y_2)$ in bits is ______.

A BSC with $p=\frac12$ makes its output uniform whatever the input: $H(Y_1)=1$; likewise $H(Y_2)=1$. Total $=\mathbf2$.

A speech signal band-limited to 4 kHz is sampled at 1.25 times the Nyquist rate. The samples, statistically independent and uniform on $[-5\,\text{V},+5\,\text{V}]$, are quantised by an 8-bit uniform quantiser and transmitted over an AWGN voice-grade channel with signal-to-noise power ratio 26 dB. The minimum channel bandwidth (kHz, two decimals) for arbitrarily small error probability is ______.

Rate $=10\,\text{ksamples/s}\times8=80$ kbps. $C=B\log_2(1+398.1)=8.64B\ge80\,000\Rightarrow B=9.26$ kHz.

A DMC has inputs $a,b,c$ and outputs $a,b,c$. Each input is received correctly with probability $1-\alpha$, and as the next letter ($a\to b,\ b\to c,\ c\to a$) with probability $\alpha$. The value of $\alpha\in[0.25,1]$ that maximises the capacity is ______ (two decimals).

$C=\log_23-H_b(\alpha)$; $H_b$ is minimal (=0) at $\alpha=1$ on that interval. Answer 1.00.

An AWGN channel has bandwidth $W$, noise PSD $N_0/2$, and average transmit power $P_{av}$ fixed. How does the capacity $C$ depend on $W$ (with $P_{av},N_0$ fixed)?
  1. It increases with $W$, concavely, and saturates at $\frac{P_{av}}{N_0}\log_2e$
  2. It increases linearly without bound
  3. It decreases with $W$
  4. It first increases then decreases

$C=W\log_2(1+\frac{P}{N_0W})$: increasing, concave, with limit $1.44P/N_0$. (A).

Bernoulli variables $X,Y$ with $P(0,0)=0.06,\ P(0,1)=0.14,\ P(1,0)=0.24,\ P(1,1)=0.56$. The mutual information $I(X;Y)$ (two decimals) is ______.

$P(X=0)=0.20$, $P(Y=0)=0.30$; $0.2\times0.3=0.06$, $0.2\times0.7=0.14$, $0.8\times0.3=0.24$, $0.8\times0.7=0.56$: independent ⇒ $I=0$.

$B=1$ MHz, $kT=-174$ dBm/Hz, received signal power $S=-80$ dBm over an additive Gaussian channel. Which options are TRUE for the Shannon capacity $C$?
  1. $C=B$
  2. $C=2B$
  3. $C>3B$
  4. $C

SNR $=34$ dB, $C=11.3B$. Only (C).

A BSC has crossover probability $p=0.11$. Its capacity (bits/use, using $H_b(0.11)=0.5$) is ______.

$C=1-H_b(0.11)=0.50$.

Two identical BSCs, each with $p=0.1$, are cascaded. The capacity of the cascade (bits/use) is ______. (Use $H_b(0.18)=0.68$.)

$p'=2(0.1)(0.9)=0.18$; $C=1-H_b(0.18)=0.32$.

A telephone channel has bandwidth 4 kHz and SNR 30 dB. Its Shannon capacity in kbit/s is ______.

$4000\log_2(1001)=39.87$ kbit/s.

A voice-grade AWGN telephone channel has bandwidth 4.0 kHz and two-sided noise PSD $\eta/2=2.5\times10^{-5}$ W/Hz. Information is sent at 52 kbit/s with arbitrarily small error probability. The minimum bit energy $E_b$ (mJ/bit) required is ______.

$N_0=\eta=5\times10^{-5}$ W/Hz; noise power $N_0B=0.2$ W. Capacity condition $52\,000=4000\log_2(1+S/N)\Rightarrow S/N=2^{13}-1=8191$, so $S=8191\times0.2=1638$ W. $E_b=S/R_b=1638/52000=\mathbf{31.5}$ mJ/bit. (An absurd number for a real telephone line; the point is the method.)

A binary memoryless channel has transition probabilities $P(0|0)=0.25,\ P(1|0)=0.75,\ P(0|1)=0.25,\ P(1|1)=0.75$. The channel is
  1. lossless
  2. noiseless
  3. useless
  4. deterministic

Both rows are $(0.25,\ 0.75)$: the output distribution does not depend on the input, so $I(X;Y)=0$: useless.

Chapter 11 in one breath

  • DMC = transition matrix. $I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y)=H(Y)-H(Y|X)\ge0$; $=0$ iff independent. Data-processing: $X\to Y\to Z\Rightarrow I(X;Z)\le I(X;Y)$.
  • $C=\max_{p(x)}I(X;Y)$. Symmetric: $C=\log|Y|-H(\text{row})$. BSC $1-H_b(p)$; BEC $1-\varepsilon$; Z-channel $\log_2(1+(1-p)p^{p/(1-p)})$. Useless (identical rows) = 0; noiseless = $\log K$. Cascade of $N$ BSCs: $p_N=\frac12[1-(1-2p)^N]$.
  • Coding theorem: $RC$ ⇒ impossible (Fano). Existence, with $n\to\infty$.
  • Gaussian maximises entropy ⇒ $C=\frac12\log_2(1+P/\sigma^2)$ per real sample; Shannon–Hartley $C=B\log_2(1+S/N)$; $C_\infty=1.44P/N_0$; $E_b/N_0\ge\frac{2^\eta-1}\eta\to\ln2=-1.59$ dB.
  • Uncoded QAM sits $\approx9$ dB from capacity ($\Gamma=\frac13[Q^{-1}(P_e/4)]^2$); shaping ≤ 1.53 dB; hard decisions lose ≈ 2 dB. Water-filling $P_i=(\nu-N_i)^+$ (DSL, OFDM, MIMO). Fading lowers capacity (Jensen); MIMO capacity ∝ $\min(N_t,N_r)$.
