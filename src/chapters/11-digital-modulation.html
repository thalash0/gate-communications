History · from telegraph clicks to 1024-QAM

The oldest digital modulation is ASK: Samuel Morse's key switching a current on and off (1844), later a radio spark. Frequency-shift keying arrived with early radioteletype and the Bell 103 modem (1962; two tones for each direction, 300 bit/s). Phase-shift keying came with the space race: Mariner and Voyager use BPSK because it gets the lowest error rate for the little power they can spare. Then in 1960s AT&T and Codex realised that phase and amplitude could be varied together: QAM. The V.29 fax modem (9600 bit/s, 1976) used 16-QAM; V.32 (1984) added the trellis-coded modulation of Gottfried Ungerboeck (1982), which married the error-correcting codes of Chapter 12 to the constellation, squeezing 33.6 kbit/s out of the ordinary phone line, within a few kbit/s of Shannon's limit. Today your Wi-Fi 6 router sends 1024 points per symbol (10 bits).

The bandpass signal in the IQ plane

From Chapter 2, any digital carrier signal can be written $$s(t)=\sum_k\Big[I_k\,p(t-kT)\cos2\pi f_ct-Q_k\,p(t-kT)\sin2\pi f_ct\Big],$$ where $(I_k,Q_k)$ is the $k$-th symbol, a point in the I–Q plane, and $p(t)$ the pulse shape (rectangular, or the raised-cosine of Chapter 9). The collection of allowed points is the constellation. Everything follows from the picture:

  • $M$ points ⇒ $\log_2M$ bits per symbol: $R_b=R_s\log_2M$.
  • The distance of a point from the origin is the amplitude; its angle is the phase. Average symbol energy $E_s=\overline{I^2+Q^2}\times\tfrac{T}2$ (the $\tfrac12$ from the carrier, Chapter 2) and $E_b=E_s/\log_2M$.
  • Noise is a round Gaussian cloud around each point (Chapter 10). Error probability is dominated by the minimum distance $d_{\min}$ between points: for a given $E_s$ we want the points spread as far apart as possible.
  • The signal's power spectrum is the baseband spectrum of $p(t)$ shifted to $\pm f_c$: bandwidth $2\times$ the baseband bandwidth. With a raised-cosine, $B=(1+\alpha)R_s$.

The family, one by one

ASK / OOK

$s(t)=A\,m(t)\cos2\pi f_ct$, $m\in\{0,1\}$. Simple, but the two points are $0$ and $A$: the "1" must carry all the distance, so for the same average energy it behaves like orthogonal signalling: $P_b=Q\big(\sqrt{E_b/N_0}\big)$ (coherent) with $E_b$ the average energy. It can be detected without a carrier reference by an envelope detector (non-coherent), at a small penalty. Bandwidth $=2R_b$ (null-to-null with rectangular pulses).

BPSK

$s=\pm A\cos2\pi f_ct$. Two antipodal points: the best possible for binary. $d=2\sqrt{E_b}$: $$P_b=Q\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{2E_b}{N_0}}\Big)=\tfrac12\erfc\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{E_b}{N_0}}\Big).$$ Bandwidth (null-to-null, rectangular pulses) $=2R_b$; with raised-cosine $(1+\alpha)R_b$. It needs a coherent reference (a Costas loop, as in Chapter 4) and has a phase ambiguity: the recovered carrier could be inverted (resolved by differential encoding).

DPSK

Encode the bit in the change of phase from the previous symbol (a "1" means "flip", "0" means "don't"). The receiver multiplies each symbol by the previous one: no carrier recovery. The price: the previous symbol is itself noisy, so noise appears twice: $P_b=\tfrac12e^{-E_b/N_0}$, about 0.75 dB worse than coherent BPSK at $10^{-5}$ (10.3 dB vs 9.6 dB), but far simpler.

QPSK

Four points $(\pm1,\pm1)\sqrt{E_s/2}$: two BPSK signals in quadrature (the quadrature-carrier multiplexing of Chapter 4). Each bit rides its own orthogonal carrier, so they don't interfere, and each sees exactly the BPSK noise problem with the same $E_b$: $$P_b=Q\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{2E_b}{N_0}}\Big)\quad(\text{identical to BPSK}),\qquad P_s=1-\big(1-P_b\big)^2\approx2P_b.$$ Because the symbol rate is half the bit rate, QPSK uses half the bandwidth of BPSK at the same BER: free efficiency. Variants: OQPSK (offset I and Q by $T_s/2$: no $180^\circ$ jumps, less envelope variation) and $\pi/4$-QPSK (IS-136).

$M$-PSK

$M$ points on a circle: $d_{\min}=2\sqrt{E_s}\sin(\pi/M)$, so $P_s\approx2Q\big(\sqrt{2E_s/N_0}\sin\frac\pi M\big)$ and (Gray) $P_b\approx P_s/\log_2M$. Doubling $M$ costs about 3.5–6 dB for large $M$: the points crowd on a circle (8-PSK 13.0 dB, 16-PSK 17.4 dB at $10^{-5}$).

FSK

Send one of $M$ tones. Tones spaced by $k/(2T)$ are orthogonal for coherent detection; by $1/T$ for non-coherent. In signal space, orthogonal tones are the $M$ axes: points at distance $\sqrt{2E_s}$. $P_b=Q(\sqrt{E_b/N_0})$ (coherent binary; 3 dB worse than BPSK), $P_b=\tfrac12e^{-E_b/2N_0}$ (non-coherent binary). But watch the trade for large $M$: more tones means more dimensions (more bandwidth!) and better power efficiency: with $M\to\infty$ the required $E_b/N_0\to-1.59$ dB, the Shannon limit, at the cost of bandwidth $\to\infty$. MSK is binary FSK with tone spacing $\frac{1}{2T}$ and continuous phase: constant envelope, narrow main lobe (null-to-null $1.5R_b$), and the BER of BPSK. GMSK adds a Gaussian filter before the modulator; this is GSM.

Three-tone orthogonal FSK lives in 3-D signal space: the three tones are the three axes and the three signal points sit at distance $\sqrt{E_s}$ along each. The noise cloud (500 samples around the selected symbol) is a round ball. The decision boundaries are the planes equidistant from two axes (e.g. $x=y$). Increase the noise and watch points cross the planes. Neighbouring signal points are $\sqrt{2E_s}$ apart, versus $2\sqrt{E_s}$ for antipodal signals: the 3 dB penalty of Chapter 10.

QAM

Put the points on a square grid: $\sqrt M\times\sqrt M$ levels of $I$ and $Q$. Because the grid fills the plane, it packs points much more tightly than a ring. For square $M$-QAM with minimum distance $d$: $E_s=\frac{(M-1)d^2}{6}$ (check: QPSK, $M=4$: $E_s=d^2/2$ ✓), and $$P_s\approx4\Big(1-\frac1{\sqrt M}\Big)Q\Big(\sqrt{\frac{3E_s}{(M-1)N_0}}\Big),\qquad P_b\approx\frac4{\log_2M}\Big(1-\frac1{\sqrt M}\Big)Q\Big(\sqrt{\frac{3\log_2M}{M-1}\frac{E_b}{N_0}}\Big).$$ The price of density is power: each doubling of $M$ (one more bit/symbol) costs about 3 dB more $E_b/N_0$ at high $M$ (16-QAM 13.4 dB, 64-QAM 17.8 dB, 256-QAM 22.5 dB at $10^{-5}$), and the receiver needs ever cleaner SNR, linear amplifiers (the signal has amplitude variations: high peak-to-average power ratio) and accurate phase tracking. This is why 256-QAM is used on short, clean links (cable, home Wi-Fi) while the deep-space link uses BPSK.

Worked examples

Average energy of 16-QAM. Levels $\{\pm1,\pm3\}$ on each axis ($d=2$): mean of $x^2$ is $\frac{1+9}2=5$; $E_s\propto I^2+Q^2=10$; compare $\frac{(M-1)d^2}6=\frac{15\cdot4}6=10$ ✓. $E_b=E_s/4$.

GATE 2022 Q.35. A stream alternates QPSK (2 bits) and 16-QAM (4 bits) symbols at 1 Msym/s: average $=\frac{2+4}{2}=3$ bits/symbol ⇒ raw data rate $=\mathbf{3.0}$ Mbit/s (key 2.99–3.01).

GATE 2026 Q.32. 16-QAM at 4 Mbit/s: $R_s=1$ Msym/s, minimum bandwidth $=R_s=1$ MHz (ideal Nyquist, passband). GATE 2026 Q.44. QPSK at $E_b/N_0=8.4$ dB: $\approx10^{-4}$ (Chapter 10).

Spectral efficiency. Passband raised-cosine: $\dfrac{R_b}{B}=\dfrac{\log_2M}{1+\alpha}$ bit/s/Hz: QPSK with $\alpha=0.35$: $1.48$; 64-QAM, $\alpha=0.25$: $4.8$.

Traps
  • BER vs symbol error: QPSK $P_s\approx2P_b$; $M$-ary Gray: $P_b\approx P_s/\log_2M$. Read which one the question asks.
  • $E_s/N_0$ vs $E_b/N_0$: $E_s=E_b\log_2M$. In dB, subtract $10\log_2M\cdot\ldots$ e.g. QPSK: $E_s/N_0=E_b/N_0+3$ dB.
  • "Bandwidth" may mean null-to-null (rectangular pulses, $2R_s$ passband), Nyquist ($R_s$), or raised-cosine ($(1+\alpha)R_s$). The problem will usually say, or say "minimum".
  • FSK has no $\sqrt2$ advantage like BPSK: coherent BFSK is 3 dB worse; non-coherent is a further ≈0.8 dB.

Practice

A symbol stream contains alternate QPSK and 16-QAM symbols. If symbols are transmitted at 1 mega-symbol per second, the raw (uncoded) data rate is ______ Mbit/s (one decimal).

Average bits per symbol $=(2+4)/2=3$ ⇒ 3.0 Mbit/s.

QPSK in AWGN has $E_s/N_0=10$ dB. The bit error probability, using $P_b=Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})$ with Gray coding, is about ______ ($Q(3.16)=7.8\times10^{-4}$).

$E_s=2E_b$ ⇒ $E_b/N_0=5$; $\sqrt{2\cdot5}=3.16$; $P_b=7.8\times10^{-4}$.

A modem carries 2 Mbit/s using 16-QAM with raised-cosine pulses in a passband channel of 1 MHz. The maximum roll-off factor $\alpha$ allowed is ______.

$R_s=2/4=0.5$ Msym/s; $B=(1+\alpha)R_s=1$ MHz ⇒ $\alpha=1$.

An 8-PSK system signals at 1.2 Mbaud. The bit rate in Mbit/s is ______.

$1.2\times\log_28=3.6$.

Compared with BPSK at the same bit rate and the same $E_b/N_0$, QPSK has
  1. half the BER and the same bandwidth
  2. the same BER and half the bandwidth
  3. twice the BER and half the bandwidth
  4. the same BER and twice the bandwidth

Two orthogonal BPSK streams, each at half the bit rate: same BER, half the bandwidth.

Which statements are TRUE?
  1. Coherent BFSK needs 3 dB more $E_b/N_0$ than BPSK for the same BER.
  2. 16-QAM and 16-PSK need the same $E_b/N_0$ for a given BER.
  3. MSK is a continuous-phase FSK with tone spacing $1/(2T)$.
  4. DPSK can be demodulated without carrier recovery.

(A) ✓ ($\sqrt\gamma$ vs $\sqrt{2\gamma}$). (B) ✗: 16-QAM (13.4 dB) beats 16-PSK (17.4 dB) by 4 dB because QAM points are farther apart. (C) ✓. (D) ✓.

A square 16-QAM constellation has $I,Q\in\{\pm1,\pm3\}$ (units of volts, amplitude scaling so that $E_s=\overline{I^2+Q^2}$). The average energy per bit (same units) is ______.

$\overline{I^2+Q^2}=10$, $E_b=10/4=2.5$.

Non-coherent binary FSK has $P_b=\frac12e^{-E_b/2N_0}$. The $E_b/N_0$ (dB) needed for $P_b=10^{-5}$ is ______.

$e^{-\gamma/2}=2\times10^{-5}$ ⇒ $\gamma/2=10.82$ ⇒ $\gamma=21.6\to13.35$ dB.

An ideal band-pass channel occupies 500 Hz–2000 Hz. A modem sends 4800 bit/s using 16-QAM with raised-cosine pulses that exactly fill the band. The roll-off factor is ______.

$B=1500$ Hz $=(1+\alpha)R_s$ (passband), $R_s=4800/4=1200$ sym/s ⇒ $1+\alpha=1.25$, $\alpha=\mathbf{0.25}$.

A GSM carrier occupies 200 kHz and is shared by 8 users in TDMA. At a given time 12 users are talking in a cell. The minimum total bandwidth (kHz) of the signal received by the base station is ______.

One 200 kHz carrier supports 8 time slots. 12 users need $\lceil12/8\rceil=2$ carriers: $2\times200=\mathbf{400}$ kHz. (GSM modulation itself is GMSK: constant-envelope MSK with a Gaussian filter.)

Chapter 11 in one breath

  • Symbols are points in the IQ plane; $R_b=R_s\log_2M$; $E_b=E_s/\log_2M$; noise cloud $\sigma^2=N_0/2$; $d_{\min}$ rules.
  • BPSK = QPSK BER $Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})$; QPSK half the bandwidth. BFSK coherent $Q(\sqrt{E_b/N_0})$; non-coherent $\frac12e^{-E_b/2N_0}$; DPSK $\frac12e^{-E_b/N_0}$.
  • $M$-PSK: $2Q\big(\sqrt{2E_s/N_0}\sin\frac\pi M\big)$. Square $M$-QAM: $E_s=\frac{(M-1)d^2}6$, $P_s\approx4(1-\frac1{\sqrt M})Q\big(\sqrt{3E_s/((M-1)N_0)}\big)$.
  • Bandwidth: null-null $2R_s$, Nyquist $R_s$, raised-cosine $(1+\alpha)R_s$; efficiency $\frac{\log_2M}{1+\alpha}$.
  • PSK/QAM = bandwidth-efficient, power-hungry; FSK = power-efficient, bandwidth-hungry; MSK = constant envelope with BPSK BER.
