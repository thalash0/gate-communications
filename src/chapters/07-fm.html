History · the man who would not stop

Static. Every AM listener in the 1920s knew it: lightning, car ignitions and electric motors all add random amplitude spikes, and an AM receiver hears them all. Edwin Howard Armstrong had already given the world the regenerative receiver (1912) and the superheterodyne (1918), but static bothered him. If the message rides on the amplitude, noise will always corrupt it. What if it rode on the frequency instead, and the receiver simply ignored amplitude?

The conventional wisdom came from John R. Carson of AT&T, who analysed FM in 1922 and concluded that it could never beat AM: its spectrum was wider, and “this type of modulation inherently distorts without any compensating advantages.” Carson’s reasoning was right for narrow deviations. Armstrong guessed what Carson’s analysis missed: if you swing the frequency very widely, you pay in bandwidth but the noise suppression grows even faster. In 1933 he demonstrated wide-band FM, static-free; his first FM station, W2XMN at Alpine, New Jersey, went on air in 1938. Powerful interests (RCA, who owned the AM networks and television plans) fought him for years, and the patent suits drained his fortune. Armstrong died in 1954. His widow won the lawsuits.

The irony: the rule for the bandwidth of an FM signal that every engineer uses today ($B\approx2(\Delta f+f_m)$) is called Carson’s rule.

Real world · where FM lives

FM broadcast (88–108 MHz; in India the AIR FM stations), the sound carrier of analog TV, two-way police and taxi radios (narrow-band FM, $\pm5$ kHz), Bluetooth and GSM (both use a smoothed frequency-shift keying, GFSK/GMSK, which is digital FM), satellite TV (the video is frequency-modulated onto the microwave carrier), the “chirp” of radar, LiDAR and LoRa, and the phase-locked loops inside every processor, phone and radio (clock generation, synthesisers, clock recovery).

Phase, frequency, and the spinning arrow again

Write any sinusoid as $s(t)=A_c\cos\theta(t)$. The instantaneous frequency is the speed at which the phase angle $\theta$ advances, $f_i(t)=\dfrac1{2\pi}\dfrac{d\theta}{dt}$ (Chapter 3). For an unmodulated carrier $\theta=2\pi f_ct$ and $f_i=f_c$. There are two ways to impose a message: $$\textbf{PM:}\quad\theta(t)=2\pi f_ct+k_p\,m(t)\ \Rightarrow\ f_i=f_c+\frac{k_p}{2\pi}\frac{dm}{dt},$$ $$\textbf{FM:}\quad\theta(t)=2\pi f_ct+2\pi k_f\!\int_{-\infty}^{t}\!m(\tau)d\tau\ \Rightarrow\ f_i=f_c+k_f\,m(t).$$ In FM the instantaneous frequency follows the message; in PM it follows the derivative of the message. They are therefore the same thing in disguise: FM is PM of the integral of $m$, PM is FM of the derivative of $m$. The envelope $A_c$ is constant for both, so the transmitted power is $A_c^2/2$ whatever the message does. (Contrast AM, Chapter 6, where power depends on the depth.)

Why the integral? Think of an arrow spinning at angular speed $\omega(t)$: the angle it has turned is the integral of the speed. To make the speed follow $m(t)$ the angle must be $\int m$; just as the odometer is the integral of the speedometer.

Narrowband FM: the small-angle special case

When $\beta\ll1$ we can linearise. For $m(t)=A_m\cos\omega_mt$ the FM signal is $s=A_c\cos(\omega_ct+\beta\sin\omega_mt)$. Expand the cosine of a sum and use $\cos x\approx1$, $\sin x\approx x$ for small $x=\beta\sin\omega_mt$: $$s=A_c\big[\cos\omega_ct\cos(\beta\sin\omega_mt)-\sin\omega_ct\sin(\beta\sin\omega_mt)\big]\approx A_c\cos\omega_ct-A_c\beta\sin\omega_mt\,\sin\omega_ct.$$ Using $\sin a\sin b=\frac12[\cos(a-b)-\cos(a+b)]$: $s\approx A_c\cos\omega_ct-\dfrac{\beta A_c}2\cos(\omega_c-\omega_m)t+\dfrac{\beta A_c}2\cos(\omega_c+\omega_m)t$. Compare with single-tone AM from Chapter 6, $A_c\cos\omega_ct+\frac{\mu A_c}2\cos(\omega_c-\omega_m)t+\frac{\mu A_c}2\cos(\omega_c+\omega_m)t$. Same three spectral lines, and the same $2f_m$ bandwidth, but the lower sideband has the opposite sign. In phasor language: AM’s sidebands add in line with the carrier (the resultant grows and shrinks); NBFM’s sidebands add in quadrature to the carrier (the resultant swings back and forth in angle with almost constant length). Hence the 90° of Chapter 3: AM moves the tip of the complex envelope along a radius; FM moves it along a circle. (The small residual amplitude variation $\sqrt{1+\beta^2\sin^2\omega_mt}$ is the price of the linearisation, and disappears as more sidebands are included.)

Engineering practice · why narrowband FM is still used

Two-way radios, marine VHF, taxi and police radios, and pagers use NBFM with $\Delta f=2.5$–$5$ kHz and $f_m\le3$ kHz (so $B\approx2(5+3)=16$ kHz in a $25$ kHz channel; modern 12.5 kHz channels use $\Delta f=2.5$ kHz). The combination is a good compromise: much better noise performance and interference resistance than AM, constant envelope (so cheap, efficient class-C power amplifiers can be used; very important for battery handsets), and a receiver as simple as a discriminator.

The spectrum of a frequency-modulated tone

AM was easy because multiplication shifts the spectrum. FM is non-linear: $\cos(\omega_ct+\beta\sin\omega_mt)$ is the real part of $e^{j\omega_ct}\cdot e^{j\beta\sin\omega_mt}$, and the second factor is periodic in time with period $1/f_m$, so it has a Fourier series.

Derivation · the Bessel spectrum

The complex Fourier series of $g(t)=e^{j\beta\sin\omega_mt}$ (Chapter 2, with $x=\omega_mt$ so the period is $2\pi$) has coefficients $$c_n=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{j\beta\sin x}\,e^{-jnx}\,dx\ \overset{\rm def}{=}\ J_n(\beta),$$ the Bessel function of the first kind of order $n$ (Friedrich Bessel, 1824, who invented it for planetary perturbations, not radio). Since $g(t)$ is the sum of its harmonics, $$e^{j\beta\sin\omega_mt}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_n(\beta)\,e^{jn\omega_mt}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad s(t)=\mathrm{Re}\{A_ce^{j\omega_ct}g(t)\}=A_c\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_n(\beta)\cos\big[(\omega_c+n\omega_m)t\big].$$ So a single-tone FM wave is a carrier plus an infinite ladder of sidebands at $f_c\pm nf_m$, with amplitudes $J_n(\beta)$. Properties, each easy to prove from the integral:

  • $\sum_nJ_n^2(\beta)=1$ for every $\beta$: Parseval for the series, because $|g(t)|=1$ so its average power is 1. Hence the total power is constant, $A_c^2/2$ ✓ (the algebraic version of “constant envelope”). The power in the $n$th line is $\frac{A_c^2}2J_n^2(\beta)$.
  • $J_{-n}(\beta)=(-1)^nJ_n(\beta)$ (substitute $x\to-x$), so the sidebands are symmetric in magnitude.
  • Recurrence $J_{n-1}(\beta)+J_{n+1}(\beta)=\frac{2n}\beta J_n(\beta)$, which lets you fill in a table from two values.
  • For small $\beta$: $J_0\approx1-\beta^2/4$, $J_1\approx\beta/2$, $J_{n\ge2}\approx(\beta/2)^n/n!$. This reproduces narrow-band FM above.
  • The carrier amplitude $J_0(\beta)$ goes to zero at $\beta=2.405,\ 5.520,\ 8.654,\ 11.79,\dots$: at those values all the power is in the sidebands. This is used to calibrate deviation (set $f_m$ and raise the audio level until the carrier vanishes on a spectrum analyser; then $\Delta f=2.405f_m$) and is a favourite exam trick. Similarly $J_1=0$ at $\beta=3.832,7.016,\dots$.
  • For large $\beta$ the sidebands are significant out to $|n|\approx\beta+1$: the spectrum spreads over about $2\beta f_m=2\Delta f$.

Multi-tone messages: no superposition

FM is non-linear: for a message of two tones the spectrum is not the sum of the two single-tone spectra. Writing $e^{j(\beta_1\sin\omega_1t+\beta_2\sin\omega_2t)}$ as a product of two Bessel series gives lines at $f_c\pm nf_1\pm kf_2$ with amplitudes $J_n(\beta_1)J_k(\beta_2)$: carrier, each tone’s sidebands and cross-product (“intermodulation”) lines. This is the reason noise-like speech, music and stereo multiplex signals (which have a very large number of components) are analysed through Carson’s rule rather than line by line.

How wide is an FM signal? Carson’s rule

In theory infinite, in practice more than 98% of the power lies in the sidebands with $|n|\le\beta+1$ (numerically: $\beta=1$: 99.9%, $\beta=2$: 99.8%, $\beta=5$: 99.4%, $\beta=10$: 99.0%). That is Carson’s rule: $$\boxed{\,B_T\approx2(\Delta f+f_m)=2f_m(\beta+1)\,}\qquad\text{(general message: }B_T=2(\Delta f+W)=2W(D+1),\ D=\Delta f/W).$$ Check the extremes: NBFM ($\beta\ll1$): $B_T\approx2f_m$ ✓ same as AM. Wide-band FM ($\beta\gg1$): $B_T\approx2\Delta f$: the bandwidth is just the swing of the frequency, as common sense says (a “quasi-static” picture: the signal just sweeps over $f_c\pm\Delta f$, and the spectrum is the histogram of the instantaneous frequency).

Worked example · FM broadcast

Peak deviation $\Delta f=75$ kHz, audio up to $W=15$ kHz: $D=5$, $B_T=2(75+15)=180$ kHz, fitted into a 200 kHz channel. Compare AM broadcast with the same audio: 30 kHz. FM pays 6× the bandwidth, and (Chapter 8) buys a large SNR improvement for it. This is the bandwidth-for-noise trade that Armstrong understood and Carson did not. The stereo multiplex (below) extends the baseband to 53 kHz (plus RDS to 60 kHz) but the same $\Delta f=75$ kHz limit still applies, so the effective $D$ falls to $75/53\approx1.4$ for the stereo subchannel, one reason stereo reception is noisier.

Traps
  • Carson’s rule uses peak deviation $\Delta f$, not $\beta$ alone; for PM with a tone, $\Delta f=\beta f_m$, so $B_T=2f_m(\beta+1)$ grows with $f_m$.
  • If the message amplitude and frequency both change, recompute $\Delta f$ for FM (depends on amplitude only) and $\beta$ (amplitude/frequency).
  • FM power does not depend on $\beta$. A common mistake is to apply $P=P_c(1+\mu^2/2)$.
  • For a message of several tones, $\Delta f=k_f\max|m|$ is $k_f\times$ the sum of the tone amplitudes (the peaks can coincide), and $W$ is the highest tone.
Engineering practice · the FM chirp: bandwidth × time = processing gain

Let the frequency sweep linearly, $f_i=f_0+\kappa t$ over a duration $T$ (a chirp, linear FM). The phase is $\theta=2\pi(f_0t+\frac12\kappa t^2)$ and the bandwidth is $B=\kappa T$ (Carson with $\beta\gg1$). A matched filter (Chapter 14) compresses the long pulse of energy $A^2T/2$ into a narrow peak of width $\approx1/B$, giving a pulse-compression gain equal to the time–bandwidth product $BT$. A radar chirp of $B=10$ MHz over $T=100\,\mu$s has $BT=1000$ (30 dB): it detects like a short, very high-power pulse while the transmitter needs only modest peak power. The same chirp, at lower rates, is the modulation of LoRa IoT networks and of FMCW automotive radar.

Making FM, and what non-linearities do to it

Direct FM

Make an oscillator whose frequency depends on a voltage: a VCO. In an LC oscillator $f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$; put a varactor diode (capacitance that varies with reverse voltage) in the tank so $C=C_0+\Delta C\,m(t)$. For small $\Delta C$, $f\approx f_0\big[1-\frac{\Delta C}{2C_0}m(t)\big]$: the frequency follows $m$ linearly. Simple, wide deviation, but the centre frequency drifts with temperature and supply, so direct FM transmitters need automatic frequency control (a PLL locking the average frequency to a crystal, below).

Indirect (Armstrong) FM

Integrate the message, use it for narrow-band phase modulation of a crystal oscillator (stable but only $\beta\lesssim0.5$), then use frequency multipliers.

Derivation · multipliers multiply the phase, mixers do not

Pass $A\cos(\omega_ct+\varphi(t))$ through $y=x^n$ (or any strongly non-linear device) and use a band-pass filter tuned to the $n$th harmonic: by $\cos^n\theta\ni\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}\cos n\theta$ the output contains $\cos(n\omega_ct+n\varphi(t))$. The phase deviation is multiplied by $n$, so $f_c$, $\Delta f$ and $\beta$ all multiply by $n$; message frequency is unchanged. A mixer (multiply by $\cos\omega_Lt$ and keep the difference) shifts the carrier to $f_c-f_L$ but leaves $\varphi(t)$, hence $\Delta f$ and $\beta$, unchanged: $\cos(\omega_c t+\varphi)\cos\omega_Lt\to\frac12\cos((\omega_c-\omega_L)t+\varphi)$.

Worked example · an Armstrong transmitter for 91.2 MHz broadcast FM

Target: carrier $91.2$ MHz, $\Delta f=75$ kHz, audio $50$ Hz–$15$ kHz. The narrowband stage can tolerate $\beta\le0.5$ at the lowest audio frequency, so with $f_{m,\min}=50$ Hz: $\Delta f_1\le25$ Hz. Choose a crystal at $f_1=200$ kHz, $\Delta f_1=25$ Hz. The total multiplication needed is $75\,000/25=3000$. Chain: $\times64$ → $12.8$ MHz, $\Delta f=1.6$ kHz; mix with a $10.9$ MHz crystal and keep the difference → $1.9$ MHz, still $\Delta f=1.6$ kHz; $\times48$ → $91.2$ MHz, $\Delta f=76.8$ kHz (total multiplication $3072$; the deviation is then trimmed to 75 kHz by the audio level). The mixer is the trick that keeps the multiplier chain from needing a 200 kHz × 3000 = 600 MHz first stage: it lets us multiply first, shift down, and multiply again.

Worked example · GATE 2023 Q.45: a non-linearity applied to FM

$x(t)=A\cos(\omega_ct+k_f\!\int m)$ occupies a band of width $B_T$ around $f_c$. It passes through $y=2x+5x^2$. Since $x^2=\frac{A^2}2[1+\cos(2\omega_ct+2k_f\!\int m)]$, the output contains: (i) a DC term; (ii) the original FM band at $f_c\pm B_T/2$; (iii) a new FM signal at $2f_c$ with doubled phase deviation, so width $\approx2B_T$, spanning $2f_c\pm B_T$. To recover $x$ with a band-pass filter, bands (ii) and (iii) must not overlap: $f_c+\frac{B_T}2\le2f_c-B_T$, i.e. $f_c\ge\frac32B_T$. Answer (B) $\frac32B_T$ (the official key). A non-linear amplifier is harmless to FM (the information is in the zero crossings) as long as a filter removes the harmonics: this is why FM transmitters use efficient class-C stages.

Getting the message back

The discriminator

Differentiate $s(t)=A_c\cos\theta(t)$: $\dot s=-A_c\dot\theta\sin\theta(t)=-2\pi A_c\,f_i(t)\,\sin\theta(t)$. The result is an AM wave whose envelope is proportional to the instantaneous frequency $f_i=f_c+k_fm$, i.e. to $m(t)$ plus a DC. An envelope detector finishes the job. In the frequency domain this is obvious: a differentiator has $|H|=2\pi f$, a ramp, which turns frequency deviation into amplitude deviation. In practice a tuned circuit’s skirt acts as the differentiator, and a balanced pair of such circuits (tuned slightly above and below $f_c$) cancels the DC and doubles the linear range: the balanced discriminator, whose “S-curve” of output voltage against frequency is linear over about $\pm\Delta f$ and then bends over. Foster–Seeley, ratio and quadrature detectors are refinements; the last, a multiplier fed with the signal and a 90°-delayed copy, dominates in integrated circuits.

A limiter before the discriminator clips off any amplitude variation: this is the entire secret of FM’s static immunity. Only noise that moves the zero crossings survives.

FM demodulator: band-pass filter → limiter (kills amplitude noise) → differentiator (turns frequency into amplitude) → envelope detector → DC block → message.

The phase-locked loop

The workhorse of modern FM receivers, and of far more besides. A voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) is compared with the input by a phase detector; the error voltage, filtered, steers the VCO until it tracks the input phase. If the input frequency wanders, the control voltage must wander the same way to keep the VCO in step, so the control voltage is the demodulated message.

Phase-locked loop used as an FM demodulator.
Derivation · the PLL equations, lock range and steady-state error

Input $A\sin(\omega_ct+\theta_i(t))$, VCO output $\cos(\omega_ct+\theta_o(t))$ with $\dot\theta_o=K_vv_c$ ($K_v$ in rad/s per volt). The multiplier output is $\frac A2\sin(\theta_i-\theta_o)+(\text{terms at }2\omega_c)$; the loop filter removes the $2\omega_c$ terms, leaving $v_d=K_d\sin\theta_e$ with phase error $\theta_e=\theta_i-\theta_o$. For a simple loop with no extra filter ($v_c=v_d$), differentiating $\theta_e$: $$\boxed{\ \dot\theta_e=\dot\theta_i-K\sin\theta_e,\qquad K=K_dK_v\ \ (\mathrm{rad/s})\ }$$ Lock range. If the input frequency is offset by a constant $\Delta\omega$ from the VCO’s free-running frequency, $\dot\theta_i=\Delta\omega$ and a steady state $\dot\theta_e=0$ needs $\sin\theta_e=\Delta\omega/K$: it exists only if $|\Delta\omega|\le K$. So the loop can track offsets up to $\pm K$ rad/s, with a static phase error $\theta_e=\arcsin(\Delta\omega/K)$ that grows from 0 to $90^\circ$ at the edge; beyond it, the loop slips cycles and never locks. Linearised model. For $\theta_e$ small, $\sin\theta_e\approx\theta_e$ and, in the Laplace domain, $$H(s)=\frac{\Theta_o(s)}{\Theta_i(s)}=\frac{K}{s+K}:$$ a first-order low-pass with bandwidth $K$: the VCO phase tracks the input phase for message components below $K/2\pi$ Hz. For an FM input $\theta_i=2\pi k_f\int m$, the control voltage $v_c=\dot\theta_o/K_v\approx\dot\theta_i/K_v=2\pi k_fm(t)/K_v$: a faithful, linear copy of the message, as long as $K$ exceeds $\max|\dot\theta_i|=2\pi\Delta f$ (i.e. the loop is wider than the deviation) and the loop passes the message band. Second-order loop. Insert a proportional–integral filter $F(s)=\frac{1+s\tau_2}{s\tau_1}$: then $H(s)=\dfrac{(K/\tau_1)(1+s\tau_2)}{s^2+(K\tau_2/\tau_1)s+K/\tau_1}$ with natural frequency $\omega_n=\sqrt{K/\tau_1}$ and damping $\zeta=\frac12\tau_2\omega_n$ (typically $\zeta\approx0.7$). The integrator drives the steady-state phase error for a frequency step to zero, and the lock range becomes limited only by the VCO’s tuning range.

First-order PLL simulated in baseband. The blue curve is the input phase (an FM tone with swing $\beta=3$ rad at 1 Hz); the loop tries to make the VCO phase (yellow) follow it. The bottom plot is the phase error. With a high loop gain $K$ the error stays small and the control signal reproduces the message; with $K$ below the signal’s fastest phase slope ($2\pi\beta f_m\approx19$ rad/s) the loop cannot keep up and loses lock (“cycle slipping”).
Engineering practice · the PLL’s five jobs

(1) FM demodulation, above. (2) Carrier recovery in coherent receivers: a PLL locks to a pilot tone, or (Costas loop, Chapter 16) to the carrier hidden in a DSB-SC or BPSK signal. (3) Clock/data recovery in every serial link (USB, PCIe, Ethernet, optical): the VCO is steered by the transitions of the incoming data (Chapter 16). (4) Frequency synthesis, next. (5) Jitter cleaning and clock multiplication inside every microprocessor: a 100 MHz crystal becomes a 4 GHz core clock. The trade-off in all of them is the same: a narrow loop bandwidth rejects noise on the reference but tracks slowly and has long lock time; a wide loop tracks fast but passes reference noise.

PLL as a frequency synthesizer

The same PLL, used backwards, makes the clocks and carriers of every digital device. Put a divide-by-$N$ counter in the feedback path from the VCO to the phase detector. The loop locks when the divided VCO frequency equals the reference: $f_{VCO}/N=f_{ref}$, so $$f_{out}=N\,f_{ref}.$$ A single crystal reference (very stable, but fixed) thereby yields a whole comb of frequencies, selected by changing $N$; the step size is $f_{ref}$. This is how your radio tunes to 94.3 MHz and how a Wi-Fi chip makes 2.4 GHz from a 40 MHz crystal. (GATE 2016: a 5 kHz reference with ÷2, ÷4, ÷8, ÷16 taps in the feedback path gives outputs of 10, 20, 40, 80 kHz.)

Frequency synthesizer: the loop forces $f_{out}/N=f_{ref}$.
Engineering practice · integer-N, fractional-N and phase noise

For FM broadcast tuning with 100 kHz steps, $f_{ref}=100$ kHz and $N\approx880$–$1080$. A small $f_{ref}$ forces a narrow loop and a huge $N$, which multiplies the reference’s phase noise by $20\log N$ dB ($N=1000$ costs 60 dB). Fractional-N synthesisers alternate $N$ and $N+1$ with a sigma–delta pattern so the average division ratio is fractional, allowing a high $f_{ref}$ with fine steps. The close-in phase noise of the synthesiser matters directly for digital modulation: random rotation of the constellation limits high-order QAM (Chapter 16).

Stereo, pre-emphasis and the capture effect

FM stereo. A mono receiver must still work, so the baseband sent to the FM modulator is $L+R$ (0–15 kHz) plus a 19 kHz pilot tone plus $L-R$ as a DSB-SC signal on a 38 kHz subcarrier (the second harmonic of the pilot, so the receiver regenerates the subcarrier coherently by doubling the pilot). Chapter 6 in action: $$m_{\rm mux}(t)=[L+R]+k_p\cos(2\pi\cdot19\,\mathrm{kHz}\,t)+[L-R]\cos(2\pi\cdot38\,\mathrm{kHz}\,t).$$ A stereo receiver multiplies by $\cos(2\pi\cdot38\text{ kHz }t)$ and low-pass filters to recover $\frac12(L-R)$, then forms $L=\frac12[(L+R)+(L-R)]$ and $R$ by sum and difference (the “matrix”). A mono receiver’s 15 kHz filter simply never sees the subcarrier. RDS (the station-name display) is a BPSK data stream at $1187.5$ bit/s on a $57$ kHz subcarrier $(=3\times19$ kHz).

Baseband spectrum of an FM stereo multiplex signal.

Pre-emphasis / de-emphasis. After the discriminator, FM noise has a PSD that grows as $f^2$ (derived in Chapter 8), but speech and music energy falls with frequency. The fix: boost the highs before transmission, $H_{pe}(f)=1+j\frac f{f_0}$ ($\tau=\frac1{2\pi f_0}=75\,\mu$s in the USA, $50\,\mu$s in India/Europe), and apply the exact inverse $H_{de}=\dfrac1{1+jf/f_0}$ (an RC low-pass with $RC=\tau$) after detection. The message sees $H_{pe}H_{de}=1$ but the noise is shaped by $H_{de}$ alone: it is attenuated at high frequencies, where it was strongest. (The corner $f_0=2.1$–$3.2$ kHz is chosen where programme energy starts to fall. A transmitter limiter keeps the boosted highs from over-deviating.)

Derivation · the capture effect and FM’s interference rejection

Let a wanted FM signal of amplitude $A$ and an interferer of amplitude $a$ on the same channel arrive together. Their phasor sum is $Ae^{j\theta_1(t)}\big[1+\frac aAe^{j(\theta_2-\theta_1)}\big]$. For $a\ll A$, $\ln(1+\epsilon)\approx\epsilon$ gives a total phase $\theta_1+\frac aA\sin(\theta_2-\theta_1)$: the discriminator output is the wanted $\dot\theta_1$ plus an interference term of amplitude only $\propto a/A$, and the amplitude disturbance is removed by the limiter. For $a/A=0.1$ the interference is 20 dB down; as $a/A\to1$ the approximation fails and the receiver abruptly captures the stronger signal: a margin of just 1–3 dB in desired-to-interferer ratio gives a clean output with the weaker signal suppressed almost completely. Contrast AM, where both signals are heard additively. Capture is wonderful for broadcast (neighbouring stations vanish) and dreadful in a pilot–tower radio (a weaker aircraft can be blotted out entirely, so aviation uses AM).

Set A · Concept checks

Which statements about a tone-modulated FM signal are correct?
  1. The transmitted power is independent of $\beta$.
  2. The carrier line always has the largest amplitude among all lines.
  3. The sum of the squares of all line amplitudes (normalised to the carrier amplitude) is 1.
  4. The bandwidth is exactly $2\Delta f$.

(A) and (C): constant envelope ⇒ constant power, and $\sum J_n^2=1$. (B) false: at $\beta=2.405$ the carrier vanishes and at $\beta=5$ the largest line is $J_4$. (D) false: Carson is approximate and includes $f_m$: $2(\Delta f+f_m)$.

The component of an FM receiver that removes the effect of amplitude noise is the
  1. discriminator
  2. limiter
  3. de-emphasis network
  4. local oscillator

Amplitude noise is clipped off by the limiter; only zero-crossing (phase) noise passes. (B).

A tone $m(t)=A_m\cos2\pi f_mt$ drives an FM modulator. If $f_m$ is doubled (amplitude fixed), then
  1. $\Delta f$ doubles and $\beta$ is unchanged
  2. both $\Delta f$ and $\beta$ double
  3. $\Delta f$ is unchanged and $\beta$ halves
  4. $\Delta f$ halves and $\beta$ doubles

FM: $\Delta f=k_fA_m$ is independent of $f_m$; $\beta=\Delta f/f_m$ halves. (C).

Set B · Numerical practice

$m(t)=3\cos(2\pi\cdot2000\,t)$ V frequency-modulates a carrier with sensitivity $k_f=10$ kHz/V. The Carson bandwidth in kHz is ______.

$\Delta f=10\times3=30$ kHz; $B_T=2(30+2)=\mathbf{64}$ kHz. ($\beta=15$.)

A single-tone FM signal with $f_m=5$ kHz has zero carrier amplitude. The smallest non-zero frequency deviation (kHz) for which this happens is ______.

$J_0(\beta)=0$ at $\beta=2.405$: $\Delta f=2.405\times5=12.02$ kHz.

An FM wave $s(t)=10\cos(2\pi\cdot10^6t+7\sin2\pi\cdot10^3t)$ is applied across a $1\,\Omega$ resistor. The power in watts is ______.

Constant envelope: $P=A_c^2/2=50$ W, whatever $\beta$ is.

A narrow-band FM signal has $\beta=0.2$. What percentage of its total power is in the two first-order sidebands? (Use $J_0(0.2)=0.990$, $J_1(0.2)=0.0995$.)

$2J_1^2=2(0.0995)^2=0.0198$: about 1.98% of the power (the carrier keeps $J_0^2=98\%$).

$m(t)=2\cos(2\pi\cdot1000t)+\cos(2\pi\cdot3000t)$ V frequency-modulates a carrier with $k_f=5$ kHz/V. The Carson bandwidth in kHz is ______.

The peaks can coincide: $\max|m|=3$ V, so $\Delta f=5\times3=15$ kHz. The highest message frequency is $W=3$ kHz. $B_T=2(15+3)=\mathbf{36}$ kHz.

A narrowband-FM two-way radio has a peak deviation of 5 kHz and a maximum audio frequency of 3 kHz. The Carson bandwidth in kHz is ______.

$2(5+3)=\mathbf{16}$ kHz, fitting a 25 kHz channel.

An Armstrong FM modulator produces $\Delta f_1=20$ Hz at its narrowband stage. The total frequency-multiplication factor needed to reach $\Delta f=75$ kHz is ______.

$75\,000/20=\mathbf{3750}$. (Mixers do not change $\Delta f$; only multipliers do.)

A first-order PLL uses a phase detector with $K_d=0.5$ V/rad and a VCO with sensitivity $K_v=2\pi\times10^4$ rad/s per volt. The largest constant frequency offset (in Hz) between input and the free-running VCO that the loop can track is ______.

$K=K_dK_v=0.5\times2\pi\times10^4=31\,416$ rad/s, so $|\Delta\omega|\le K\Rightarrow|\Delta f|\le K/2\pi=\mathbf{5000}$ Hz.

For the same loop ($K/2\pi=5$ kHz), the input is offset by $2$ kHz. The steady-state phase error in degrees is ______.

$\sin\theta_e=\Delta\omega/K=2/5=0.4\Rightarrow\theta_e=\arcsin0.4=\mathbf{23.6}^\circ$.

A single-tone FM signal has $\beta=2$. What percentage of the total power is in the carrier? ($J_0(2)=0.224$.)

$J_0^2=0.224^2=0.050$: 5.0%. The carrier of a wide-band FM signal contains little power: unlike AM, the “carrier” carries no wasted power.

A wanted FM signal is 10 times stronger in amplitude than a co-channel interferer. The peak phase disturbance the interferer causes in the wanted signal, in radians, is about ______.

$a/A=1/10=\mathbf{0.1}$ rad (interference reduced by 20 dB; derivation in the capture-effect box).

A radar sends a linear FM chirp sweeping 10 MHz in 100 µs. The pulse-compression gain from matched filtering, in dB, is ______.

$BT=10^7\times10^{-4}=1000\to10\log_{10}1000=\mathbf{30}$ dB.

  1. $B_T+W$

  3. $2B_T+W$

An FM broadcast uses the pre-emphasis filter $H_{pe}(\omega)=1+j\omega/\omega_0$ with $\omega_0=10^4$ rad/s. For an RC low-pass network to act as the corresponding de-emphasis filter, an appropriate pair $(R,C)$ is
  1. $R=1\ \mathrm{k\Omega},\ C=0.1\ \mu$F
  2. $R=2\ \mathrm{k\Omega},\ C=1\ \mu$F
  3. $R=1\ \mathrm{k\Omega},\ C=2\ \mu$F
  4. $R=2\ \mathrm{k\Omega},\ C=0.5\ \mu$F

$H_{de}=\frac1{1+j\omega RC}$ must equal $\frac1{1+j\omega/\omega_0}$: $RC=10^{-4}$ s. (A): $10^3\cdot10^{-7}=10^{-4}$ ✓. Others give $2\times10^{-3}$, $2\times10^{-3}$, $10^{-3}$. Key A (the paper allowed multiple-select but only A matched).

$x(t)=A(t)\cos[2\pi f_ct+\theta(t)]$ is a real narrowband signal with the maximum frequency components of $A(t)$ and $\theta(t)$ equal to $f_M$, and $f_c=1000f_M$. Which statements are correct?
  1. $x(t)$ represents a PSK-modulated signal for suitable choices of $A(t),\theta(t)$.
  2. $x(t)$ represents an amplitude-modulated signal for suitable choices of $A(t),\theta(t)$.
  3. $x(t)$ represents a band-limited Gaussian noise process.
  4. $x(t)$ never represents a narrowband FM signal.

This is the general bandpass form of Chapter 2. PSK: $A$ const, $\theta$ switching (A ✓). AM: $\theta=0$, $A=A_c(1+\mu m)$ (B ✓). Narrowband Gaussian noise is exactly $A\cos(\cdot+\theta)$ with Rayleigh $A$ and uniform $\theta$ (C ✓). With constant $A$ and $\theta=\beta\sin$ with small $\beta$ it is narrowband FM, so (D) is false. Key A, B, C.

A frequency synthesizer has a PLL whose feedback path contains a divide-by-$N$ counter with selectable $N=2,4,8,16$. It is excited by a 5 kHz reference. The synthesized frequencies for $N=2,4,8,16$ are
  1. 10 kHz, 20 kHz, 40 kHz, 80 kHz
  2. 20 kHz, 30 kHz, 80 kHz, 160 kHz
  3. 80 kHz, 40 kHz, 20 kHz, 10 kHz
  4. 160 kHz, 80 kHz, 40 kHz, 20 kHz

$f_{out}=Nf_{ref}$: $10,20,40,80$ kHz. (A).

A message $m(t)=A_m\sin2\pi f_mt$ phase-modulates a carrier: $y(t)=A_c\cos(2\pi f_ct+m(t))$. The bandwidth of $y(t)$
  1. depends on $A_m$ but not on $f_m$
  2. depends on $f_m$ but not on $A_m$
  3. depends on both $A_m$ and $f_m$
  4. does not depend on either

Here $\beta=k_pA_m=A_m$ and Carson gives $B=2f_m(\beta+1)=2f_m(A_m+1)$: it depends on both $A_m$ and $f_m$. (C).

In the Armstrong chain of the text, the narrowband stage has $f_1=200$ kHz and $\Delta f_1=25$ Hz; it is followed by $\times64$, a mixer with a 10.9 MHz oscillator (difference frequency kept), then $\times48$. The final peak deviation in kHz is ______.

$\Delta f=25\times64\times48=76\,800$ Hz $=\mathbf{76.8}$ kHz (the mixer leaves $\Delta f$ unchanged). Final carrier: $(200\ \mathrm{kHz}\times64-10.9\ \mathrm{MHz})\times48=1.9\times48=91.2$ MHz.

Chapter 7 in one breath

  • $f_i=\frac1{2\pi}\dot\theta$. FM: $f_i=f_c+k_fm(t)$. PM: $f_i=f_c+\frac{k_p}{2\pi}\dot m$. Constant envelope ⇒ power $A_c^2/2$ regardless of $\beta$.
  • Tone: FM $\Delta f=k_fA_m$, $\beta=\Delta f/f_m$; PM $\beta=k_pA_m$, $\Delta f=\beta f_m$.
  • NBFM ($\beta\ll1$): carrier + two sidebands in quadrature, $B=2f_m$. General: $s=A_c\sum_nJ_n(\beta)\cos(\omega_c+n\omega_m)t$ (derived from the Fourier series of $e^{j\beta\sin x}$); $\sum J_n^2=1$; carrier null at $\beta=2.405,5.52,\dots$
  • Carson: $B_T=2(\Delta f+f_m)=2f_m(\beta+1)$ (>98% of the power). NBFM $\approx2f_m$; WBFM $\approx2\Delta f$. Multi-tone messages use $\Delta f=k_f\max|m|$.
  • Multipliers multiply $\Delta f$; mixers shift the carrier only. Armstrong: NBFM + multipliers + mixer. A non-linearity creates a band at $2f_c$ of doubled width.
  • Demodulation: limiter + differentiator + envelope detector, or PLL: $\dot\theta_e=\dot\theta_i-K\sin\theta_e$, lock range $\pm K$, control voltage = message. Synthesizer: $f_{out}=Nf_{ref}$.
  • Stereo: $L+R$, 19 kHz pilot, $L-R$ DSB-SC at 38 kHz. Pre/de-emphasis $RC=1/\omega_0$. Capture: interferer suppressed by $20\log(A/a)$.
