History · the man who would not stop

Static. Every AM listener in the 1920s knew it: lightning, car ignitions and electric motors all add random amplitude spikes, and an AM receiver hears them all. Edwin Howard Armstrong had already given the world the regenerative receiver (1912) and the superheterodyne (1918), but static bothered him. If the message rides on the amplitude, noise will always corrupt it. What if it rode on the frequency instead, and the receiver simply ignored amplitude?

The conventional wisdom came from John R. Carson of AT&T, who analysed FM in 1922 and concluded that it could never beat AM: its spectrum was wider, and "this type of modulation inherently distorts without any compensating advantages." Carson's reasoning was right for narrow deviations. Armstrong guessed what Carson's analysis missed: if you swing the frequency very widely, you pay in bandwidth but the noise suppression grows even faster. In 1933 he demonstrated wide-band FM, static-free; his first FM station, W2XMN at Alpine, New Jersey, went on air in 1938. Powerful interests (RCA, who owned the AM networks and television plans) fought him for years, and the patent suits drained his fortune. Armstrong died in 1954. His widow won the lawsuits.

The irony: the rule for the bandwidth of an FM signal that every engineer uses today ($B\approx2(\Delta f+f_m)$) is called Carson's rule.

Real world · where FM lives

FM broadcast (88–108 MHz; in India the AIR FM stations), the sound carrier of analog TV, two-way police and taxi radios (narrow-band FM, $\pm5$ kHz), Bluetooth and GSM (both use a smoothed frequency-shift keying, GFSK/GMSK, which is digital FM), satellite TV (the video is frequency-modulated onto the microwave carrier), and the "chirp" of radar and LiDAR.

Phase, frequency, and the spinning arrow again

Write any sinusoid as $s(t)=A_c\cos\theta(t)$. The instantaneous frequency is the speed at which the phase angle $\theta$ advances, $f_i(t)=\dfrac1{2\pi}\dfrac{d\theta}{dt}$. For an unmodulated carrier $\theta=2\pi f_ct$ and $f_i=f_c$. There are two ways to impose a message: $$\textbf{PM:}\quad\theta(t)=2\pi f_ct+k_p\,m(t)\ \Rightarrow\ f_i=f_c+\frac{k_p}{2\pi}\frac{dm}{dt},$$ $$\textbf{FM:}\quad\theta(t)=2\pi f_ct+2\pi k_f\!\int_{-\infty}^{t}\!m(\tau)d\tau\ \Rightarrow\ f_i=f_c+k_f\,m(t).$$ In FM the instantaneous frequency follows the message; in PM it follows the derivative of the message. They are therefore the same thing in disguise: FM is PM of the integral of $m$, PM is FM of the derivative of $m$. The envelope $A_c$ is constant for both, so the transmitted power is $A_c^2/2$ whatever the message does.

(1) GATE 2025 Q.14. $f(t)=A_c\cos(2\pi f_ct+3\sin2\pi f_1t+4\sin6\pi f_1t)$. The spectrum of a frequency-modulated tone

AM was easy because multiplication shifts the spectrum. FM is non-linear: $\cos(\omega_ct+\beta\sin\omega_mt)$ is the real part of $e^{j\omega_ct}\cdot e^{j\beta\sin\omega_mt}$, and the second factor is periodic in time with period $1/f_m$, so it has a Fourier series. Its coefficients are the Bessel functions of the first kind (Friedrich Bessel, 1824, who invented them for planetary perturbations, not radio): $$e^{j\beta\sin\omega_mt}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_n(\beta)\,e^{jn\omega_mt}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad s(t)=A_c\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_n(\beta)\cos\big[(\omega_c+n\omega_m)t\big].$$ So a single-tone FM wave is a carrier plus an infinite ladder of sidebands at $f_c\pm nf_m$, with amplitudes $J_n(\beta)$. Properties worth knowing:

  • $\sum_nJ_n^2(\beta)=1$ for every $\beta$: total power constant, $A_c^2/2$ ✓ (this is the algebraic version of "constant envelope").
  • $J_{-n}=(-1)^nJ_n$ so the sidebands are symmetric in magnitude.
  • For small $\beta$: $J_0\approx1$, $J_1\approx\beta/2$, $J_{n\ge2}\approx0$. Narrow-band FM: $s\approx A_c[\cos\omega_ct-\beta\sin\omega_mt\,\sin\omega_ct]$: a carrier and two sidebands like AM, but the sidebands are 90° out of phase so the amplitude doesn't vary. Bandwidth $2f_m$ (same as AM).
  • The carrier amplitude $J_0(\beta)$ goes to zero at $\beta=2.405,\ 5.520,\ 8.654,\dots$ (the zeros of $J_0$): at those values all the power is in the sidebands. This is used to calibrate deviation and is a favourite exam trick.
  • For large $\beta$ the sidebands are significant out to $|n|\approx\beta+1$: the spectrum spreads over about $2\beta f_m=2\Delta f$.

How wide is an FM signal? Carson's rule

In theory infinite, in practice about 98% of the power lies in the sidebands with $|n|\le\beta+1$. That is Carson's rule: $$\boxed{\,B_T\approx2(\Delta f+f_m)=2f_m(\beta+1)\,}\qquad\text{(general message: }B_T=2(\Delta f+W)=2W(D+1),\ D=\Delta f/W).$$ Check the extremes: NBFM ($\beta\ll1$): $B_T\approx2f_m$ ✓ same as AM. Wide-band FM ($\beta\gg1$): $B_T\approx2\Delta f$: the bandwidth is just the swing of the frequency, as common sense says.

Worked example · FM broadcast

Peak deviation $\Delta f=75$ kHz, audio up to $W=15$ kHz: $D=5$, $B_T=2(75+15)=180$ kHz, fitted into a 200 kHz channel. Compare AM broadcast with the same audio: 30 kHz. FM pays 6× the bandwidth, and (Chapter 6) buys a large SNR improvement for it. This is the bandwidth-for-noise trade that Armstrong understood and Carson did not.

Traps
  • Carson's rule uses peak deviation $\Delta f$, not $\beta$ alone; for PM with a tone, $\Delta f=\beta f_m$, so $B_T=2f_m(\beta+1)$ grows with $f_m$.
  • If the message amplitude and frequency both change, recompute $\Delta f$ for FM (depends on amplitude only) and $\beta$ (amplitude/frequency).
  • FM power does not depend on $\beta$. A common mistake is to apply $P=P_c(1+\mu^2/2)$.

Making FM, and what non-linearities do to it

Direct FM: make an oscillator whose frequency depends on a voltage (a varicap diode in the tank circuit): a VCO. Simple, wide deviation, but the centre frequency drifts. Indirect (Armstrong) FM: integrate the message, use it for narrow-band phase modulation of a crystal oscillator (stable but only $\beta\approx0.2$), then use frequency multipliers: a non-linear device that multiplies the instantaneous phase by $n$ also multiplies $f_c$ and $\Delta f$ (so $\beta$) by $n$. Mixers then move the carrier down. Armstrong's 1938 transmitter did exactly this.

Worked example · GATE 2023 Q.45: a non-linearity applied to FM

$x(t)=A\cos(\omega_ct+k_f\!\int m)$ occupies a band of width $B_T$ around $f_c$. It passes through $y=2x+5x^2$. Since $x^2=\frac{A^2}2[1+\cos(2\omega_ct+2k_f\!\int m)]$, the output contains: (i) a DC term; (ii) the original FM band at $f_c\pm B_T/2$; (iii) a new FM signal at $2f_c$ with doubled phase deviation, so width $\approx2B_T$, spanning $2f_c\pm B_T$. To recover $x$ with a band-pass filter, bands (ii) and (iii) must not overlap: $f_c+\frac{B_T}2\le2f_c-B_T$, i.e. $f_c\ge\frac32B_T$. Answer (B) $\frac32B_T$ (the official key).

Getting the message back

Slope detector / differentiator. Differentiate $s(t)=A_c\cos\theta(t)$: $\dot s=-A_c\dot\theta\sin\theta(t)=-2\pi A_c\,f_i(t)\,\sin\theta(t)$. The result is an AM wave whose envelope is proportional to the instantaneous frequency, i.e. to $m(t)$. An envelope detector finishes the job. (In practice a tuned circuit's skirt acts as the differentiator; a balanced pair of such circuits cancels the DC and doubles the linear range: the balanced discriminator.) A limiter before the discriminator clips off any amplitude noise: this is the entire secret of FM's static immunity.

FM demodulator: band-pass filter → limiter (kills amplitude noise) → differentiator (turns frequency into amplitude) → envelope detector → DC block → message.

Phase-locked loop (PLL). The workhorse of modern FM receivers. A voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) is compared with the input by a phase detector; the error voltage, filtered, steers the VCO until it tracks the input phase. If the input frequency wanders, the control voltage must wander the same way to keep the VCO in step, so the control voltage is the demodulated message. A PLL locks as long as the input phase does not change faster than the loop can follow (loop gain $K>\max|\dot\varphi_{\rm in}|$).

Phase-locked loop used as an FM demodulator.
First-order PLL simulated in baseband. The blue curve is the input phase (an FM tone with swing $\beta=3$ rad at 1 Hz); the loop tries to make the VCO phase (yellow) follow it. The bottom plot is the phase error. With a high loop gain $K$ the error stays small and the control signal reproduces the message; with $K$ below the signal's fastest phase slope ($2\pi\beta f_m\approx19$ rad/s) the loop cannot keep up and loses lock ("cycle slipping").

PLL as a frequency synthesizer

The same PLL, used backwards, makes the clocks and carriers of every digital device. Put a divide-by-$N$ counter in the feedback path from the VCO to the phase detector. The loop locks when the divided VCO frequency equals the reference: $f_{VCO}/N=f_{ref}$, so $$f_{out}=N\,f_{ref}.$$ A single crystal reference (very stable, but fixed) thereby yields a whole comb of frequencies, selected by changing $N$. This is how your radio tunes to 94.3 MHz and how a Wi-Fi chip makes 2.4 GHz from a 40 MHz crystal. (GATE 2016: a 5 kHz reference with ÷2, ÷4, ÷8, ÷16 taps in the feedback path gives outputs of 10, 20, 40, 80 kHz.)

Frequency synthesizer: the loop forces $f_{out}/N=f_{ref}$.

Stereo and pre-emphasis: two engineering touches

FM stereo. A mono receiver must still work, so the baseband sent to the FM modulator is $L+R$ (0–15 kHz) plus a 19 kHz pilot tone plus $L-R$ as a DSB-SC signal on a 38 kHz subcarrier (the second harmonic of the pilot, so the receiver regenerates the subcarrier coherently). Chapter 4 in action:

Baseband spectrum of an FM stereo multiplex signal.

Pre-emphasis / de-emphasis. After the discriminator, FM noise has a PSD that grows as $f^2$ (derived in Chapter 6), but speech and music energy falls with frequency. The fix: boost the highs before transmission, $H_{pe}(f)=1+j\frac f{f_0}$ ($\tau=\frac1{2\pi f_0}=75\,\mu$s in the USA, $50\,\mu$s in India/Europe), and apply the exact inverse $H_{de}=\dfrac1{1+jf/f_0}$ (an RC low-pass with $RC=\tau$) after detection. The message sees $H_{pe}H_{de}=1$ but the noise is shaped by $H_{de}$ alone: it is attenuated at high frequencies, where it was strongest.

  2. $x(t)$ represents an amplitude-modulated signal for suitable choices of $A(t),\theta(t)$.

This is the general bandpass form of Chapter 2. PSK: $A$ const, $\theta$ switching (A ✓). AM: $\theta=0$, $A=A_c(1+\mu m)$ (B ✓). Chapter 5 in one breath

  • $f_i=\frac1{2\pi}\dot\theta$. FM: $f_i=f_c+k_fm(t)$. PM: $f_i=f_c+\frac{k_p}{2\pi}\dot m$. Constant envelope ⇒ power $A_c^2/2$ regardless of $\beta$.
  • Tone: FM $\Delta f=k_fA_m$, $\beta=\Delta f/f_m$; PM $\beta=k_pA_m$, $\Delta f=\beta f_m$.
  • Spectrum: $\sum_nJ_n(\beta)\cos(\omega_c+n\omega_m)t$; $\sum J_n^2=1$; carrier null at $\beta=2.405,5.52,\dots$
  • Carson: $B_T=2(\Delta f+f_m)=2f_m(\beta+1)$. NBFM $\approx2f_m$; WBFM $\approx2\Delta f$.
  • Demodulation: limiter + differentiator + envelope detector, or PLL (VCO control voltage = message).
  • Non-linearities multiply phase: $x^2$ gives an FM band at $2f_c$ with doubled deviation. Pre/de-emphasis: $RC=1/\omega_0$.
