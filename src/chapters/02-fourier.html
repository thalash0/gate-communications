What you will be able to do after this chapter
  • Derive the Fourier series coefficients from orthogonality, compute them for square, triangle and pulse-train waves, and explain Gibbs’ overshoot.
  • Obtain the Fourier transform as the limit of the series when the period becomes infinite, and prove the key properties (shift, scaling, duality, convolution, Parseval) rather than memorise them.
  • Derive the standard transform pairs (rect, exponential, Gaussian, impulse train) and use them to find spectra by inspection.
  • Define and compute energy spectral density, power spectral density and autocorrelation of deterministic signals.
  • State and prove the time–bandwidth trade-off ($\Delta t\,\Delta f\ge\frac1{4\pi}$); relate it to why data rate needs bandwidth.
  • Use the DFT/FFT correctly: frequency resolution, zero-padding, windowing, spectral leakage.

History · a scandal about heat

In December 1807 Joseph Fourier read a paper to the Paris Academy claiming that any temperature profile along a metal rod, however jagged, could be written as a sum of sines. Lagrange, one of the greatest mathematicians alive, thought this was nonsense. The paper was not published for fifteen years. Fourier, who had earlier gone to Egypt with Napoleon’s army and come home convinced that desert heat was good for the health, turned out to be right (with a few technical conditions that Dirichlet cleaned up in 1829).

Seventy years later, Alexander Graham Bell was trying to send several telegraph messages down one wire by giving each a different pitch, which is “frequency-division multiplexing” long before anyone had the words. Bell’s “harmonic telegraph” failed as a telegraph, but while working on it he and Watson accidentally built the telephone. Every radio, every Wi-Fi router and every mobile tower is a descendant of the idea that a wire (or the sky) can carry many conversations at once, because different conversations occupy different frequencies. That idea needs Fourier.

Why sinusoids? The idea behind the whole chapter

In Chapter 1 we proved that every LTI system treats the complex exponential $e^{j2\pi ft}$ in the simplest imaginable way: it multiplies it by a number $H(f)$. If we could write every signal as a sum of such exponentials, the response to any signal would follow from this one fact, with no convolution integral in sight. The Fourier series does it for periodic signals and the Fourier transform for everything else. The payoff is not only mathematical: the sinusoid decomposition is exactly the picture that a radio engineer, a spectrum analyser and your ear all share.

A pure musical note is a sine wave, and a sine wave is the shadow of an arrow spinning in a circle: an arrow of length $A$ rotating $f$ times per second has a tip at height $A\sin(2\pi ft)$. Euler’s formula packages this: $$e^{j\theta}=\cos\theta+j\sin\theta,\qquad\cos\theta=\frac{e^{j\theta}+e^{-j\theta}}2,\qquad\sin\theta=\frac{e^{j\theta}-e^{-j\theta}}{2j}.$$ A real cosine is therefore two arrows spinning in opposite directions, at $+f$ and $-f$, whose imaginary parts cancel. That is the entire reason spectra of real signals are symmetric and contain “negative frequencies”: they are bookkeeping for the second arrow.

Fourier series: periodic signals as a sum of harmonics

Orthogonality: the one fact you need

Let $x(t)$ have period $T$ and $f_0=1/T$. Consider the harmonics $e^{j2\pi nf_0t}$, $n=0,\pm1,\pm2,\dots$. Integrate a product of two of them over one period: $$\int_0^{T}e^{j2\pi mf_0t}\,e^{-j2\pi nf_0t}\,dt=\int_0^Te^{j2\pi(m-n)t/T}dt=\begin{cases}T,&m=n\\[2pt]\dfrac{e^{j2\pi(m-n)}-1}{j2\pi(m-n)/T}=0,&m\ne n.\end{cases}$$ The harmonics are orthogonal, exactly as the unit vectors $\hat x,\hat y,\hat z$ are, and $\int_T|e^{j2\pi nf_0t}|^2dt=T$ plays the role of $|\hat x|^2$. Whenever you have an orthogonal set, the coefficients of an expansion are found by projecting.

Derivation · Fourier series coefficients

Assume $x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}c_ke^{j2\pi kf_0t}$. Multiply both sides by $e^{-j2\pi nf_0t}$ and integrate over a period: $$\int_0^Tx(t)e^{-j2\pi nf_0t}dt=\sum_kc_k\underbrace{\int_0^Te^{j2\pi(k-n)f_0t}dt}_{T\,\delta_{kn}}=T\,c_n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{\,c_n=\frac1T\int_{T}x(t)\,e^{-j2\pi nf_0t}\,dt\,}$$ $c_0$ is the average (DC value). For a real signal $c_{-n}=c_n^*$. The equivalent real (trigonometric) form follows by grouping $\pm n$: with $c_n=\tfrac12(a_n-jb_n)$, $$x(t)=a_0+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big[a_n\cos(2\pi nf_0t)+b_n\sin(2\pi nf_0t)\big],\quad a_n=\frac2T\!\int_Tx\cos,\ \ b_n=\frac2T\!\int_Tx\sin,$$ and the amplitude of the $n$th harmonic is $2|c_n|=\sqrt{a_n^2+b_n^2}$ with phase $\angle c_n$. Parseval for periodic signals: multiply the series by its conjugate and average over a period; by orthogonality the cross terms vanish, leaving $$P=\frac1T\int_T|x|^2dt=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}|c_n|^2=a_0^2+\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{a_n^2+b_n^2}2.$$ Power in each harmonic adds, just as for the sinusoids of Chapter 1.

Worked example · the square wave (derive its coefficients)

$x(t)=+1$ for $0Power check (Parseval). $P_x=1$. The harmonics carry $\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac{(4/\pi n)^2}2=\frac8{\pi^2}\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac1{n^2}=\frac8{\pi^2}\cdot\frac{\pi^2}8=1$ ✓ (using $\sum_{\rm odd}n^{-2}=\pi^2/8$). The fundamental alone carries $8/\pi^2=81.1\%$ of the power, the third harmonic $9.0\%$, the fifth $3.2\%$. Total harmonic distortion (power in all harmonics except the fundamental, relative to the fundamental) $=\sqrt{(1-0.811)/0.811}=48\%$.

Build a periodic wave from its harmonics. Increase the number of harmonics $N$: the partial sum converges to the target everywhere except right at a jump, where it always overshoots by about 9% of the jump however large $N$ is (Gibbs). The overshoot gets narrower, not smaller. The bar chart shows the line spectrum: how the harmonic amplitudes fall off ($1/n$ for jumps, $1/n^2$ for a triangle, whose slope jumps instead).
Smoothness decides the spectrum

A signal with a jump (discontinuity) has harmonics falling like $1/n$; one that is continuous but with a corner (triangle) like $1/n^2$; one with continuous first derivative like $1/n^3$; infinitely smooth signals (a Gaussian pulse train) fall faster than any power. This is not just maths: sharp edges cost bandwidth. A 3.3 V digital clock edge with a 1 ns rise time has significant spectral content to $\approx0.35/t_r=350$ MHz, and its harmonics are what radiate and fail EMC tests; designers deliberately slow the edges down (“slew-rate control”).

Rectangular pulse train: the spectrum with a sinc envelope

Pulses of height $A$ and width $\tau$, repeating every $T$ (duty cycle $d=\tau/T$). Using the definition, $$c_n=\frac1T\int_{-\tau/2}^{\tau/2}Ae^{-j2\pi nt/T}dt=\frac AT\cdot\frac{\sin(\pi n\tau/T)}{\pi n/T}=\frac{A\tau}{T}\,\sinc\!\Big(\frac{n\tau}{T}\Big)=Ad\,\sinc(nd).$$ The lines sit at multiples of $1/T$; their heights follow a sinc envelope that first crosses zero at $f=1/\tau$. So: narrow pulses (small $\tau$) ⇒ the envelope is wide; long period ⇒ the lines crowd closer together. Take the limit $T\to\infty$ and the lines merge into a continuous curve. That limit is the Fourier transform.

From series to transform

Derivation · the Fourier transform as the limit of the series

Take an aperiodic signal $x(t)$ (say a single pulse) and build a periodic one by repeating it every $T$. Its series is $x_T(t)=\sum_nc_ne^{j2\pi nt/T}$ with $c_n=\frac1T\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}x(t)e^{-j2\pi nt/T}dt$. Define $X(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}dt$. For large $T$ the integral over a period is the whole integral, so $c_n\approx\frac1TX(n/T)$. Substitute in the series, with $\Delta f=1/T$: $$x_T(t)=\sum_{n}X(n\Delta f)\,e^{j2\pi(n\Delta f)t}\,\Delta f\ \xrightarrow{\ T\to\infty\ }\ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X(f)e^{j2\pi ft}\,df,$$ a Riemann sum turning into an integral. That gives the pair $$\boxed{X(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\,e^{-j2\pi ft}dt,\qquad x(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X(f)\,e^{+j2\pi ft}df.}$$ Interpretation: $X(f)\,df$ is the (complex) amplitude of the arrow spinning at frequency $f$ within the band $df$; $|X(f)|$ is a spectral density (volts per hertz), not an amplitude. We use $f$ in hertz rather than $\omega$ so that no stray $2\pi$’s appear in the inverse. Sufficient condition for existence: $\int|x|<\infty$ (plus mild regularity); signals like constants and sinusoids are handled by allowing impulses in $X(f)$.

The Fourier transform has a one-sentence meaning: “How much of the arrow spinning at frequency $f$ is hiding in my signal?” To find out, take $x(t)$ and wind it around the origin at $f$ turns per second: at time $t$ plot the point $x(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}$. If $x$ contains a component that spins at exactly $f$, the winding cancels its spin: that component now sits still, always pulling the same way, and the whole curve gets lopsided. The centre of mass of the wound-up curve is the Fourier transform at $f$. For every other $f$ the curve is balanced and the centre of mass stays near zero.

The signal is $g(t)=\cos(2\pi\cdot3t)+0.6\cos(2\pi\cdot5t)$ for $0\le t\le4\,\mathrm{s}$. Slide the winding frequency. When it passes 3 or 5, the wound curve suddenly swings off-centre and the yellow centre-of-mass dot jumps out. The bottom-left plot is the length of that dot versus $f$: the spectrum. (There is no peak at $-3,-5$ in the plot only because we show positive $f$.)

$|X(f)|$ is the amplitude spectrum and $\angle X(f)$ the phase spectrum. For real $x(t)$: $X(-f)=X^*(f)$, so $|X|$ is even and the phase is odd. If additionally $x$ is even then $X$ is real and even; if $x$ is odd, $X$ is purely imaginary and odd (the even/odd split of Chapter 1 maps to the real/imaginary split).

Transform pairs, derived

Four derivations that give you almost every pair

1. The rectangle

$$\mathcal F\{\rect(t/T)\}=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}e^{-j2\pi ft}dt=\frac{e^{-j\pi fT}-e^{j\pi fT}}{-j2\pi f}=\frac{\sin\pi fT}{\pi f}=T\,\sinc(fT).$$

2. The one-sided exponential

$$\mathcal F\{e^{-at}u(t)\}=\int_0^\infty e^{-(a+j2\pi f)t}dt=\frac1{a+j2\pi f},\qquad a>0.$$ By even/odd symmetry, $e^{-a|t|}=e^{-at}u(t)+e^{at}u(-t)$ gives $\dfrac1{a+j2\pi f}+\dfrac1{a-j2\pi f}=\dfrac{2a}{a^2+4\pi^2f^2}$.

3. The Gaussian is its own transform

Let $X(f)=\int e^{-\pi t^2}e^{-j2\pi ft}dt$. Differentiate under the integral: $X'(f)=\int(-j2\pi t)e^{-\pi t^2}e^{-j2\pi ft}dt$. Since $\frac d{dt}e^{-\pi t^2}=-2\pi te^{-\pi t^2}$, this is $j\int\big[\frac{d}{dt}e^{-\pi t^2}\big]e^{-j2\pi ft}dt$; integrate by parts (boundary terms vanish): $=j\cdot(j2\pi f)\int e^{-\pi t^2}e^{-j2\pi ft}dt=-2\pi fX(f)$. The solution of $X'=-2\pi fX$ is $X=X(0)e^{-\pi f^2}$, and $X(0)=\int e^{-\pi t^2}dt=1$. Hence $e^{-\pi t^2}\leftrightarrow e^{-\pi f^2}$, and by scaling $e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}\leftrightarrow\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-2\pi^2\sigma^2f^2}$.

4. The impulse and the constant

Sifting gives $\mathcal F\{\delta(t)\}=\int\delta(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}dt=1$. By duality (proved below) $\mathcal F\{1\}=\delta(f)$, and by the frequency-shift property $\mathcal F\{e^{j2\pi f_0t}\}=\delta(f-f_0)$ and $\cos(2\pi f_0t)\leftrightarrow\tfrac12[\delta(f-f_0)+\delta(f+f_0)]$: the “two counter-rotating arrows” of the start of the chapter, each now a spike. The step: $u(t)=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\sgn t$ and $\mathcal F\{\sgn t\}=\frac1{j\pi f}$ (the limit $a\to0$ of the odd signal $\sgn(t)e^{-a|t|}$, whose transform $\frac1{a+j2\pi f}-\frac1{a-j2\pi f}=\frac{-j4\pi f}{a^2+4\pi^2f^2}\to\frac1{j\pi f}$).

Proofs of the properties that matter most

Time shift

$\int x(t-t_0)e^{-j2\pi ft}dt\overset{u=t-t_0}{=}e^{-j2\pi ft_0}\int x(u)e^{-j2\pi fu}du=X(f)e^{-j2\pi ft_0}$. A delay is a linear phase: this is the distortionless channel of Chapter 1.

Frequency shift and modulation

$\int x(t)e^{j2\pi f_0t}e^{-j2\pi ft}dt=\int x(t)e^{-j2\pi(f-f_0)t}dt=X(f-f_0)$. Writing $\cos2\pi f_ct=\tfrac12(e^{j2\pi f_ct}+e^{-j2\pi f_ct})$ gives the cosine rule. Multiplying by a carrier copies the spectrum to $\pm f_c$ at half height. This single line is amplitude modulation.

Scaling

For $a>0$: $\int x(at)e^{-j2\pi ft}dt\overset{u=at}{=}\frac1a\int x(u)e^{-j2\pi(f/a)u}du=\frac1aX(f/a)$ (a similar substitution gives $1/|a|$ for $a<0$). Compress in time by $a$, stretch in frequency by $a$, the root of “fast data needs wide bandwidth”.

Differentiation

Differentiate the inverse transform under the integral: $\dot x(t)=\int X(f)\,(j2\pi f)\,e^{j2\pi ft}df$. A differentiator multiplies by $j2\pi f$: it boosts high frequencies and underlies FM demodulation (Chapter 7).

Duality

The forward and inverse transforms differ only by the sign in the exponent. So if $x(t)\leftrightarrow X(f)$, then $X(t)\leftrightarrow x(-f)$. Examples: rect↔sinc becomes sinc↔rect; $\delta(t)\leftrightarrow1$ becomes $1\leftrightarrow\delta(f)$.

Convolution theorem

$$\mathcal F\{x*h\}=\int\!\!\Big[\int x(\tau)h(t-\tau)d\tau\Big]e^{-j2\pi ft}dt=\int x(\tau)e^{-j2\pi f\tau}\!\!\int h(t-\tau)e^{-j2\pi f(t-\tau)}dt\,d\tau=X(f)H(f).$$ Its dual: multiplication in time is convolution in frequency, $\mathcal F\{xy\}=X*Y$: this is why a product widens the spectrum (bandwidths add) and why mixing and modulation create sum and difference frequencies.

Parseval’s theorem

$\int|x|^2dt=\int x(t)x^*(t)dt=\int x(t)\Big[\int X^*(f)e^{-j2\pi ft}df\Big]dt=\int X^*(f)\Big[\int x(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}dt\Big]df=\int|X|^2df$. Energy can be counted in either domain; $|X(f)|^2$ is the energy spectral density (J/Hz).

Multiplying by $\cos 2\pi f_ct$ copies the spectrum up to $\pm f_c$, at half height. That is amplitude modulation. Everything in Chapter 6 is a variation on this one line.

Squeeze in time, spread in frequency

Look at the rect pair. A narrow rectangle in time gives a wide, low sinc in frequency; a wide rectangle gives a tall, narrow sinc. You cannot be short in time and narrow in frequency. This is not a technology limit, it is mathematics (the same mathematics as Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle). It is why fast data needs wide bandwidth, the single most important sentence in digital communication.

Pick a pulse and drag its width $T$. Watch the spectrum do the opposite. The area of the time pulse equals the height of the spectrum at $f=0$, and the height of the pulse at $t=0$ equals the area of the spectrum.
Derivation · the time–bandwidth (uncertainty) inequality

For a real, energy-$E$ signal define the RMS time width $\Delta t^2=\frac1E\int t^2x^2dt$ and RMS bandwidth $\Delta f^2=\frac1E\int f^2|X|^2df$ (signal centred at the origin in both domains). By the differentiation property and Parseval, $\int\dot x^2dt=4\pi^2\int f^2|X|^2df=4\pi^2E\,\Delta f^2$. Apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $\big(\int ab\big)^2\le\int a^2\int b^2$ to $a=tx$, $b=\dot x$: $$\Big(\int t\,x\dot x\,dt\Big)^2\le\Delta t^2E\cdot4\pi^2E\Delta f^2.$$ Integrate by parts: $\int tx\dot x\,dt=\tfrac12\int t\,d(x^2)=-\tfrac12\int x^2dt=-\tfrac E2$ (the boundary term vanishes for finite-energy signals). Hence $\frac{E^2}4\le4\pi^2E^2\Delta t^2\Delta f^2$: $$\boxed{\Delta t\;\Delta f\ \ge\ \frac1{4\pi}}$$ with equality only for the Gaussian. A pulse of duration $\tau$ needs bandwidth of order $1/\tau$; a system of bandwidth $B$ can resolve events no closer than about $1/B$. Radar range resolution $\Delta R=c/2B$, a sampling scope’s bandwidth, the symbol rate $R_s\le2B$ of Chapter 13, and the pulse-width limits of every digital link all come from this one inequality.

Convolution: flip, slide, multiply, add up

A linear time-invariant system (a filter, a cable, a radio channel) responds to every instant of the input with a scaled, delayed copy of its impulse response $h(t)$. We derived in Chapter 1 that the output is $$y(t)=(x*h)(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)\,h(t-\tau)\,d\tau .$$ Visually: flip $h$ left-right, slide it so that its origin sits at $t$, multiply with $x$ point by point, and take the area. Slide a different distance, get a different number: that is $y(t)$. In the frequency domain this laborious operation becomes plain multiplication, $Y(f)=X(f)H(f)$, by the convolution theorem just proved. That is why we love Fourier.

The shaded area under the product $x(\tau)h(t-\tau)$ is the output value at the current $t$ (yellow dot, lower plot). Drag $t$ or press play. Two rectangles give a triangle; a rectangle through an RC filter gives a charging-then-discharging curve.
Worked example · rect * rect = triangle, three ways

Time domain: the overlap of two unit rectangles slid by $t$ is $1-|t|$ for $|t|\le1$, i.e. $\tri(t)$. Frequency domain: $\sinc f\cdot\sinc f=\sinc^2f$, and indeed $\tri(t)\leftrightarrow\sinc^2f$ (the pair in the table). Check with the area rule: $\int\tri=1=\sinc^2(0)$ ✓. This is the reason the spectrum of a triangular or “half-sine” pulse falls as $1/f^2$ instead of $1/f$: each extra convolution with a rectangle smooths the pulse and multiplies the spectrum by another sinc.

Energy, power and correlation

A signal with finite $E=\int|x|^2dt<\infty$ is an energy signal (a single pulse); one that never dies but has finite average power is a power signal (sine, random bit stream). For energy signals, define the autocorrelation $R_x(\tau)=\int x(t)x^*(t-\tau)dt$: “how much does the signal resemble a copy of itself delayed by $\tau$?”. Its maximum is at $\tau=0$ (value $E$), and by the convolution theorem (correlation is convolution with the flipped conjugate) $$R_x(\tau)\ \longleftrightarrow\ |X(f)|^2=\mathcal E_x(f)\quad(\text{energy spectral density}).$$ For periodic power signals the same logic gives a power spectral density $S_x(f)=\sum_n|c_n|^2\delta(f-nf_0)$ with $R_x(\tau)=\sum|c_n|^2e^{j2\pi nf_0\tau}$ (a periodic function). The identical relation, $R\leftrightarrow S$, holds for random signals; it is the Wiener–Khinchin theorem of Chapter 5. When a signal passes through an LTI filter, $\mathcal E_y=|H|^2\mathcal E_x$: the filter acts on power through $|H|^2$ and ignores phase.

Bandwidth has no unique meaning: null-to-null (main lobe), 3-dB (half power), RMS, or “contains 99% of the energy”. Always read which one the question wants. For a rectangular pulse of width $\tau$: null-to-null $2/\tau$, first-null (one-sided) $1/\tau$, 3-dB about $0.44/\tau$; about 90% of the energy lies below $1/\tau$.

Computing spectra: the DFT and FFT

A computer or a modem works with $N$ samples $x[n]$ taken at rate $f_s$. The discrete Fourier transform is $$X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]\,e^{-j2\pi kn/N},\qquad x[n]=\frac1N\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}X[k]e^{+j2\pi kn/N},\qquad k=0,\dots,N-1,$$ a projection of the data onto $N$ orthogonal complex exponentials (same orthogonality argument as for the series: the sum of $e^{j2\pi(k-m)n/N}$ over $n$ is $N\delta_{km}$). Bin $k$ corresponds to frequency $kf_s/N$ (bins above $N/2$ are the negative frequencies). Four facts determine whether you read a spectrum correctly:

  • Resolution: adjacent bins are $\Delta f=f_s/N=1/(NT_s)=1/T_{\rm obs}$ apart. To separate two tones $5$ Hz apart you must observe for at least $0.2$ s, however fast you sample.
  • Zero-padding (appending zeros before the FFT) interpolates the spectrum smoothly but adds no new resolution.
  • Leakage: observing $N$ samples is multiplying by a rectangular window, which convolves the spectrum with a sinc. A tone that does not fit an integer number of cycles in the window smears across bins with $-13$ dB sidelobes decaying slowly. A smooth window (Hann, Hamming, Blackman) lowers the sidelobes (to $-31$ dB, $-43$ dB, $-58$ dB) at the price of a wider main lobe. Exactly the smoothness-versus-bandwidth rule of the pulse train.
  • Cost: evaluating the sums directly takes $N^2$ complex multiplications; the fast Fourier transform (Cooley–Tukey 1965, but known to Gauss in 1805) splits an $N$-point DFT into two $N/2$-point DFTs plus $N/2$ “butterflies”, giving $\tfrac N2\log_2N$ multiplications: for $N=4096$ that is $24\,576$ instead of $16.8$ million.
Engineering practice · the FFT is a bank of matched filters

Each FFT bin is the output of a narrow filter matched to a sinusoid at that bin’s frequency. A tone of amplitude $A$ adds coherently over $N$ samples, giving $|X|=AN/2$, while white noise of variance $\sigma^2$ adds in power, giving $\sqrt N\sigma$. The tone therefore stands out of the noise floor by an extra $10\log_{10}(N/2)\,$dB (“processing gain”) compared with looking at the raw samples: a 4096-point FFT lifts a tone 33 dB. That is how a spectrum analyser finds a signal below the noise, and it will reappear as the matched filter (Chapter 14), the CDMA correlator (Chapter 19) and OFDM (Chapter 20), where the IFFT/FFT pair is the modulator and demodulator of Wi-Fi, LTE and 5G.

  3. $|X(f)|$ is even in $f$.
  4. $\angle X(f)$ is even in $f$.

Real $x$: $X(-f)=X^*(f)$, so the magnitude is even and the phase odd (C true, D false). Even $x$: only the cosine part survives, $X(f)=\int x\cos(2\pi ft)dt$ is real (A true); odd $x$ gives purely imaginary $X$ (B false).

A pulse $x(t)$ is replaced by $x(2t)$ (played twice as fast). Its spectrum $X(f)$ becomes
  1. $2X(2f)$
  2. $X(f/2)$
  3. $\tfrac12X(f/2)$
  4. $\tfrac12X(2f)$

Scaling: $x(at)\leftrightarrow\frac1{|a|}X(f/a)$ with $a=2$: twice as wide, half as high. (C). Area of the pulse halves, and $X(0)$ equals the area.

Set B · Numerical practice

A periodic train of rectangular pulses of height 5 V, width 1 ms and period 10 ms is applied to a narrow band-pass filter tuned to the fundamental (100 Hz). The amplitude of the sinusoid at the output (volts, three decimals; unity filter gain) is ______.

$c_1=Ad\,\sinc(d)=5(0.1)\sinc(0.1)=0.5\times0.9836=0.4918$. The real sinusoid has amplitude $2|c_1|=\mathbf{0.984}$ V. (DC term: $0.5$ V; first spectral null at $1/\tau=1$ kHz, i.e. at the 10th harmonic.)

The percentage of the power of a symmetric square wave contained in its fundamental frequency is ______.

$\dfrac{(4/\pi)^2/2}{1}=\dfrac8{\pi^2}=0.811$, i.e. 81.1%.

The energy of $x(t)=2\,\sinc(4t)$ is ______. (Here $\sinc(x)=\sin\pi x/\pi x$.)

$\sinc(4t)\leftrightarrow\frac14\rect(f/4)$, so $X(f)=\tfrac12\rect(f/4)$: height $0.5$ over $|f|\le2$. Energy $=\int|X|^2df=0.25\times4=\mathbf1$. (Time-domain check: $\int4\sinc^2(4t)dt=4\cdot\frac14=1$ ✓.)

$x(t)=\delta(t-2)+\delta(t+2)$. The value of its Fourier transform at $f=0.25$ Hz is ______.

$X(f)=e^{-j4\pi f}+e^{j4\pi f}=2\cos(4\pi f)$. At $f=0.25$: $2\cos\pi=\mathbf{-2}$.

A signal sampled at 8 kHz is analysed with a 1024-point FFT. The spacing between FFT bins in Hz is ______.

$\Delta f=f_s/N=8000/1024=\mathbf{7.81}$ Hz.

A rectangular pulse of duration 10 ns is transmitted. The width, in MHz, of the main lobe of its spectrum on the positive-frequency side (from 0 to the first null) is ______.

First null at $f=1/\tau=1/(10\,\mathrm{ns})=\mathbf{100}$ MHz.

Set C · Exam-style problems

Consider a real baseband signal $x(t)=e^{-2t}$ for $t\ge0$ (and 0 otherwise), $t$ in seconds. If 99% of the energy of $x(t)$ lies within $B$ Hz, then which is TRUE?
  1. $B>1\ \mathrm{kHz}$
  2. $63/\pi\ \mathrm{Hz}
  3. $126/\pi\ \mathrm{Hz}
  4. $B<1\ \mathrm{Hz}$

Derived above: $B=\tan(0.99\pi/2)/\pi=63.66/\pi$ Hz. Official key: (B).

A signal has spectrum $X(f)=\tri(f/3)$ (a triangle of height 1 and half-width 3 Hz). Its energy in joules is ______.

$E=\int\tri^2(f/3)df=3\int_{-1}^{1}(1-|u|)^2du=3\cdot2\cdot\frac13=\mathbf2$. (Time-domain: $x(t)=3\sinc^2(3t)$, $x(0)=3=\int X\,df$ ✓ area of the triangle $=3$.)

For $x(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$, the value of $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{df}{2+j2\pi f}$ (interpreted as a principal value) is
  1. $0.5$
  2. $1$
  3. $0$
  4. $\infty$

The integral is $x(0)$ by the area rule. But $x$ jumps from 0 to 1 at $t=0$, and the inverse transform converges to the midpoint, $\frac{0+1}2=0.5$. Check directly: the real part is $\int\frac{2\,df}{4+4\pi^2f^2}=\frac12$; the imaginary part is odd and integrates to zero. (A).

The Fourier transform of $\dfrac{d}{dt}\rect(t)$ is
  1. $\sinc f$
  2. $j\sinc f$
  3. $2\pi f\,\sinc f$
  4. $j2\pi f\,\sinc f$

Differentiation multiplies by $j2\pi f$, and $\rect\leftrightarrow\sinc f$. Direct check: $\frac d{dt}\rect(t)=\delta(t+\tfrac12)-\delta(t-\tfrac12)$, with transform $e^{j\pi f}-e^{-j\pi f}=2j\sin\pi f=j2\pi f\,\frac{\sin\pi f}{\pi f}$ ✓. (D).

Chapter 2 in one breath

  • Harmonics are orthogonal, so $c_n=\frac1T\int_Tx\,e^{-j2\pi nf_0t}dt$; power $=\sum|c_n|^2$ (Parseval). Jumps ⇒ $1/n$ harmonics and a 9% Gibbs overshoot; smoother signals fall faster. Pulse train: $c_n=Ad\,\sinc(nd)$.
  • Fourier transform = series with $T\to\infty$: $X(f)=\int x\,e^{-j2\pi ft}dt$. Real $x$: $X(-f)=X^*(f)$.
  • Pairs (derived): rect↔sinc, one-sided exp↔$\frac1{a+j2\pi f}$, Gaussian↔Gaussian, $\delta\leftrightarrow1$, comb↔comb. Periodic signals have impulse spectra weighted by $c_n$.
  • Shift ⇒ phase ramp; multiply by $\cos2\pi f_ct$ ⇒ copies at $\pm f_c$, half height; scale $a$ ⇒ stretch $1/a$; convolution ⇔ product; Parseval; area rules $X(0)=\int x$, $x(0)=\int X$.
  • $\Delta t\,\Delta f\ge1/4\pi$: short pulses need wide bandwidth (first null $\approx1/\tau$).
  • $R_x\leftrightarrow|X|^2$ (ESD); filters scale spectral density by $|H|^2$.
  • DFT: resolution $f_s/N$; zero-padding interpolates; windows trade leakage for resolution; FFT costs $\frac N2\log_2N$ and gives $\approx10\log N$ dB processing gain.
